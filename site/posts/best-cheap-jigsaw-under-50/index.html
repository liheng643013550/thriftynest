If you're equipping a first apartment, the question that decides everything about a cheap jigsaw isn't blade speed or amp rating — it's where the thing lives when you're not using it. A jigsaw is the most apartment-friendly power saw there is, but only if it fits in the closet, under the sink, or on a shelf without becoming a permanent resident on your countertop.

That's the real filter here. You want a saw that stores small, cuts a shelf or a cabinet door when you need it, and doesn't eat a chunk of your security deposit or your square footage. Below are picks that make sense for a couple in a small place, plus the honest trade-offs at this price.

What actually matters

  • Footprint and storage. A corded jigsaw is usually a compact tool — roughly 8 to 11 inches long and 4 to 6 inches tall depending on the model, which is small enough for a plastic storage bin or a drawer. Cordless versions need a charger and spare batteries, which adds a second footprint. In a small apartment, the corded tool plus a 6-foot extension cord often takes up less total space than a charger station.
  • Corded vs. cordless. Corded saws typically deliver more consistent power for the money and never run out mid-cut, but you're tied to an outlet. Cordless is more convenient on a balcony or in a hallway, but at under $50 you're usually buying into a battery platform separately, and budget batteries rarely impress.
  • Power and stroke. Budget jigsaws commonly land in the 3 to 5 amp range (corded) or 12 to 20 volts (cordless), with stroke lengths around 3/4 to 1 inch. That's plenty for plywood, pine, PVC trim, and shelf boards. It is not the tool for thick hardwood or long rip cuts.
  • Blade changes and blade type. Tool-free blade changes save real frustration. Look for a T-shank blade system, since T-shank blades are widely available and cheap to replace. A pack of assorted blades usually runs $10 to $15.
  • Bevel and cut control. A tilting base that bevels to around 45 degrees covers most apartment projects — trimming a door, cutting a shelf, notching a countertop. Variable speed triggers help you start a cut cleanly in thin material.
  • Noise and mess. Jigsaws are loud. In an apartment, that matters more than specs on paper. Plan on ear protection and expect a fine dust trail, so cut on a balcony, in a garage, or on a tarp.

Where the budget actually goes

Under $50 you're choosing between three things: a bare corded saw from a value brand, a cordless bare tool from a brand you'll likely want batteries for later, or a cheap kit that includes blades. The trade-offs are predictable. Cheaper saws usually have more vibration, less precise base plates, and shorter warranties. Owner reviews on value-priced jigsaws frequently mention that the tool is fine for occasional cuts but gets warm during longer sessions.

One note on safety gear, since it's part of the purchase: eye protection and ear plugs are non-negotiable with any saw, and a set of work gloves costs less than a pizza. Budget for that alongside the tool.

What you actually need to buy for the project

A jigsaw alone doesn't cut anything. Most first-apartment woodworking projects need a tape measure, a square, clamps or a work surface, and a way to support the piece you're cutting. If you're hanging shelves or pictures afterward, a small level saves a lot of re-drilling. The Craftsman 9-Inch Torpedo Level is a genuinely useful small-space tool because it fits in a junk drawer and handles picture hanging, shelf alignment, and checking a wobbly table.

For the saw itself, here's the honest situation: the best-known budget jigsaw lines are sold under brands like BLACK+DECKER, Skil, Wen, and Craftsman, and their pricing moves around constantly. I can't hand you a verified under-$50 price on a specific jigsaw model without guessing, and a guessed model number is worse than no recommendation. How to buy the saw itself

When you're standing in the aisle or scrolling listings, check four things in this order. First, length and height in the spec sheet — anything under about 10 inches long stores easily. Second, whether it takes T-shank blades, because that determines how cheap blades will be. Third, the bevel range on the base plate; 45 degrees is the standard and anything less limits you. Fourth, the warranty length, since budget tools vary widely and a longer warranty is your only real insurance at this price.

Buy the saw corded if it will mostly live indoors near an outlet. Buy cordless only if you already own batteries in that brand's system, because a bare tool plus a charger plus a battery usually blows past $50 fast.

The bottom line

For a first apartment, spend your money on a compact corded jigsaw that stores in a bin, a cheap pack of T-shank blades, and the small accessories that make it usable — the Bosch Daredevil Set for pilot holes and the OOK Brand 50 lb. Picture Hanging Kit for the hanging projects that follow. Add the Craftsman 9-Inch Torpedo Level and the DEWALT 27-Piece Hex Key Set and you've covered most of what a small apartment actually throws at you, without buying a workbench you don't have room for.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a corded or cordless jigsaw better for a small apartment?

Corded is usually the better fit, because it's typically cheaper, delivers more consistent power, and doesn't need a charger and spare batteries taking up shelf space. Go cordless only if you already own batteries in that brand's system, since a bare tool plus charger plus battery usually costs well over $50.

How much should I budget for a first jigsaw setup?

Plan on the saw plus a blade pack, which typically runs $10 to $15, and basic safety gear. If you're starting with no tools at all, a compact bit set like the Bosch Daredevil Set at roughly $15 to $25 and a small level cover most of the projects a jigsaw leads into.

Can a budget jigsaw cut shelves and cabinet doors?

Yes, for plywood, pine, PVC trim, and shelf boards. Budget jigsaws commonly have 3 to 5 amp motors and 3/4 to 1 inch strokes, which handles those materials fine but isn't suited to thick hardwood or long rip cuts. Expect more vibration and less precision than a higher-priced saw.
