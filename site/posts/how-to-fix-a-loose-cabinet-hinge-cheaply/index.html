First, Figure Out What Kind of Hinge You Have

Before you buy anything, look at the hinge. Almost all kitchen and bathroom cabinets use one of two types.

  • European-style (concealed) hinges. These are the ones you can't see when the door is closed. They mount to the inside of the door with two screws and clip onto a plate on the cabinet frame. If your cabinet is from IKEA or any builder-grade kitchen made in the last 30 years, it's almost certainly this type.
  • Exposed hinges. These are visible on the face of the door. Common on older cabinets and furniture-style vanities. Usually held on with two or three wood screws.

European hinges have adjustment screws built in, which is why they're the easier fix. Exposed hinges usually just need new screws or a little wood filler.

The 60-Second Fix: Tighten the Adjustment Screws

Most loose cabinet doors aren't broken. They're just out of adjustment. On a European hinge you'll find two small screws on the hinge body:

  • One screw moves the door left and right (side adjustment).
  • One screw moves the door in and out (depth adjustment).
  • A third screw on the mounting plate moves the door up and down.

Loosen them a quarter turn, nudge the door until it hangs straight, then tighten. If the door sags because the screws are simply loose, this alone fixes it. Cost: $0.

If the door still sags after tightening, the screws aren't holding. That's a stripped hole problem, and that's where the cheap fixes come in.

Fix Stripped Screw Holes for Pennies

When a screw spins in its hole, the wood fibers around it have given up. You have three cheap options, and you probably have two of them in your kitchen right now.

Toothpick and wood glue method

This is the classic renter fix. It costs nothing if you have toothpicks and glue.

  1. Squeeze a small amount of wood glue into the hole.
  2. Push 3 to 5 wooden toothpicks into the hole, packing them tight.
  3. Snap them off flush with the surface.
  4. Let it dry for about an hour.
  5. Drive the original screw back in.

Owner reviews and DIY forums consistently report this holds for years on light doors. It's less reliable on heavy solid-wood doors.

Longer screws

If the hole isn't completely stripped, a slightly longer screw can bite into fresh wood. Go up one size in length, not width, so you don't split the door. A small pack of assorted wood screws usually runs $3 to $6 at a hardware store.

Wood filler or epoxy

For a badly wallowed-out hole, a two-part wood filler or epoxy putty is the strongest option. You fill the hole, let it cure, then drill a small pilot hole and re-drive the screw. A small tube typically costs $4 to $8, but you'll only use a fraction of it.

When You Actually Need Replacement Parts

Sometimes the hinge itself is bent, cracked, or missing a screw, and no amount of adjustment helps. This is where small-space renters catch a break: cabinet hinges are one of the most standardized, widely available parts in the world. You do not need the original manufacturer.

Here's what makes finding a replacement easy:

  • They're sold in multipacks. A pack of 10 European hinges with mounting plates usually costs $8 to $15, which works out to under a dollar per hinge.
  • The sizing is standardized. Most European hinges use a 35mm cup bore and either a 2-inch or 2.5-inch overlay. Measure your cup diameter with a ruler and you're 90% of the way there.
  • Big-box stores stock them. Home Depot and Lowe's both carry common sizes in the hardware aisle.
  • Amazon has them next-day. Search "35mm European cabinet hinge" and you'll get dozens of matching options.

If you only need one hinge, buying a single replacement is often cheaper than a pack. But if your cabinets are 10+ years old, buy the pack. When one hinge fails, its neighbors are usually not far behind.

A Step-by-Step Fix for a Sagging Door

Here's the full sequence, from cheapest to most involved.

  1. Open the door and look at the hinge. Identify whether it's European or exposed.
  2. Tighten every visible screw. Use a screwdriver that fits the head properly; a loose-fitting driver strips the screw.
  3. Check the door alignment. If it still sags, loosen the adjustment screws, reposition, and retighten.
  4. If screws spin, address the hole. Toothpicks and glue first, then longer screws, then wood filler.
  5. If the hinge is bent or cracked, replace it. Match the cup diameter and overlay measurement.
  6. Test the door. Open and close it 10 times. Listen for scraping and check the gap around the edges.

Most people stop at step 3 or 4. That's the whole repair, and it costs less than a coffee.

Keeping It Fixed

A hinge that keeps coming loose usually has a root cause. Heavy doors with only two hinges will sag again; adding a third hinge in the middle is a $5 fix that solves it permanently. Overloaded doors (think a cabinet stuffed with cast iron) stress the hinges beyond their rating. And humidity swings in small apartments can loosen screws over time, so a quick tighten once a year keeps things solid.

If you're renting, none of these fixes are permanent. Toothpicks and glue come out with a screwdriver if you ever need to. Replacing a hinge means keeping the old one in a drawer so you can swap it back at move-out.

The Bottom Line

A loose cabinet hinge is almost never a real repair. It's a tightening job, a stripped-hole job, or a $1 replacement part. For under $5 you can fix it yourself in under an hour with a screwdriver, some toothpicks, and a little patience. The parts are standardized, widely stocked, and cheap, which makes this one of the friendliest DIY fixes for anyone in a small rental.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I fix a loose cabinet hinge without any tools?

Not really. You need at least a screwdriver that fits the screw head. If you don't own one, a small multi-bit screwdriver usually costs $5 to $10 and will handle this job plus most furniture assembly. Trying to tighten a screw with a butter knife or coin tends to strip the head and make things worse.

Why does my cabinet door keep coming loose after I tighten it?

The screw holes are probably stripped, or the door is too heavy for the number of hinges it has. Try the toothpick-and-glue method first. If the door is solid wood or holds heavy items, adding a third hinge in the middle is the most reliable long-term fix and typically costs under $5.

Do I need to match the exact brand of my existing hinge?

No. European cabinet hinges are standardized by cup diameter (usually 35mm) and overlay. Measure your existing hinge's cup and overlay, then buy any matching replacement. A pack of 10 usually costs $8 to $15, so you'll have spares for the next hinge that fails.
