Why Is My Wi-Fi Slow in One Room? Cheap Fixes to Try First

If your Wi-Fi crawls in exactly one room while the rest of the place streams fine, you're not imagining it, and nothing is broken. One-room dead zones are usually caused by distance, walls, or interference, and most fixes cost less than a pizza.

The single biggest mistake people make is buying a new router before they've checked anything else. A faster router won't push signal through a concrete wall any better than your current one, and it won't help if the real problem is a microwave, a thick door, or a device that's simply too far away. Diagnose first. Spend later, and spend small.

Cause 1: Distance and too many walls

How to confirm it: Walk from the router to the slow room with your phone and watch the Wi-Fi bars drop. If the signal fades gradually as you go, distance is your problem. Wi-Fi at 2.4 GHz travels farther but slower; 5 GHz is faster but fades much quicker, which is why a room that's fine on 2.4 GHz can be useless on 5 GHz.

The fix: Move the router. Not up on a shelf behind the TV, but out in the open, ideally central to where you actually use the internet. If you're in a dorm and can't move it, switch that one device to the 2.4 GHz band in your settings. It's slower on paper, but a stable 2.4 GHz connection beats a 5 GHz one that keeps dropping.

What it costs: $0.

Cause 2: Physical obstructions (the dorm special)

How to confirm it: Note where the signal dies. Does it happen right at a brick wall, a metal door, or a mirror? Concrete, brick, metal, and water (yes, including a fish tank or a full bookshelf) all eat Wi-Fi. Dense cinderblock dorm walls are famously brutal.

The fix: You can't knock down a wall, but you can influence what's around it. Keep the router away from metal furniture and appliances. If the slow room has a door you keep shut, a simple Door Draft Stopper won't help the Wi-Fi itself, but it's worth knowing that most "signal blockers" sold online are gimmicks. Repositioning is free and works better than any sticker or foil attachment.

What it costs: $0 to about $15 if you're chasing airflow or draft fixes instead.

Cause 3: Interference from other electronics

How to confirm it: Does the Wi-Fi get worse when the microwave runs, or in the evening when neighbors are home? If the slowdown is time-of-day dependent, interference is likely. In an apartment or dorm, you may be sharing airspace with dozens of other networks. A free Wi-Fi analyzer app on your phone will show you how crowded your channel is.

The fix: Log into your router's settings and change the channel. For 2.4 GHz, channels 1, 6, and 11 are the only ones that don't overlap; pick whichever is least crowded. Move the router away from the microwave, cordless phone base, and baby monitor. If you can't change channels (some dorm Wi-Fi is locked down), just move your device closer to the router when you need a stable call.

What it costs: $0.

Cause 4: The router itself is old or weak

How to confirm it: Check the model number on the router. If it's more than five or six years old, or it's the basic unit your internet provider handed out for free, it may simply not have the range. Owner reviews on basic ISP routers often mention exactly this: fine in one room, useless two rooms over.

The fix: Before buying anything, ask your provider if they'll swap the router for a newer model, often free or a few dollars a month. If you're buying your own, look for a router with external antennas and at least Wi-Fi 5 (802.11ac); Wi-Fi 6 is better if the budget allows. A mesh system is the real answer for a whole apartment, but it costs more.

What it costs: Free swap from your provider, or typically $40 to $80 for a decent entry-level router, $100 to $200 for a mesh setup.

Cause 5: Too many devices on one connection

How to confirm it: Count what's connected. Phones, laptops, a smart TV, a game console, a streaming stick, smart speakers, and a roommate's laptop all share the same pipe. If everything slows down in the evening, it's congestion, not coverage.

The fix: Pause big downloads and updates when you need bandwidth. On many routers you can prioritize your device in the settings (called QoS). If you're in a dorm, this is often out of your control, so schedule heavy tasks like game updates for off-peak hours.

What it costs: $0.

Cause 6: Your device, not the network

How to confirm it: Test a second device in the same slow room. If your laptop is slow but your phone is fine, the problem is the laptop's Wi-Fi card or its settings, not the router.

The fix: Update your Wi-Fi drivers, forget and rejoin the network, and turn off any "power saving" mode that throttles the adapter. A cheap USB Wi-Fi adapter with an external antenna can genuinely rescue an older laptop.

What it costs: $0 for settings, typically $15 to $30 for a USB adapter.

When to stop and replace it instead

If you've tried repositioning, switching bands, and changing channels, and one room is still dead, you've hit the limit of what a single router can do. This is the point to stop troubleshooting and add hardware.

For a single stubborn room, a Wi-Fi extender or mesh node placed roughly halfway between the router and the dead zone usually fixes it. Expect to pay typically $30 to $60 for a basic extender and $100 and up for a mesh system. Buy from a brand with a real support page and a return window, because extenders are hit-or-miss depending on your walls.

If your router is the bottleneck, replace it rather than stacking extenders on top of a weak signal. Extenders repeat a bad signal; they don't create a good one.

Preventing this next time

Place your router centrally and high, not in a closet or behind a TV. Note which band each device uses and put far-away devices on 2.4 GHz. Keep the router away from microwaves, metal, and water. And before you buy any "signal booster" gadget, check whether your provider will just upgrade your router for free. That single phone call solves more cases than any accessory.

The bottom line

Slow Wi-Fi in one room is almost always about distance, walls, or interference, and the free fixes (repositioning, switching bands, changing channels) solve most cases. Don't buy a new router first. Work through the cheap fixes, and only spend money when you've confirmed the router itself is the weak link.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 2.4 GHz or 5 GHz better for a far-away room?

2.4 GHz travels farther through walls but is slower, while 5 GHz is faster but fades quickly with distance. For a room far from the router, a stable 2.4 GHz connection usually beats a 5 GHz one that keeps dropping.

Will a Wi-Fi extender fix a dead zone in one room?

Often, yes, if you place it roughly halfway between the router and the dead zone. Expect to pay typically $30 to $60 for a basic extender, but results vary depending on your walls, so buy from a brand with a return window.

Should I buy a new router or just move the old one?

Move it first. Repositioning the router centrally and out in the open costs nothing and solves many one-room problems. Only replace the router if it's more than five or six years old or a weak provider unit, and ask your provider about a free swap before buying your own.
