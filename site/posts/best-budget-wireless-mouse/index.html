If you're furnishing a first apartment together, the single question that decides your mouse is this: how much time each week are you willing to spend cleaning it? A cheap mouse that traps crumbs in a scroll wheel groove will quietly cost you five minutes every Sunday. A slightly better one wipes clean in ten seconds. That difference, multiplied over a couple of years, matters more than any spec on the box.

What actually matters

  • Cleaning time. Look at the gap around the scroll wheel and the seam between the top shell and the base. Open grooves collect crumbs and dust; a smooth, sealed top wipes clean in seconds. This is the number one factor for a shared desk where food lives nearby.
  • Surface material. Matte plastic with a slight texture resists fingerprints and wipes down with a damp cloth. Glossy plastic shows every smear and needs more wiping.
  • Battery type and life. A single AA that lasts months is easier to live with than a built-in battery you have to remember to charge. Published specs on budget models usually claim six months to two years on one cell, and that varies a lot by model and by how much you use it.
  • Connection. A tiny USB receiver stored in the mouse is the most reliable for a budget pick. Bluetooth is convenient but can be fussier when two people share one laptop.
  • Size and grip. A mouse that's too small makes your hand cramp after an hour of spreadsheets. Medium hands generally do best with a mouse around 4 to 4.5 inches long.
  • Price band. Under $25 is the target here. At that price you're trading extra buttons and fancy sensors for reliability, and that's usually the right trade for a first apartment.

Logitech K350

Rough price band: typically $25 to $40, sometimes dipping under $25 on sale.

The K350 is a keyboard, not a mouse, so why is it here? Because the most common first-apartment mistake is buying a mouse and keyboard separately and ending up with two receivers, two battery habits, and two things to clean. The K350 uses Logitech's unifying receiver, and owner reviews consistently mention the cushioned palm rest and the curved key shape as comfortable for long typing sessions. If you buy a Logitech mouse alongside it, one receiver can often handle both.

Downsides: it's a full-size keyboard, so it eats desk space, and the wave shape isn't for everyone. The palm rest is a fabric-ish surface, which means it needs a wipe more often than plain plastic.

Who should buy it: couples who type a lot and want one receiver and one cleaning routine for the whole desk.

Microsoft Sculpt Ergonomic

Rough price band: usually $60 to $90 for the desktop set.

This is the upgrade pick if wrist pain is already showing up. The Sculpt Ergonomic set includes a dome-shaped mouse that keeps your hand in a more natural position, and reviewers often mention that the shape takes a week to get used to and then feels normal. The separate number pad is a genuine plus for a small apartment desk, because you can push it aside when you don't need it.

Downsides: it's well above the $25 target, the dome mouse is polarizing, and the soft-touch coating on the mouse picks up skin oils and needs a wipe every few days.

Who should buy it: anyone who's already feeling wrist strain and is willing to spend more to fix it.

Kensington Pro Fit Ergo

Rough price band: typically $20 to $35.

This is the closest thing here to a true budget ergonomic mouse. It's a vertical-style design, which means your hand rests on its side rather than flat, and the smooth shell has very few crevices for crumbs to hide in. Owner reviews frequently note that a vertical mouse reduces forearm twist during long sessions.

Downsides: vertical mice have a real adjustment period, often a week or two, and they're not great for fast gaming. The scroll wheel area still needs occasional attention.

Who should buy it: a couple where one person works long hours at the desk and wants ergonomics without the Sculpt price.

Kensington Pro Fit Ergo

Perixx PERIBOARD-512

Rough price band: usually $30 to $50.

Perixx makes split ergonomic keyboards, and the PERIBOARD-512 is the budget entry into that category. The split layout angles each half of the keyboard so your wrists stay straighter. For a shared desk, the appeal is that it's a single unit, so there's no cable clutter between halves.

Downsides: split keyboards have a steep learning curve, sometimes two to three weeks before typing speed returns. It's also a keyboard, so it doesn't solve your mouse problem by itself.

Who should buy it: typists with wrist discomfort who want the split-keyboard benefit at the lowest reasonable price.

Adesso Tru-Form 150

Rough price band: typically $30 to $45.

The Tru-Form 150 is another split ergonomic keyboard, and it's often the cheapest way to try the layout. It has a contoured shape with a raised middle section, and reviewers often describe it as comfortable for all-day typing once you adapt.

Downsides: build quality is described in owner reviews as functional rather than premium, and the plastic is the kind that shows dust, so it needs a wipe weekly. Same learning curve as any split board.

Who should buy it: a couple who wants to test whether a split keyboard helps before spending more.

Adesso Tru-Form 150

Dell KB700

Rough price band: usually $40 to $60.

The KB700 is a multi-device wireless keyboard that can pair with three devices and switch between them with a key press. For a first apartment with one shared laptop and maybe a tablet, that's genuinely useful. The top surface is smooth, which makes it one of the easier keyboards here to wipe down.

Downsides: it's a keyboard, so pair it with a mouse, and the low-profile keys aren't for people who like deep key travel.

Who should buy it: a couple sharing one laptop who want to switch between devices without unplugging anything.

Dell KB700

Anker Soundcore P20i

Rough price band: typically $20 to $30.

Not a mouse, but worth a line here because a shared apartment desk is often a shared audio space. These are budget earbuds with a published battery life in the range of several hours per charge plus extra from the case, and owner reviews consistently call them good value at this price.

Downsides: they're earbuds, so they don't help your mouse situation at all, and the case is another small thing to keep track of on a crowded desk.

Who should buy it: one of you who needs to focus while the other is on a call.

Sony WH-CH520

Rough price band: usually $40 to $60.

Over-ear headphones rather than earbuds, and the smooth plastic headband and earcups wipe clean easily, which matters if you eat at the desk. Published specs put battery life in the range of several dozen hours per charge, and reviewers often mention comfort for long wear.

Downsides: over-ear headphones get warm, and the earcup pads are the part that collects the most oil and needs the most attention.

Who should buy it: a couple where one person takes a lot of calls and wants something easy to wipe down.

The bottom line

If you want the shortest answer: buy a smooth-topped mouse in the $15 to $25 range with a single AA battery and a stored USB receiver, and keep a microfiber cloth on the desk. That combination usually means about ten seconds of cleaning a week instead of five minutes. If wrist pain is already a factor, the Kensington Pro Fit Ergo is the budget ergonomic pick, and the Microsoft Sculpt Ergonomic is the upgrade. Everything else on this list is about making the rest of the desk easier to live with and easier to wipe down.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often do I actually need to clean a budget wireless mouse?

For most people, once a week is enough, and it takes about ten seconds on a smooth-topped model. If you eat at the desk, expect to wipe the scroll wheel area every few days instead.

Is a vertical mouse worth it at this price?

The Kensington Pro Fit Ergo sits in the typically $20 to $35 band, which is the cheapest way to try the vertical shape. Owner reviews often mention a one to two week adjustment period before it feels normal.

Do I need a separate receiver for each device?

Not necessarily. The Logitech K350 uses a unifying receiver that can often handle a matching mouse too, and the Dell KB700 can switch between three devices on its own. That cuts down on both clutter and the number of things you have to keep clean.
