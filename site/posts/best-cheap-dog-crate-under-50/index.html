Here's the catch most "under $50" lists skip: the crate is the cheap part. Over twelve months, the crate, a bed, a chew or two, and a couple of replacement parts typically add up to $75–$150 total — and the biggest swing factor isn't the crate price, it's whether you buy the right size once. A crate that's too small gets chewed, scratched, and replaced. So we're weighting this guide toward total first-year cost, not the sticker on the box.

What actually matters

  • Size, measured right. A crate should let your dog stand without ducking, turn around, and lie flat. Measure height at the head and length from nose to tail base, then add 2–4 inches. Sizing wrong is the single most common reason people buy twice.
  • Wire gauge and latch quality. Budget crates usually use thinner wire and single-point latches. Owner reviews consistently flag flimsy latches as the first failure point, so look for double latches or a slide bolt.
  • Included divider panels. A divider lets a puppy grow into an adult-size crate instead of forcing a mid-year upgrade — that alone can save $40–$60 in year one.
  • Assembly and fold-down. Most sub-$50 crates ship flat and fold for travel. Check whether assembly needs tools; some budget models require a screwdriver and 20 minutes.
  • Coating and floor tray. Powder-coated wire resists rust better than painted wire. A removable plastic tray makes cleanup a 2-minute job instead of a scrub.
  • Replacement-part availability. Chews happen. Brands that sell replacement trays and pans separately keep a $45 crate from becoming a $45 loss.

What the first year actually costs

Budget for the crate at $30–$55. A crate pad or bed runs $15–$30. A chew or two to redirect teething — a KONG Classic or a Nylabone Dura Chew — adds $8–$15. If your dog is a heavy chewer, a Kong Extreme is the tougher version and usually runs $12–$20. A replacement tray, if needed, is typically $10–$20. Total: roughly $75–$150 for year one, and the crate is less than half of it. That's the number to plan around, not the shelf price.

If you're crate-training alongside a gate to keep the dog out of the kitchen, a North States Supergate or a Richell Wood Pet Gate is a separate purchase, usually $40–$90, and worth budgeting for if you have stairs or a hot stove.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a cheap dog crate really cost over the first year?

Plan on roughly $75–$150 total. The crate itself is typically $30–$55, a bed adds $15–$30, chews add $8–$20, and a replacement tray if needed runs $10–$20.

Do I need a crate with a divider panel?

For a puppy, yes — a divider lets a small dog use an adult-size crate and grow into it, which avoids buying a second crate mid-year. For an adult dog already at full size, a divider matters much less.
