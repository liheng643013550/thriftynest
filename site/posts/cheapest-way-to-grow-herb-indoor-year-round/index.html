The Cheapest Way to Grow Herbs Indoors Year Round

You're standing in the grocery store at 6 p.m. with a carton of basil that costs more than a gallon of gas, and you know half of it will be brown slime by Thursday. You've thought about growing your own on the kitchen windowsill, but every setup you've seen online looks like a science fair project — grow lights, timers, fans, a tent that hums all night next to your kid's bedroom wall. You don't have time to research this, and you definitely don't want something that keeps the whole house awake.

Here's the honest version: the cheapest indoor herb setup is a sunny window, a $4 bag of potting mix, and a few dollars of seed. The moment you add artificial light, you add cost — and you also add the one thing almost no guide warns you about: noise. This article covers both, in the order that actually matters for a busy parent.

Start With the Free Thing First

Before you buy anything, figure out what your windows already give you. Herbs generally want 6 to 8 hours of decent light a day. A south-facing window usually delivers the most; east and west are workable; a north-facing window typically isn't enough on its own in winter.

Do a one-day test. Pick the sill you'd actually use, check it at 10 a.m., 1 p.m., and 4 p.m., and see whether you can cast a shadow there. If yes, you may not need a grow light at all for part of the year.

Two cheap realities to accept up front:

  • Winter daylight in most of the US drops below what basil, dill, and cilantro need. They will stretch, pale, and stall.
  • Rosemary, thyme, oregano, and mint are far more forgiving of a mediocre window than basil is.

If your only good window faces north, or you live somewhere with genuinely dark winters, skip to the lighting section — but read the noise part carefully, because that's where most cheap setups fail.

Pick Herbs That Don't Fight You

The cheapest plant is the one that doesn't die. For a beginner with limited light, start with the tough ones.

  • Mint — nearly indestructible, but keep it in its own pot or it takes over everything.
  • Chives — tolerates lower light than most, and you cut it rather than replant it.
  • Thyme and oregano — slow, steady, hard to kill.
  • Basil — the one everyone wants, and the one that needs the most light and warmth.
  • Rosemary — great flavor, fussy about drainage and dry air.

Buying seeds is the cheapest route by far — a packet typically runs $2 to $4 and gives you many plantings. Buying a started plant costs more up front but saves you 3 to 6 weeks. A middle path that many gardeners use: buy one healthy supermarket herb plant, divide it into two or three pots, and let it regrow.

The Potting Mix and Container Question

Don't use garden soil from outside. It compacts in a pot and often carries pests. Any bagged indoor potting mix works; a standard 8-quart bag is usually enough for four to six small pots and typically costs under $10.

For containers, the cheapest option is free: clean out yogurt tubs, takeout containers, or a 1-gallon nursery pot you already own. Whatever you use, it needs drainage holes. Drill or poke them yourself. A saucer underneath protects the sill.

If you'd rather buy one bag of mix that covers a whole season of repotting, Miracle-Gro Potting Mix is a widely available standard choice. Owner reviews frequently mention it draining well for herbs and houseplants, though a few note it can run a bit heavy on moisture retention, so you may want to mix in a handful of perlite if your pots are small.

The Real Cost Driver: Light, and the Noise Nobody Mentions

This is the part that decides whether your setup is $15 or $150.

A sunny window costs nothing. A basic LED grow light runs roughly $20 to $40 for a small bar or bulb-style unit, and it adds a few dollars a month to your electric bill at most — a small 20- to 40-watt LED used 12 to 14 hours a day typically costs somewhere in the range of $1 to $3 per month depending on your local rate. That's cheap. The problem isn't the electricity.

The problem is the fan.

Almost every "complete" grow light kit includes a cooling fan, and cheap ones are loud. Owner reviews of budget grow-light kits commonly complain about a constant whirring or buzzing that's noticeable in a quiet room. If that light is in a hallway outside a bedroom, or in a living room where someone sleeps on the couch, it becomes a nightly argument.

Here's how to keep it quiet:

  • Skip kits with built-in fans entirely. Modern LED grow bulbs and bar lights run cool enough that many don't need a fan at all. A passive, fanless LED bar is the single best noise decision you can make.
  • Avoid anything described as a "complete system" or "tent kit" for a windowsill herb project. Those include inline fans, and inline fans are the loudest part of any grow setup.
  • If you must run a fan, put it on a mechanical timer that shuts off at night. Herbs don't need airflow while the light is off, and a fan that runs 24/7 is both pointless and the thing your neighbor will hear through a shared wall.
  • Check the decibel rating if it's listed. A fanless LED bar has no rating because there's nothing spinning. That's the point.

For a typical apartment or a bedroom-adjacent kitchen, a fanless LED bar light is the difference between "I forgot it was on" and "why is that buzzing again."

Watering, Feeding, and the $3 That Saves Your Plants

Overwatering kills more indoor herbs than darkness does. Water when the top inch of soil feels dry — for small pots in winter, that's often once every 5 to 7 days, not daily. A $3 spray bottle or a small watering can with a narrow spout makes this easier than hauling a jug.

Feeding is optional and cheap. A basic liquid houseplant fertilizer diluted to half strength, given once every 3 to 4 weeks during active growth, is plenty. A small bottle typically lasts a full year for a windowsill's worth of herbs and costs under $10.

If you want one tidy, low-mess tool for watering small pots without soaking the sill, OXO Good Grips 1.75 Gallon is a small watering can that owner reviews often describe as easy to pour precisely. It's not required — a rinsed milk jug works — but it's the kind of thing that makes the routine actually happen.

A Realistic Year-Round Schedule

Indoor herb growing isn't constant. It has a rhythm, and knowing it saves you a lot of frustration.

  • Spring and summer: your window may be enough. Rotate pots a quarter turn every few days so they grow straight.
  • Fall: daylight drops fast. This is when most people add a light. Start it at 12 hours a day.
  • Winter: expect slower growth no matter what you do. Cut back on watering and skip fertilizer for most herbs. Basil will likely struggle; lean on chives, mint, and thyme.
  • Late winter: start new seeds under the light so you have fresh plants ready for spring.

Total realistic annual cost for a windowsill setup with one fanless LED bar: roughly $40 to $70 in the first year, then $10 to $20 a year after that for mix, seed, and the small electricity bump. Compare that to $3 to $4 per grocery-store herb bunch, and it pays for itself in a season or two.

What to Skip

A few things that sound helpful but usually aren't worth it for a small indoor herb project:

  • Humidifiers and misters. Most kitchens are humid enough. Misting does little for herbs and can encourage mold on leaves.
  • Self-watering globes. They're cheap, but they tend to keep soil too wet for rosemary and thyme.
  • Grow tents. Great for tomatoes, overkill for a windowsill, and they're where the noise comes from.
  • Heat mats after germination. Useful for starting seeds, unnecessary once plants are up.

The Bottom Line

The cheapest way to grow herbs indoors year round is to use the light you already have, grow the tough herbs first, and buy potting mix and seed rather than started plants. When winter forces you to add light, choose a fanless LED bar — it costs a few dollars a month to run and, just as importantly, it stays silent, so it won't disturb sleep or annoy a neighbor through a shared wall. You don't need a kit, a tent, or a fan. You need a pot with a hole in the bottom and a window that gets some sun.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I really need a grow light, or is a windowsill enough?

For part of the year, a sunny south- or west-facing window that gets 6 to 8 hours of light is often enough, especially for chives, mint, thyme, and oregano. In winter, daylight usually drops below what basil, dill, and cilantro need, and that's when most people add a small fanless LED bar light.

How much does a basic indoor herb setup cost to run?

A small 20- to 40-watt LED grow light used 12 to 14 hours a day typically adds only about $1 to $3 per month to your electric bill, depending on local rates. The bigger first-year cost is the light itself, usually $20 to $40, plus potting mix and seed.

Why does noise matter so much with grow lights?

Many budget grow-light kits include a cooling fan, and owner reviews often complain about constant whirring or buzzing in a quiet room. Choosing a fanless LED bar avoids the problem entirely, which matters if the light sits near a bedroom or a shared wall with a neighbor.
