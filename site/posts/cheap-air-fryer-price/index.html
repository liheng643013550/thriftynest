The Price Tag Is Only Half the Story

A $40 air fryer and an $80 air fryer can both cook a bag of frozen fries. What separates them, over two or three years, is how much electricity they burn and whether the nonstick coating and heating element survive that long. If you hate replacing things, the sticker price is the least interesting number in the room.

What Actually Matters

  • Wattage and cook time together. Most budget air fryers draw somewhere in the 1,200–1,500W range; larger 5–6 quart models often run 1,500–1,700W. A higher-watt unit cooks faster, so it can use less total energy per batch even though it pulls more at peak. Specs give you the watts; only your cooking habits tell you the minutes.
  • Capacity versus household size. A 2-quart basket is a one-person machine. A 5–6 quart model feeds two to four people in one batch. Buying too small means running two cycles, which doubles your energy use and your patience cost.
  • Nonstick coating quality. Owner reviews consistently flag flaking baskets as the number one failure. A dishwasher-safe, ceramic or well-reviewed coated basket tends to last longer than the cheapest bare nonstick.
  • Mechanical versus digital controls. Dial models have fewer parts to fail and usually cost less. Digital models add presets and a timer, which matters if you walk away during cooking.
  • Warranty length. Budget brands typically offer 1 year; some mainstream names go 2 years. That warranty is your only real protection against a dead heating element in month 14.
  • Replacement part availability. A basket you can buy separately extends the machine's life. A basket you can't turns the whole unit into e-waste.

Where the Money Goes: Energy Use and Running Cost

Here's the honest math. A typical budget air fryer draws about 1,400W. If you run it 20 minutes a day, that's roughly 0.47 kWh per day, or about 14 kWh per month. At the US average electricity rate, that's usually in the range of $1.50–$2.50 per month — call it $20–$30 a year for daily use.

That number changes fast with three variables. Run it 40 minutes a day instead of 20 and you double it. Buy a larger, higher-watt model and you might add 20–30%. Live somewhere with expensive power and the same machine costs more to run than a cheaper one elsewhere.

The flip side: an air fryer heats a small chamber, so it usually beats a full-size oven for small batches. A standard oven preheats for 10–15 minutes and heats a much bigger cavity. If you're cooking for one or two, the air fryer is often the cheaper appliance to run, not just the cheaper one to buy. If you're cooking for five, the oven wins.

So when you compare cheap air fryer prices, compare them on cost-per-year, not cost-per-today. A $10 difference at checkout is nothing next to a machine that dies in a year.

The Bottom Line

The cheapest air fryer is rarely the cheapest to own. A $45 compact model running 20 minutes a day costs roughly $1.50–$2.50 a month in electricity — the same order of magnitude as a $100 machine doing the same job. What actually changes the math is capacity (buy too small and you run two cycles), wattage (bigger units draw more), and how long the thing survives.

If you cook for one, the Dash Compact Air Fryer keeps both the purchase price and the running cost at the bottom of the range. If you cook for two or more, the Gourmia 6-Quart is the honest value pick, and the Cosori Pro LE is what you buy when you're tired of replacing cheap ones. Skip the Instant Pot Duo 6 Qt unless you genuinely want the pressure cooker too — as a pure air fryer, the numbers don't work.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does an air fryer cost to run per month?

A typical budget air fryer drawing about 1,400W for 20 minutes a day uses roughly 14 kWh a month, which usually lands around $1.50–$2.50 at average US electricity rates. Larger 6-quart units and longer cook times push that higher, often into the $2–$3 range.

Is a bigger air fryer more expensive to run?

Usually yes, but not dramatically. A 6-quart model like the Gourmia 6-Quart or Amazon Basics 6-Quart draws more power than a compact unit like the Dash Compact Air Fryer, but it also cooks a full meal in one batch instead of two. Doing one cycle instead of two often cancels out the extra wattage.

What's the cheapest air fryer on this list?

The Dash Compact Air Fryer, typically around $40–$60, is the lowest-priced option and also the cheapest to run because of its small capacity and lower wattage. Just be aware that 2 quarts only cooks for one person at a time.
