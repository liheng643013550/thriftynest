It's 11 p.m., you're hungry, and the only "kitchen" you have is a two-foot stretch of dorm desk next to your laptop. You want hot leftovers, popcorn, or a mug of ramen — not a machine that hogs your whole countertop or trips the outlet every time you run it. A cheap microwave that actually fits a small space solves that problem, and this guide is about finding the one that fits yours.

What actually matters

  • Footprint, not just capacity. A "compact" microwave usually runs about 0.7 to 0.9 cubic feet and takes up roughly 17 to 19 inches of width and 12 to 14 inches of depth. Measure your shelf or desk before you buy anything.
  • Wattage and cooking time. Budget compact models are typically 700 to 900 watts. Lower wattage means food takes longer, so a 700W unit might need an extra minute or two versus a 900W one.
  • Door swing and clearance. You need room for the door to open plus a couple of inches of breathing room on the sides and back for ventilation.
  • Controls you'll actually use. Simple dial or one-touch presets beat a 20-button panel you'll never learn. For dorm life, a popcorn button and a quick-start minute button cover most of it.
  • Noise and light. Cheap microwaves are louder than you'd expect. Owner reviews often mention this, so don't be surprised.
  • Price band. Most compact budget microwaves land somewhere in the $60 to $120 range depending on wattage and brand.

What to know before you buy

Small microwaves trade power for size. A 700W compact will heat a burrito fine but takes noticeably longer than a full-size 1,000W+ unit. If you mostly reheat single servings, that's a fair trade. If you cook for two or more, you may want to step up to 900W.

Also check the plug. Most run on standard 120V household outlets, but a microwave plus a mini-fridge plus a laptop charger on one dorm circuit can trip a breaker. Run the microwave on its own outlet if you can.

Amazon Basics 6-Quart

Rough price band: typically under $40.

What makes it a good pick: If your "kitchen" is a dorm desk, a 6-quart slow cooker sounds like overkill — but it's the opposite. You dump ingredients in the morning, and dinner is ready when you get back from class. No stove, no microwave required, no babysitting. The 6-quart size makes enough for two or three meals, which means leftovers you can then reheat in your microwave.

Real downsides: It's bulky. A 6-quart cooker takes up real counter or shelf space, so measure first. It's also slow by design — this is not a "I'm hungry right now" appliance.

Who should buy it: Students who want to batch-cook once and eat for days, and who have a shelf or floor corner to spare.

Dash Mini Rice Cooker

Rough price band: usually $20 to $30.

What makes it a good pick: This is the footprint champion. A mini rice cooker takes up roughly the space of a large coffee mug and sits happily on a dorm desk. It cooks rice, oatmeal, and even steams small portions of veggies. For a student who eats a lot of rice, it's a genuinely useful $25.

Real downsides: Capacity is tiny — think one to two servings. It's not a meal cooker, it's a side-dish cooker. Owner reviews often note it's slow and that the nonstick coating needs gentle handling.

Who should buy it: One person, one bowl of rice, minimal counter space.

Aroma Housewares 6-Cup

Rough price band: typically $30 to $45.

What makes it a good pick: A step up from the mini — 6 cups cooked is enough for a few meals, and many models in this line include a steamer tray so you can cook rice and steam protein at the same time. It's still compact enough for a small kitchen shelf.

Real downsides: It's bigger than the Dash Mini, so it needs more room. The steamer tray and inner pot are hand-wash items on most versions.

Who should buy it: Students who cook rice regularly and want leftovers, not just one bowl.

Dash Compact Air Fryer

Rough price band: usually $40 to $60.

What makes it a good pick: A compact air fryer gives you crispy food without a full oven — frozen fries, nuggets, roasted veggies. The "compact" in the name matters here: it's designed for small counters, and it heats faster than a full-size air fryer because there's less volume.

Real downsides: Small basket means small batches. You'll cook in rounds if you're feeding more than one person. It's also louder than you'd guess.

Who should buy it: Anyone in a dorm or small apartment who misses crispy food and has no oven.

Who should buy it: Students who want actual baking and toasting capability, not just reheating.
