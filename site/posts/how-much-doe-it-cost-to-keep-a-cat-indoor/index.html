How Much Does It Cost to Keep an Indoor Cat Per Year?

Keeping an indoor cat in a small rental usually runs somewhere between $600 and $1,400 in the first year, and $450 to $950 a year after that. The gap between those two numbers is almost entirely about stuff — litter, food, a place to scratch, and one or two vet visits — not about how many square feet you have.

The quick answer

For a single indoor cat in a small space, expect:

  • First year: roughly $600 to $1,400. This includes startup gear (litter box, scratcher, carrier, bowls) plus a spay/neuter or a first vet visit if it hasn't happened yet.
  • Ongoing years: roughly $450 to $950. Food and litter are the two big recurring lines. Everything else is small.
  • Monthly average after year one: about $40 to $80, depending heavily on what you feed and whether you buy litter in bulk.

What this range can do: cover a healthy adult cat, decent kibble, clumping litter, a scratching post, and routine vet care. What it can't do: absorb a dental cleaning, a blocked urinary tract, or an emergency visit. Those are typically $300 to $2,000+ depending on the problem, and they're the single biggest reason a "cheap" cat stops being cheap. Pet insurance usually runs a few hundred dollars a year if you want to smooth that out.

The good news for a small-space renter: your square footage barely matters. Your storage matters more than your floor plan.

How the number is built

There's no wattage formula here — a cat isn't an appliance. The math is simpler than that. It's consumption times frequency, plus a gear budget, plus a vet budget.

Food. A typical adult cat eats roughly 5 to 8 ounces of dry food a day, or about 5 to 6 ounces of wet. A 16-pound bag of dry food lasts a single cat roughly 5 to 7 weeks. Two to three cases of wet food a month is common if you feed wet only.

Litter. One cat, one box, scooped daily and fully changed every 2 to 4 weeks. A 20 to 40 pound box or jug typically lasts one to three months.

Gear. Bought once, replaced when it wears out. Scratching posts and cardboard scratchers are the items that get destroyed fastest.

Vet. An annual exam plus core vaccines typically lands in the $100 to $250 range in most US cities. It varies a lot by region.

Stack those four lines and you land in the range above. The variables are what move you up or down.

What pushes the cost up in a small space

Cats need vertical territory. In a studio or a 400-square-foot rental, you can't spread out horizontally, so you buy height instead — and height costs more than a flat scratcher.

A tall cat tree is the classic example. The Feandrea Cat Tree is a common pick for renters because the footprint is small relative to how much climbing it gives you. Owner reviews often mention that assembly takes a while and that the top perch is the favorite spot. Expect to spend more here than on any other piece of gear.

The second cost driver is replacement. A cheap cardboard scratcher lasts weeks, not years. If you don't provide something to scratch, your cat will choose your sofa or your doorframe, and that's a security deposit problem. A more durable option like the PetFusion Ultimate Cat Scratcher costs more up front but reviewers consistently report it lasting far longer than flat cardboard pads.

Third: storage. If you can't buy a 40-pound jug of litter because you have nowhere to put it, you end up buying smaller, more expensive packages. That single constraint can add $50 to $100 a year to your litter line without you noticing.

Fourth: a second box. The general guidance is one box per cat plus one. In a tiny apartment most people run one box, which saves money but means more frequent full changes.

What keeps the cost down

Buying food in the largest bag your storage allows is the single biggest lever. A 16-pound bag costs meaningfully less per pound than a 3-pound bag of the same line. Iams ProActive Health is a widely stocked option in larger bag sizes, and buying the big bag is where the savings actually show up.

Litter in bulk works the same way. Kirkland Signature litter is a common bulk buy for exactly this reason — the per-pound cost drops sharply versus grocery-store jugs, if you have a closet corner to store it.

For gear, the cheapest efficient move is a simple upright post rather than a full tree. The Amazon Basics Cat Scratching Post is about as low as a real sisal post gets, and it takes up roughly a dinner-plate footprint. Reviewers generally say it's fine for a single cat and that the base can wobble on carpet — a common note on budget posts.

Cardboard scratchers are the other cheap lever. The Pawz Road Cat Scratcher with Catnip is inexpensive enough to replace a few times a year, and the reversible design roughly doubles its life if you flip it. The Petstages Cat Tracks is another low-cost item that gives a small-space cat something to do without eating floor space.

Interactive play matters too, and it's nearly free. A Catstages Cat Dancer costs a few dollars and is one of the most consistently recommended cheap toys among owners. A Laser Pointer Cat Toy does the same job for a cat that won't chase a wand.

Grooming is a small but real line. A Millers Forge Nail Clipper is a one-time purchase that saves you a monthly groomer trip, and clipping at home is routine for indoor cats who don't wear their claws down naturally.

The footprint question, specifically

Here's the part most cost guides skip: in a small rental, the constraint isn't dollars, it's where things go. A litter box needs roughly 2 square feet. A tall cat tree needs about 1.5 to 2 square feet of floor but several feet of height. A food and water station needs maybe 1 square foot.

That's under 6 square feet total. What actually breaks a studio apartment is the supply — the 16-pound food bag, the 35-pound litter jug, the carrier, the spare scratcher. Plan for one closet shelf or one under-bed bin, and you'll be fine.

If you're short on floor space, go vertical. A tall post beats a wide tree every time. The M-Pets Multi-Level Cat Scratching Post is built around that idea — narrow base, multiple levels. The Amazon Basics Cat Tree is the other common budget route for the same reason.

The bottom line

An indoor cat in a small apartment is a $450 to $950 a year commitment after the first year, and the space you have is not the limiting factor — your storage is. Vertical gear solves the footprint problem for well under $100. Food and litter solve themselves if you can buy in bulk.

Three concrete ways to pay less:

  1. Buy the biggest bag and the biggest litter jug your closet allows. Per-pound cost drops steeply, and it's the only lever that compounds every month.
  2. Go vertical, not wide. One tall post plus one cardboard scratcher covers scratching and climbing for a fraction of a full tree.
  3. Set aside $20–$30 a month into a vet fund. It's not a cost reduction, but it's the difference between a routine bill and a credit card emergency.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does an indoor cat cost per month?

After the first year, most single-cat households land somewhere between $40 and $80 a month. Budget setups with dry food and bulk litter sit near the bottom of that range, while wet-only feeding and premium litter push toward the top.

Does a small apartment make cat ownership more expensive?

Not directly — the gear footprint is only about 6 square feet total. The real cost risk is storage. If you can't store a 16-pound food bag or a 35-pound litter jug, you end up buying smaller packages at a higher per-pound price, which can add $50 to $100 a year.

What's the cheapest way to set up scratching for a small space?

A simple upright sisal post plus an inexpensive cardboard scratcher covers most cats.
