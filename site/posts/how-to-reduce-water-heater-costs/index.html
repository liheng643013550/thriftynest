Most households don't think about their water heater until it breaks or the utility bill spikes. But heating water is typically the second-biggest energy expense in a home, behind only heating and cooling. The good news is that you can cut that cost without buying a new water heater or taking cold showers.

Start by Finding Out What You're Actually Paying

In practice, Before you change anything, get a baseline. A typical electric water heater uses about 4,000 to 4,500 watts when the heating element kicks on, and a gas unit burns roughly 30,000 to 40,000 BTU per hour. If you run the math on your utility rate, heating water usually costs the average US family somewhere between $400 and $600 a year.

As a rule of thumb, Pull out a recent electric or gas bill and find your rate per kWh or per therm. Then check the yellow EnergyGuide sticker on your water heater if it's still there. That sticker estimates annual operating cost based on average use. Write that number down. Every fix below should move it down, and knowing the starting point tells you whether you're actually saving money or just guessing, in my experience.

Turn the Thermostat Down to 120°F

To be fair, this is the single easiest win, and most people never do it. Factory settings on many water heaters sit at 140°F. The Department of Energy recommends 120°F for most homes.

Every 10°F you drop saves roughly 3% to 5% on your water heating bill. Going from 140°F to 120°F can shave $30 to $60 a year off a typical bill, depending on your fuel type and usage.

Here's the practical part: 120°F is still hot enough for showers, dishwashing, and laundry. If you have a dishwasher without a built-in heater, some manufacturers recommend 140°F, so check your manual first. If anyone in the house has a compromised immune system, keep it at 140°F for safety and instead install a mixing valve at the tank, which blends hot and cold water so you get 140°F in the tank but 120°F at the tap, honestly.

On a gas heater, the dial is on the front of the tank. On an electric one, you'll need to remove the access panels and adjust the two thermostats with a screwdriver. Turn off the breaker first.

Insulate the Tank and Pipes

If your water heater is more than 10 years old, it probably loses heat through the tank walls all day long. That's called standby heat loss, and it's pure waste.

A water heater insulation blanket costs about $20 to $30 and can cut standby losses by 25% to 45%, saving roughly $20 to $45 a year. That pays for itself in under a year, in practice.

Two things to know before you wrap it:

  • If your heater is gas or oil, don't cover the top, the burner, or the flue. You need that airflow for safe combustion.
  • If your tank is already labeled "insulated" or has foam insulation under the jacket, skip the blanket.

Pipe insulation matters too, especially on the first 6 feet of hot and cold pipes coming out of the tank. Foam pipe sleeves run about $2 to $4 for a 6-foot section. On an electric heater, insulate the first 6 feet of the hot pipe. On a gas unit, keep insulation at least 6 inches away from the flue.

Fix Leaks and Drips Immediately

A dripping hot water faucet doesn't look like much, but it adds up. One drip per second wastes about 3,000 gallons of water a year. If that's hot water, you're paying to heat it and then watching it run down the drain, honestly.

Replace worn washers and cartridges in faucets. A basic faucet repair kit costs under $10. Showerhead leaks are just as common and just as easy to fix.

While you're at it, check for hidden leaks. Look under sinks, around the water heater's pressure relief valve, and at the base of the tank. A small leak at the T&P valve often means the valve needs replacing, which is a $15 part.

Low-flow showerheads are another quiet saver. A standard showerhead pushes 2.5 gallons per minute. A WaterSense-labeled 1.75 GPM model cuts that by 30% without a noticeable difference in pressure. For a family of four taking daily showers, that's real money, believe it or not.

Change Your Habits (Free and Immediate)

You don't need to buy anything for this section. Small behavior changes add up faster than most people expect.

  • Take shorter showers. Cutting 3 minutes off a daily 8-minute shower saves about 7 gallons of hot water per person per day.
  • Run the dishwasher only when full, and use the air-dry setting instead of heated dry.
  • Wash laundry in cold water. About 90% of the energy a washing machine uses goes to heating the water.
  • Fix the order of your morning routine so hot water isn't sitting unused in the pipes.

I started washing everything except towels and bedding in cold water two years ago. Our gas bill dropped about $8 a month, which is close to $100 a year for basically no effort.

Consider a Timer or Smart Control

If your household has predictable hours, a water heater timer can help. There's no point keeping 40 or 50 gallons of water hot at 3 a.m. if nobody's awake, believe it or not.

A basic mechanical timer for an electric water heater costs $40 to $70 and installs inline with the power. Set it to heat for two windows: one before morning showers and one before evening use. Savings range from $20 to $60 a year depending on how long your off-hours are.

Gas heaters can't use a simple timer the same way, but smart controllers can help. Devices like the Rheem EcoNet or Aquanta controller let you schedule heating and monitor usage from your phone. They cost $100 to $200, so they make more sense if your usage is high and irregular.

One caveat: if you have a tankless heater, skip the timer talk. Tankless units only heat on demand, so there's nothing sitting around losing heat, in my experience.

Know When It's Time to Replace

Sometimes the biggest savings come from replacing the unit. Water heaters last 8 to 12 years on average. If yours is past 10 and you've already done the cheap fixes, a new one may pay for itself.

Here's a rough comparison:

Type Typical Cost Annual Operating Cost Lifespan
Standard gas tank $600–$1,200 installed $400–$500 8–12 years
Standard electric tank $500–$1,000 installed $450–$600 8–12 years
Heat pump water heater $1,200–$2,500 installed $150–$300 13–15 years
Tankless gas $1,500–$3,000 installed $200–$400 20 years

Heat pump water heaters are the standout for savings. They pull heat from the surrounding air instead of generating it, which cuts energy use by 50% to 65% compared to a standard electric tank. They qualify for a federal tax credit of up to $2,000 under the Inflation Reduction Act, plus many utility rebates. That changes the math considerably.

If you go that route, brands like Rheem, A.O. Smith, and Bradford White all make models worth comparing. Check your state's rebate database first, since those incentives vary widely, believe it or not.

The Bottom Line

You don't have to pick just one of these. Start with the free stuff: turn the thermostat to 120°F and fix any drips. Then spend $30 on a tank blanket and pipe insulation. Together, those three steps can knock $75 to $150 a year off a typical bill with maybe an hour of work.

If your water heater is over 10 years old, run the numbers on a heat pump model before it fails on you. Replacing a water heater in an emergency costs more than planning ahead, and the tax credits won't last forever.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much can I really save by lowering my water heater temperature?

Dropping from 140°F to 120°F saves about 3% to 5% for every 10°F reduction, which typically works out to $30 to $60 a year. It's the fastest fix and costs nothing, in practice.

Is a tankless water heater worth it for saving money?

It depends on your usage. Tankless units cost more upfront ($1,500 to $3,000 installed), last longer, and eliminate standby heat loss. If your family uses a lot of hot water and you plan to stay in your home for years, the savings can add up. If you use very little hot water, a heat pump tank is often the better buy.

How often should I flush my water heater?

Once a year for most homes, or every six months if you have hard water. Sediment builds up on the bottom of the tank and forces the burner or element to work harder, which raises your bill and shortens the tank's life. It takes about 30 minutes with a garden hose.
