For two people setting up a first apartment, the meal prep question that decides everything is simple: how much does it actually cost to run this thing every month? A $30 appliance that pulls 1,000 watts for an hour a day can quietly add more to your electric bill than the gadget itself cost. This guide focuses on tools that are cheap to buy and cheap to live with, sized for one person so you're not cooking for four or cleaning a giant pot every night.

What actually matters

  • Running cost per month, not sticker price. Most small kitchen appliances pull somewhere between 300 and 1,500 watts. At a typical US rate of roughly 12 to 17 cents per kilowatt-hour, a 700-watt appliance used 30 minutes a day costs somewhere around $1.50 to $3 a month. A 1,500-watt one used an hour a day can run $6 to $8. Check the wattage on the label before you fall in love with the shape.
  • Cooking time as an energy multiplier. A tool that finishes rice in 20 minutes beats one that takes 45, even at the same wattage, because you're paying for minutes, not for the appliance.
  • Capacity matched to one person. A 3-quart pot for one person is generous. A 6-quart is a batch-cooking tool, not a nightly one. Oversized means longer heat-up times and more energy wasted heating empty space.
  • Counter and cabinet footprint. In a first apartment, the tool you can leave out gets used. The one buried behind the plates does not.
  • Cleanup effort. Nonstick and dishwasher-safe parts get used more often than anything that needs scrubbing. Owner reviews consistently flag cleanup as the reason a tool gets abandoned.
  • Whether it replaces another appliance. A rice cooker that also steams vegetables replaces a pot and a steamer basket. Fewer appliances plugged in means a lower baseline draw.

The bottom line

For a first apartment, buy in this order. Start with the scale — it costs about as much as lunch and it stops you cooking more than you'll eat. Add a rice cooker sized for two; the Aroma Housewares 6-Cup is the safe default, and the Dash Mini Rice Cooker is the pick if your counter is genuinely tiny. Then add one cooking appliance based on how you actually eat: the Dash Compact Air Fryer if you reheat and crisp, the Black+Decker Crisp 'N Bake if you also want toast, or the Crock-Pot Artisan 5-Quart Enameled Cast Iron Dutch Oven if you batch cook on weekends. The immersion blender and the digital rice cooker are upgrades you add later, once you know which one you'll actually use.

On running cost, the pattern is consistent: low-wattage tools used for short bursts are the cheapest to live with, and batch cooking is what makes a higher-wattage appliance worth it. You're not saving money by buying the cheapest box — you're saving it by buying the tool that matches how often you cook.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to run a small kitchen appliance each month?

It depends on wattage and minutes used, but most small appliances in this list land somewhere around $1.50 to $3 a month at typical use, based on a rate of roughly 12 to 17 cents per kilowatt-hour. Higher-wattage tools like the Black+Decker Crisp 'N Bake cost more per session than the Dash Mini Rice Cooker.

Which of these is cheapest to run?

The Amazon Basics Digital Kitchen Scale runs on batteries and draws essentially nothing, and the Cuisinart Smart Stick CSB-75 runs for seconds at a time. Among the cooking appliances, the Dash Mini Rice Cooker and Aroma Housewares 6-Cup are the lowest-wattage options.

Do I need a full-size oven if I have an air fryer?

No, not for two people. The Dash Compact Air Fryer and Black+Decker Crisp 'N Bake both handle reheating, crisping, and small-batch roasting, usually for less energy than heating a full oven. You'll still want the oven for large trays, whole birds, or big batches.
