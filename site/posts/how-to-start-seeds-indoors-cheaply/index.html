How to Start Seeds Indoors Cheaply (No Fancy Gear Needed)

Starting seeds indoors sounds like a hobby for people with grow lights, heat mats, and a spare bedroom. It isn't. I've started tomatoes, peppers, basil, and marigolds on a $12 setup that lived on top of my refrigerator for six weeks. Here's how to do it without buying things you'll use once and store forever.

Figure Out What You Actually Need

In most cases, Seed starting comes down to five things: a container, a growing medium, water, light, and warmth. That's it. Everything sold at garden centers is a variation on those five, and most of it is optional.

What you can skip:

  • Heat mats (a warm spot in your house does the same job)
  • Grow light systems (a $20 shop light works)
  • Peat pellets and starter kits (a yogurt cup works)
  • Seed starting fertilizer (seeds carry their own food)

What's worth spending on:

  • Good seed starting mix (not potting soil)
  • A cheap light source if you don't have a bright south window
  • Seeds from a reputable brand

That said, My total cost last spring for 72 seedlings: about $22, including seeds. That's roughly 30 cents per plant. A single tomato start at the nursery runs $4 to $6, honestly.

Pick Your Containers (Free Is Fine)

From real-world use, Almost anything holds soil and drains. I've used paper egg cartons, toilet paper tubes cut in half, solo cups with a hole punched in the bottom, and takeout containers. All of them worked.

A few things to know:

  • Egg cartons dry out fast and the cells are tiny. Fine for herbs, cramped for tomatoes.
  • Toilet paper tubes work great for anything you'll transplant within 4 weeks. Plant the whole tube.
  • Solo cups (16 oz) are my favorite. They're about 4 cents each in a 100-pack, and the depth handles tomatoes and peppers well.
  • Newspaper pots made with a wooden pot maker are free if you already get a paper.

Whatever you use, punch drainage holes. Soggy roots kill more seedlings than cold does.

If you want a middle ground between free junk and a full kit, a basic seed starting tray with humidity dome runs about $10 and lasts several seasons. I use one for peppers, which germinate slowly and benefit from the dome.

Mix Your Own Seed Starting Medium

Bagged seed starting mix costs $5 to $8 for 8 quarts. You can make something close for less, in my experience.

My mix:

  • 1 part peat moss or coco coir
  • 1 part vermiculite
  • 1 part finished compost, sifted

That's it. The peat holds moisture, the vermiculite keeps it light, and the compost adds just enough nutrients without burning tender roots. A bale of peat moss ($15) plus a bag of vermiculite ($12) makes enough mix for years of seed starting. Split the cost with a neighbor if you don't want the storage.

Don't use garden soil straight from outside. It compacts in small containers and often carries weed seeds and damping-off fungus.

Moisten the mix before you fill your containers. Aim for the consistency of a wrung-out sponge. Dry mix repels water and you'll spend 20 minutes chasing it around the cup, honestly.

Sow at the Right Depth and Time

The two most common mistakes are planting too deep and starting too early.

Depth: A good rule is to plant seeds two to three times as deep as the seed is wide. Most seeds are tiny, so that means barely covered. I press them into the surface and dust a thin layer of mix over them. Tomatoes and basil need light to germinate, so they get almost nothing on top.

Timing: Count backward from your last frost date. Here in zone 6b, that's around May 10. So:

Crop Weeks before last frost Start indoors
Peppers 8 to 10 Late February
Tomatoes 6 to 8 Mid-March
Basil 4 to 6 Early April
Broccoli 4 to 6 Early April
Zinnias 4 to 6 Early April

Start too early and you'll have leggy, root-bound plants by April that never recover. I learned this the hard way with peppers one year. Waiting two extra weeks would have given me better plants.

Label everything. Every seedling looks the same for the first ten days, and you will forget which cup is which, in my experience.

Give Them Light Without Buying a Grow Light

A south-facing window works if it gets 6 or more hours of direct sun. Most windows don't, especially in late winter.

The cheap fix: a 4-foot LED shop light. Mine was $19 at a hardware store and pulls about 40 watts. Hang it 2 to 3 inches above the seedlings and raise it as they grow. Run it 14 to 16 hours a day on a $8 mechanical timer so you don't have to remember.

Two things matter more than wattage:

  • Distance. Seedlings stretch toward light. If the light is 12 inches away, you get tall, thin, floppy plants. Keep it close.
  • Duration. 14 to 16 hours is plenty. Plants need a dark period to grow properly.

A cheap reflector helps too. I lined the sides of my shelf with aluminum foil taped to cardboard. It bounces light back onto the outer seedlings and costs nothing, in practice.

Water and Warmth on a Budget

Bottom watering is the easiest way to avoid damping-off, that fuzzy fungus that flattens seedlings overnight. Set your containers in a tray, pour water into the tray, and let them wick it up for 15 to 20 minutes. Dump whatever's left.

Check moisture by weight. A dry solo cup feels light. A wet one feels like a small brick. After a week you'll know without thinking about it.

For warmth, skip the heat mat and use what you have:

  • Top of the refrigerator (classic for a reason)
  • Near a water heater
  • On top of a cable box or router
  • A shelf above a radiator

Peppers and tomatoes germinate fastest at 75 to 80°F. Once they sprout, move them somewhere cooler, around 65°F. Cooler air after germination keeps stems short and sturdy.

Thin, Feed, and Harden Off

Once seedlings have their first set of true leaves, thin them. Snip the weaker one at soil level with scissors. Don't pull, or you'll disturb the roots of the one you're keeping. One plant per cup.

Feed them lightly after 3 to 4 weeks, once the seed's stored food runs out. A diluted liquid fertilizer at quarter strength every two weeks is plenty. I use a basic fish emulsion, about $10 a bottle, and it lasts two seasons, believe it or not.

About a week before planting outside, start hardening off. Set plants outside in shade for an hour on day one. Add an hour and a bit of sun each day. By day seven they can handle full sun and a full day outdoors. Skip this and a sunny afternoon will scorch leaves that spent six weeks indoors.

The bottom line

You can start a full tray of seedlings for under $25 and about 15 minutes of actual work per week. Use free containers, mix your own medium, hang a cheap shop light close to the plants, and keep them warm until they sprout. The gear matters far less than timing, light distance, and not overwatering. Start with three or four crops your first year and see what works in your house.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I really need a heat mat to start seeds indoors?

No. Most seeds germinate fine at room temperature, just a few days slower. Peppers and tomatoes benefit from extra warmth, but the top of your fridge or a spot near a water heater does the same thing for free, in my experience.

How much does it cost to start seeds indoors?

For a 72-cell tray, expect $20 to $30 total: seeds, seed starting mix, and a cheap light if you need one. Containers can be free. That works out to roughly 30 cents per plant, compared to $4 or more for a nursery start.

Why are my seedlings tall and thin?

They aren't getting enough light. Move your light source to 2 to 3 inches above the tops of the plants, run it 14 to 16 hours a day, and keep the room a bit cooler. Leggy seedlings rarely recover fully, so fix it as soon as you notice.
