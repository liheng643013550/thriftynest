The Biggest Mistake First-Time Buyers Make

The single biggest mistake is assuming a "$20 circular saw" means a complete, usable saw. In reality, almost nothing at that price is a full-size circular saw — it is usually a mini or compact saw, or a bare tool with no blade, and sometimes both. Buyers see a low price, order it, then discover they still need a blade, a clamp, and a stable surface to cut on. The real cost creeps past $40 fast.

If you rent a small apartment, that math matters twice. A saw you rarely use still has to be stored, and the dust it throws has to be cleaned off your floor, counter, and baseboards every single time. So before you buy anything, decide whether you actually need a saw at all — or whether a hand tool, a rental, or a friend's saw does the job for less money and less cleanup.

What Actually Matters

  • Blade size and depth of cut. Budget saws are usually 3-1/8 inch to 4-1/2 inch. That limits you to roughly 1 inch of cut depth in most materials, which is fine for plywood, trim, and shelving but not for 2x4 framing.
  • Wattage and amp rating. Compact saws typically run 4 to 6 amps. Lower amperage means slower cutting and more binding in thicker stock, which in turn means more kickback risk.
  • Blade included or not. Many cheap listings ship with no blade. A replacement 4-1/2 inch blade usually adds $8 to $15, which changes the whole value calculation.
  • Dust and cleanup. Saws without a dust port or bag spread fine sawdust across a room. In a studio apartment, that is 10 to 20 minutes of wiping down surfaces and vacuuming after even a short cut.
  • Storage footprint. A compact saw is roughly the size of a large book and fits on a closet shelf. A full-size 7-1/4 inch saw does not.
  • Cord vs. battery. Corded compact saws are cheaper and lighter for occasional use. Battery saws at this price are almost always bare tools without a charger.

A Note on What $20 Actually Buys

At the time of writing, most listings marketed as budget circular saws under $20 are compact models from lesser-known brands, and owner reviews often mention weak motors, wobbly base plates, and blades that dull within a few cuts. Reviewers frequently report that the saw works for one small project and then struggles. That is a real pattern, not a one-off.

Because of that, this guide does not name a $20 circular saw. There is no model in the approved product list that fits the category honestly, and inventing one would waste your money. Instead, here are the tools that genuinely help a renter with a small space get cutting and cleanup done — plus the cleanup gear that makes the weekly time cost lower.

What makes it a good pick: This is a drill and driver bit set, not a saw, but it is the honest starting point for most small-space renters. The Bottom Line

Do not buy a $20 circular saw. At that price you are almost always getting a compact saw with no blade, a weak motor, and a base plate that owner reviews describe as flimsy — and you still have to clean up the sawdust every time you use it. If your real projects are shelves, brackets, and flat-pack furniture, spend the money on a bit set, a level, and a hammer instead. Then add a light vacuum and a spin mop so cleanup takes minutes, not an hour, in a small space. That combination costs less than a decent saw and gets used far more often.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a circular saw under $20 worth buying?

Usually not. Models in that range are typically compact saws from lesser-known brands, often sold without a blade, and owner reviews frequently mention weak motors and wobbly base plates. Budget for a blade and clamps too, and the real cost climbs well past $20.

What should a renter buy instead of a cheap circular saw?

For shelf, bracket, and furniture projects, a bit set like the Bosch Daredevil Set, a Craftsman 9-Inch Torpedo Level, and a hammer such as the Milwaukee 16 oz Framing or Vaughan 16 oz Curved Claw cover most jobs. For wall hanging without a drill, the OOK Brand 50 lb. Picture Hanging Kit takes about two minutes to install.

How much time does cleanup add after a small project?

Plan on roughly 3 to 5 minutes with a light vacuum like the Bissell Featherweight, plus about 5 to 10 minutes with a spin mop like the O-Cedar EasyWring Spin Mop if you have hard floors. Skipping cleanup is not really an option in a small space, since fine dust spreads across counters and baseboards quickly.
