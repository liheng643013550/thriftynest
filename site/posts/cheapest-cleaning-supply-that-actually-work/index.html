Eight Cheapest Cleaning Supplies That Actually Work

Cheap cleaning gear fails in one of two ways: it breaks, or it doesn't clean. Sometimes it does both, and you end up buying the same mop or spray bottle three times in a year. The trick is figuring out which cheap products are genuinely good and which ones just look like a bargain on the shelf.

The reality check up front: the cheapest supplies that actually work are almost never the cheapest thing on the shelf. They're the ones where the cost per year is low because you buy once, or because refills are cheap, or because the thing survives being wrung out, dropped, and used twice a week. A $4 mop that dies in three months costs more per year than a $25 mop that lasts three years. That's the math this list is built on.

If this helped, you'll also want to read Why Your Windows Are Still Streaky After Cleaning and How to Keep a Bathroom Clean With Less Effort Each Week.

Scroll past anything with a proprietary refill cartridge you can't refill yourself, and be skeptical of anything with a battery you can't replace. Everything below is judged on total cost over the first year of ownership — purchase price plus whatever you have to spend to keep it running.

Microfiber cloths: the whole year for under $15

This is the single best dollar-per-use cleaning purchase in a house. A pack of microfiber cloths replaces paper towels, glass cleaner pads, and dust rags, and each cloth survives hundreds of wash cycles if you don't put it in the dryer on high heat. A 12-pack of HOMEXCEL 12-Pack typically runs well under $15, which works out to roughly a dollar or so per cloth. Even if you lose half of them behind the couch, you're still at a few cents per cleaning session for the year.

The catch is that cheap microfiber sheds and stops grabbing after a while. Buy a heavier-weight pack for glass and a lighter one for dusting. The Amazon Basics Microfiber Cloths are the budget benchmark most people compare against, and Zwipes 24-Pack gets you a bigger stash for roughly the same money per cloth. Owner reviews consistently mention that the cheap ones last fine if you skip fabric softener.

A spin mop: no cartridges, no refill tax

The hidden cost of most mops isn't the mop — it's the $8 replacement pads you buy every six weeks. A spin mop sidesteps that entirely because you wring it by foot and reuse the same head for months. The O-Cedar EasyWring Spin Mop is the model most people have seen in stores, and the head is machine-washable. The Vileda Turbo Microfiber Spin Mop is the same idea with a different bucket design.

Over a year, a spin mop typically costs you the purchase price plus maybe one or two replacement heads at a few dollars each. Compare that to a disposable-pad sweeper, where pads usually run $6–$10 per pack and a heavy user burns through a pack a month. That's the difference between roughly $30 and roughly $100 in year one.

Spray mops: cheap refills beat cheap hardware

If you want the convenience of a spray mop, buy one that takes a refillable bottle instead of proprietary cartridges. The Rubbermaid Reveal Spray Mop accepts any liquid you mix yourself, which means your floor cleaner costs pennies per fill instead of a few dollars per cartridge. The Bona Hardwood Floor Spray Mop is the better pick for wood specifically, and Bona's concentrate is widely available, so you're not locked into one seller.

Here's the year-one math: a spray mop plus a bottle of concentrate usually lands under $50 total, and the concentrate lasts most of the year. Pad-based sweepers with disposable cloths can hit $80–$120 in the same window. The mop is the cheaper product. The refills are what make it that way.

Scrub sponges that don't smell like a pond in a week

The classic cellulose sponge is the cheapest sponge you can buy and also the fastest to turn into a biohazard. A better move is a scrub sponge with an open-cell design that drains and dries, plus a stiff abrasive side that doesn't shred. Scrub Daddy Microfiber Cloths pair with that kind of sponge setup nicely for the rinse-and-wipe stage. Mr. Siga 12-Pack and Scotch-Brite Large Microfiber Cloths are the other budget staples people stock up on.

A 12-pack of decent sponges usually costs less than $15 and lasts a typical household most of a year if you rotate them and let them dry between uses.

Squeegees and scrapers: two dollars that save twenty

A Squeegee costs a few dollars and eliminates the need for a shower spray if you use it after every shower. That's the whole trick: 30 seconds of work, no chemical buildup, no scrubbing later. The plastic scraper does the same job for stuck-on food on counters and stovetops — it replaces a lot of scrubbing pads and a lot of elbow grease.

Neither item wears out in a year under normal use. Total year-one cost: under $10 for both.

A grout brush set: the tool most people never buy

Grout lines get ignored until they're black, and then people buy a $20 bottle of specialty cleaner they use twice. A Grout Brush Set costs a few dollars and works with whatever cleaner you already own. Stiff nylon bristles plus hot water handle most of it. Owner reviews often mention these brushes holding up better than expected on tile and around faucets.

A dusting tool with a long handle

Baseboards, ceiling fans, and the tops of cabinets are where dust lives and where cheap dusters fail — they're too short, so you climb, or you skip it. A Microfiber Extendable Duster usually runs under $15 and the microfiber head is washable, so there's no refill to buy. That's the pattern again: cheap hardware, zero consumables.

Glass cloths that replace paper towels

If you're still buying paper towels for mirrors and windows, that's typically $20–$40 a year for something a cloth does better. E-Cloth General Purpose Pack is the well-known version of this — the cloths are designed to clean glass with just water, which also means you stop buying glass cleaner. The pack typically costs around $15–$20 and lasts well past a year with normal washing.

The bottom line

The cheapest cleaning supplies that actually work are the ones with no refill tax and no battery. Cloths, brushes, squeegees, and a spin mop all cost you once and then keep working, which is why they beat out the flashier options on a year-one basis. If you're starting from nothing, spend your first $60 on microfiber cloths, a spin mop, and a squeegee. That covers most of a house, and none of it needs a subscription.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should I expect to spend in the first year?

For the cloth-and-mop basics on this list, typically under $100 total. The spin mops and spray mops are the bigger line items, usually $30–$50 each, while cloths, squeegees, and scrapers are each under $20 and often under $15.

Why is a spin mop cheaper than a disposable-pad sweeper?

Because there are no pads to rebuy. A spin mop's head is machine-washable, so your year-one cost is the mop plus maybe one spare head. A sweeper's disposable pads usually run $6–$10 per pack, and a heavy user goes through a pack a month.

Do cheap microfiber cloths actually last?

Yes, generally, if you wash them and skip fabric softener. A 12-pack like the HOMEXCEL 12-Pack or Amazon Basics Microfiber Cloths typically costs under $15 and survives hundreds of wash cycles. The lighter-weight ones wear out faster than the heavier ones, so buy a heavier pack for glass.
