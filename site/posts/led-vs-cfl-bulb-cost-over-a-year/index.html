The Dorm Light Bulb Math Nobody Does Until the Bill Comes

Your dorm room has one overhead fixture, a desk lamp, and a string of lights you're not supposed to have. The bulb in the desk lamp burned out, and you're standing in the hardware aisle looking at a $2 CFL and a $5 LED, wondering why anyone would pay more than double for a light bulb. Here's the thing: the sticker price is the smallest number in this decision. The real question is what each bulb costs you across a full year of dorm life — and for most students, the answer isn't close.

CFLs suit someone who needs cheap upfront light in a fixture that rarely runs. LEDs suit anyone paying their own electric bill, anyone who leaves a lamp on while studying until 2 a.m., and anyone who doesn't want to think about bulbs again for years.

Quick verdict

  • Cheapest first year in a dorm where the light runs 4+ hours a night: LED. Lower wattage plus a lifespan measured in years, not months, wins the year-one math even at a higher purchase price.
  • Lowest possible upfront cost when you're broke in September: CFL. If $3 today matters more than $9 across the year, a CFL gets you light now.
  • Fixture that gets bumped, knocked over, or sits in a cold room: LED. CFLs are glass, they don't love being knocked around, and they dim and start slowly in cold rooms — a real issue in winter if your window isn't sealed.

Cost to own over the first year

This is where the decision actually gets made. A bulb that runs four hours a night burns about 1,460 hours a year. At the national average electricity rate — which varies by state, but typically lands somewhere around 15–17 cents per kWh — the math looks like this.

An LED drawing 9W for 1,460 hours uses roughly 13 kWh, or about $2 of electricity for the year. A CFL drawing 14W for the same hours uses about 20 kWh, or roughly $3.20. The gap is real but modest — call it a dollar and change per bulb per year.

The bigger number is replacement. Owner reviews of CFLs frequently mention bulbs dying within a year or two in fixtures that run nightly, which lines up with their rated 6,000–10,000 hours. An LED rated at 15,000–25,000 hours typically outlasts your entire degree. So over one year, a CFL might cost you $3 upfront plus $3.20 in electricity plus a replacement bulb; an LED costs $5 upfront plus $2 in electricity and stays put.

If you want to actually see your own numbers instead of trusting a range, a plug-in meter like the P3 P4460 Kill A Watt EZ lets you measure whatever lamp or device you plug into it. Students who share a room with a mini-fridge and a space heater often find the bulbs are the least of their problems — the Dreo Space Heater and similar units draw far more than any bulb ever will.

Verdict: LED wins the year on total cost. CFL wins only if the purchase price is the only number you can afford today.

Space and fixture fit

Dorm fixtures are cramped. Desk lamps often have shallow shades, and a CFL's spiral or tube shape can poke out or cast light sideways instead of down. LEDs run cooler, which matters in a small lamp housing with poor ventilation — a CFL in an enclosed shade traps heat and shortens its own life.

For a standard desk lamp, an LED in the 8–12W range gives you the brightness of an old 60W incandescent without the heat. If your room came with a smart plug setup, pairing a lamp with a Kasa Smart Plug HS103 or a Wyze Plug lets you schedule lights off at midnight automatically — which saves more electricity than the bulb choice itself.

Verdict: LED, mostly because it's smaller and cooler in tight shades.

Cleaning and breakage

Nobody enjoys cleaning up a shattered bulb. CFLs contain a small amount of mercury, and if one breaks, the EPA recommends ventilating the room and avoiding a vacuum. That's a genuine hassle in a 150-square-foot dorm room with a roommate.

LEDs are plastic and aluminum. If you knock your lamp off the desk — and you will — an LED usually survives. When it doesn't, cleanup is a broom and a minute. Keep a pack of Amazon Basics Microfiber Cloths around for dusting lampshades; dust on a shade cuts light output noticeably and makes you want to crank the brightness.

Verdict: LED, clearly.

Speed: instant-on matters more than you think

A CFL in a cold room takes 30 seconds to two minutes to reach full brightness, and it starts dim and slightly greenish. In a dorm with a drafty window, that's every winter morning. Turning on a lamp to find a dim orange glow while you're trying to find your shoes is genuinely irritating.

LEDs hit full brightness instantly at any temperature. There's no warm-up, no flicker at startup, and no color shift.

If your room runs cold, sealing the window is a separate cheap win — a 3M Indoor Shrink Film Kit or the Window Insulation Kit by Frost King costs less than a pizza and keeps the cold from undoing your heating.

Verdict: LED, and it isn't close.

Noise and flicker

Some CFLs hum. It's a faint buzz from the ballast, and in a quiet room at night it's exactly the kind of thing that keeps you awake. Owner reviews mention buzzing most often when CFLs are on dimmers or in older fixtures.

LEDs are silent, but cheap ones can flicker on incompatible dimmers. If your lamp has a dimmer, check that the bulb is marked dimmable. For a plain on/off lamp, this is a non-issue.

Verdict: LED for silence; check dimmer compatibility either way.

Versatility across your whole setup

A CFL is basically a light bulb. An LED is a light bulb that also works in a clip lamp, a desk lamp, a bathroom vanity, and a reading light without overheating any of them. If you're moving between dorm rooms, sublets, and a first apartment, LEDs travel better and don't need replacing every move.

While you're setting up, a Honeywell TurboForce or a Genesis Clip Fan handles air movement in a room with one window, and a Honey-Can-Do Foldable Drying Rack saves you the $2-per-load dryer fee that quietly eats more money than lighting ever will.

Verdict: LED for flexibility across fixtures.

When to choose each one

Choose a CFL if you need light today for the absolute lowest upfront cost, the fixture runs only occasionally, and you're not sensitive to warm-up time or a faint hum.

Choose an LED if the lamp runs most evenings, your room gets cold, you're paying your own electric bill, or you just don't want to buy the same bulb twice. For a dorm, that's almost everyone.

The bottom line

Across one year, a CFL saves you a couple of dollars at checkout and costs you more in electricity, replacement, and annoyance. An LED costs a few dollars more upfront and then quietly disappears from your budget for years. If you're buying one bulb for a desk lamp you use nightly, buy the LED. If you're buying a four-pack for fixtures you rarely touch, a CFL is a defensible cheap call — just know you'll be back in that aisle sooner than you think.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is LED actually cheaper than CFL over one year?

Yes, for a bulb used nightly. An LED drawing around 9W costs roughly $2 in electricity over 1,460 hours, while a 14W CFL costs about $3.20 — plus the CFL is more likely to need replacing within the year, since its rated lifespan is typically 6,000–10,000 hours versus 15,000–25,000 for an LED.

Do CFL bulbs really take time to warm up?

Yes. CFLs typically take 30 seconds to two minutes to reach full brightness, and they start dim and slightly greenish in cold rooms. LEDs reach full brightness instantly at any temperature, which is why they're the better pick for a cold dorm room in winter.

Which bulb should a college student buy for a dorm desk lamp?

An LED in the 8–12W range. It runs cooler in a cramped shade, survives being knocked off a desk better than a glass CFL, and costs less to own across a full year of nightly use. A CFL only makes sense if the lowest possible purchase price is your deciding factor.
