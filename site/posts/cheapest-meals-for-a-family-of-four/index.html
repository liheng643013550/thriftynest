The 10 Cheapest Meals for a Family of Four (Under $10 Each)

We’ve all been there: it’s 4:30 PM, the kids are hungry, and you have about $12 in your checking account until payday. Feeding four people on a tight budget feels like a math problem you didn’t sign up for. But after years of feeding my own family of four on a shoestring, I can tell you that it’s totally doable. You just need a game plan and a list of reliable, cheap meals that don’t taste like punishment.

As a rule of thumb, I’m not talking about eating plain rice and beans every single night (though that’s a solid backup). I’m talking about real, filling dinners—pasta, tacos, soup, and casseroles—that cost less than $10 for the whole pot. Here are the ten cheapest meals I actually make in my own kitchen, plus the exact strategies I use to keep the grocery bill low.

My Non-Negotiable Rules for Cheap Family Dinners

Worth noting, Before we get to the list, let’s set the ground rules. These aren't fancy tips from a cooking show; they are the habits that keep my weekly total under $100, believe it or not.

If this helped, you'll also want to read How to Meal Prep on a Budget: A Beginner's 7-Day Plan and Best Budget Cookware Sets in 2026: What's Worth Buying.

First, I almost always buy store-brand staples. The generic penne pasta or canned tomatoes at my local Kroger are often 50 cents cheaper than the name brand, and nobody at my dinner table can tell the difference. I also keep a core pantry stocked at all times: pasta, rice, canned beans, diced tomatoes, chicken broth, and flour. When these items go on sale for under a dollar, I buy two or three extras. This "stockpile" is the secret to never paying full price for a meal.

To be fair, Second, I plan my menu around what’s already in the fridge and pantry. I check my cabinets on Sunday night and build the week's meals around those ingredients. This stops me from buying a $4 jar of a specific spice I’ll use once. If the recipe calls for fresh herbs and I don't have them, I skip it. Dried herbs work just fine.

Finally, I embrace "stretch" proteins. A single pound of ground beef or a couple of chicken breasts can feed six people if you mix them with cheaper fillers like rice, beans, potatoes, or pasta. This isn't about being cheap; it's about being smart with expensive ingredients, in practice.

The Ultimate List: Meals That Cost Under $10

These prices are based on my current grocery runs in the Midwest. Obviously, prices fluctuate by region, but these are all realistic estimates for a family of four. I calculated the cost of ingredients I actually use, not the cost of the whole jar of spice.

1. Classic Spaghetti with Lentil Marinara

This is my go-to "I have nothing" dinner. Instead of buying ground beef ($5+ a pound), I use brown lentils, which cost about $1.50 per bag and cook up in 20 minutes. The texture is surprisingly meaty, and the kids don't even notice.

The Cost:

  • 1 lb spaghetti: $1.00
  • 1 cup dry brown lentils: $0.75
  • 2 cans (15 oz) crushed tomatoes: $1.50
  • 1 onion + 2 cloves garlic: $0.80
  • Dried oregano and basil (pantry cost): $0.10
  • Total: ~$4.15

I sauté the onion and garlic in olive oil, add the lentils and canned tomatoes, then add one can of water. I let it simmer for about 25 minutes until the lentils are soft. While that cooks, I boil the pasta. It hits the table in 30 minutes flat. Serve with a sprinkle of grated parmesan if you have it, but it’s fine without, believe it or not.

2. Hearty Chicken and Rice Casserole

Buying a whole chicken is the single best way to save money on meat. I roast a 5-pound bird on Monday for dinner, then pick the leftovers for this casserole on Wednesday. You can also buy a rotisserie chicken from Costco for $4.99 and use half of it for this dish.

The Cost:

  • 2 cups cooked, shredded chicken: $2.50 (if using rotisserie)
  • 1 cup long-grain white rice: $0.50
  • 1 can cream of chicken soup: $1.00
  • 1 cup frozen mixed vegetables: $0.75
  • 2 cups chicken broth (from bouillon): $0.30
  • Total: ~$5.05

Mix the rice, soup, broth, veggies, and chicken in a greased 9x13 baking dish. Cover with foil and bake at 375°F for 45 minutes, then uncover for the last 10 minutes. It comes out creamy and comforting. This is one of those meals where you can add a can of drained black beans to stretch it even further if you have guests.

3. Cheesy Black Bean Quesadillas

If you have a package of tortillas and a bag of shredded cheese, you are literally 10 minutes away from dinner. This is my emergency meal for nights when the schedule is chaos. I pair these with a simple side of corn (canned or frozen) to round it out, believe it or not.

The Cost:

  • 10 large flour tortillas: $1.50
  • 2 cans black beans, drained: $1.50
  • 2 cups shredded cheddar or Mexican blend: $2.50
  • 1 jar salsa (for dipping): $1.50
  • Total: ~$7.00

I mash the beans slightly with a fork and spread them on half of each tortilla. I top with cheese, fold, and cook in a dry skillet over medium heat for 2-3 minutes per side until golden and crispy. Cut into triangles and serve with salsa. Nobody complains about this meal. Ever.

4. Budget-Friendly Taco Night (With a Twist)

Taco night is a classic, but buying the "taco kit" is a rip-off. I buy the cheapest corn or flour tortillas, a can of refried beans, and make my own taco seasoning from pantry spices (chili powder, cumin, garlic powder, onion powder, and salt).

The Cost:

  • 1 lb ground turkey or beef (85/15): $3.50
  • 1 packet or homemade taco seasoning: $0.25
  • 12 small tortillas: $1.00
  • 1 can refried beans: $0.90
  • Lettuce, tomato, and shredded cheese (small amounts): $2.00
  • Total: ~$7.65

To make the meat go further, I cook the ground meat and then stir in a can of rinsed black beans or pinto beans. This adds bulk and fiber without adding much cost. We set up a little assembly line, and everyone builds their own. It’s interactive and cheap, in my experience.

5. Creamy Tuna Noodle Casserole

Canned tuna is a budget hero. While it's not glamorous, a classic tuna casserole is pure nostalgia and comfort. It costs pennies per serving and uses mostly pantry staples.

The Cost:

  • 12 oz egg noodles: $1.25
  • 2 cans tuna (5 oz each), drained: $1.80
  • 1 can cream of mushroom soup: $1.00
  • 1 cup frozen peas: $0.75
  • 1/2 cup milk: $0.15
  • 1 cup crushed potato chips or breadcrumbs: $0.50
  • Total: ~$5.45

Boil the noodles, then mix in the soup, milk, tuna, and peas. Pour into a baking dish, top with crushed chips, and bake at 375°F for 20 minutes until bubbly. It’s rich, salty, and filling. My kids actually ask for this one.

6. Loaded Baked Potatoes with Broccoli and Cheese

A giant potato is one of the cheapest things you can buy. I often find 5-pound bags on sale for $2.99. This is a great meatless meal that still feels indulgent because of the melted cheese, believe it or not.

The Cost:

  • 4 large russet baking potatoes: $1.50
  • 1 large head of broccoli (or 1 bag frozen): $1.50
  • 1.5 cups shredded cheddar: $1.80
  • 1/2 cup sour cream (optional): $0.50
  • Butter, salt, pepper: $0.20
  • Total: ~$5.50

I scrub the potatoes, poke them with a fork, and microwave them for about 8-10 minutes (turning halfway) until they are soft. Alternatively, you can bake them in the oven at 425°F for an hour. I steam the broccoli in the microwave with a little water. Then, I split the potatoes, fluff the insides, pile on the broccoli, and shower them with cheese. Pop them back in the microwave for 30 seconds to melt the cheese. This is a complete, nutritious meal.

7. One-Pot Sausage and Potato Skillet

Smoked sausage (like Hillshire Farm or the store brand) is often on sale for under $3. It’s highly flavorful, so a little goes a long way. This is a one-pan dinner that makes cleanup a breeze.

The Cost:

  • 1 lb smoked sausage, sliced: $2.50
  • 4-5 medium potatoes, diced: $1.00
  • 1 green bell pepper, chopped: $0.80
  • 1 large onion, chopped: $0.60
  • 2 tbsp cooking oil: $0.10
  • 1 tsp smoked paprika: $0.10
  • Total: ~$5.10

I start by boiling the diced potatoes in salted water for about 7 minutes to soften them. Then I drain them and add them to a hot skillet with the oil, sausage, onion, and bell pepper. I cook it all over medium-high heat, stirring occasionally, until the potatoes are golden and the sausage is browned at the edges. It takes about 15 minutes total and fills the house with an amazing smell, in my experience.

8. Vegetable Fried Rice (A Great Fridge Cleaner)

If you have leftover rice from a previous meal, this is the cheapest meal on the planet. If not, a fresh batch of rice costs pennies. This is my Friday night special because it uses up all the sad vegetables in the crisper drawer.

The Cost:

  • 3 cups cooked white rice (day-old is best): $0.40
  • 2 eggs, beaten: $0.40
  • 1 cup frozen peas and carrots: $0.75
  • 1/2 onion, diced: $0.30
  • 3 tbsp soy sauce: $0.30
  • 1 tbsp sesame oil (or vegetable oil): $0.15
  • Total: ~$2.30

I scramble the eggs in a hot wok or large skillet, remove them, and then stir-fry the onions and frozen veggies. I add the rice, breaking up clumps, and then add the soy sauce and sesame oil. Finally, I toss the egg back in. If I have leftover rotisserie chicken or pork, I chop it up and throw it in. This is the definition of a budget meal.

9. Big Pot of Chili with Cornbread

Chili is the king of budget meals. It’s forgiving, freezes well, and gets better the next day. I use a mix of ground beef and extra beans to keep the cost down, in my experience.

The Cost:

  • 1 lb ground beef: $3.50
  • 2 cans kidney beans: $1.50
  • 1 can diced tomatoes: $0.75
  • 1 can tomato sauce: $0.60
  • 1 onion and 1 green pepper: $1.20
  • Chili powder and cumin: $0.20
  • Total: ~$7.75

Brown the beef with the onion and pepper. Drain the fat. Add everything else and let it simmer for at least 30 minutes. I usually serve this with a box of Jiffy cornbread mix (costs about $0.55). That brings the total to just over $8.00 for a massive, soul-warming meal.

10. Breakfast for Dinner (Pancakes and Eggs)

This is the ultimate low-effort, high-comfort meal. It's also the cheapest on this list if you use a basic pancake recipe (flour, milk, baking powder, salt, sugar, egg).

The Cost:

  • 2 cups flour: $0.30
  • 2 cups milk: $0.40
  • 2 eggs: $0.40
  • 1 dozen eggs (for scrambling): $1.50
  • Maple syrup or honey: $0.50
  • Butter: $0.30
  • Total: ~$3.40

I whisk up the pancakes and cook them on a griddle. While the last batch is cooking, I scramble the other eggs in a pan. It’s a carb-heavy meal, but it’s cheap, fast, and everyone is happy. Sometimes I add a handful of chocolate chips to a few pancakes as a special treat, in practice.

My Favorite Kitchen Tools for Budget Cooking

You don't need fancy equipment, but a few specific tools genuinely make this type of cooking easier and faster. These are things I use weekly.

A good, heavy-bottomed stockpot is essential for making large batches of soup and chili. It heats evenly and won't scorch your food on the bottom, in my experience.

For the casseroles and baked potatoes, a sturdy glass baking dish is a must. I use my 9x13 Pyrex dish for almost everything. It goes from the oven to the fridge safely, which is great for leftovers.

If you are eating a lot of rice and beans, a basic Electric Rice Cooker is a game-changer. I set mine and forget it—it keeps the rice warm while I prep the rest of the meal. It saves me from burning pots on the stove.

I also rely on a Digital Kitchen Scale to portion out meat and dough accurately. It helps me stick to the budget by ensuring I know exactly how much I'm using.

How to Slash Your Bill Even Further

The meals above are a great start, but you can always push the envelope. Here are three more tweaks I use to keep the grocery bill low.

First, I buy "family packs" of meat when they are on sale. I repackage them into one-pound portions at home and freeze them. Buying a 5-pound tube of ground beef is often 30% cheaper per pound than buying a single one-pound pack, in practice.

Second, I use a rotisserie chicken for multiple meals. I buy one on Sunday for $5. We eat the legs and thighs for dinner. I then pick the breast meat for tacos or casseroles on Tuesday. Finally, I boil the carcass with an onion and some celery to make a rich broth for soup on Thursday. That $5 chicken feeds us for three nights.

Third, don't shop hungry. This sounds silly, but I have wasted more money on impulse buys when my stomach is growling than on any other mistake. I always eat a snack or a small meal before I walk into the grocery store.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the absolute cheapest meal I can make?

The absolute cheapest meal is usually a simple rice and beans dish. A pound of dry rice costs about $1.00, and a pound of dry beans costs about $1.50. When cooked, that combination yields roughly 10-12 cups of food. With a little onion, garlic, and spices, you can feed a family of four for less than $3.00 total, honestly.

Can I freeze these meals for later?

Yes, most of these freeze exceptionally well. I regularly double the chili, the lentil marinara, and the chicken and rice casserole. I let them cool completely, then portion them into freezer-safe containers or zip-top bags. They will keep for up to three months. Just thaw them in the fridge overnight and reheat gently.

How do I make these meals more filling for teenagers?

Teenagers are bottomless pits. To make these meals more filling, focus on adding bulk without adding cost. I always add an extra can of beans to ground meat dishes. I also serve a big green salad or a plate of steamed veggies on the side to fill the tummy. The fiber and water in vegetables help create that feeling of fullness quicker than just eating carbs alone.

The Bottom Line

Feeding a family of four on a budget is not about deprivation; it’s about strategy. By focusing on pantry staples, stretching your proteins, and cooking from scratch, you can easily put dinner on the table for under $10 a night. These meals are the ones I rely on week after week because they are cheap, fast, and most importantly, they get eaten without complaint. Start with one or two of these recipes this week, and you will see the savings add up quickly, in practice.
