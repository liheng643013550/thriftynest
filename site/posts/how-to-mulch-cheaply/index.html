How to Mulch Cheaply (Free Sources of Mulch You're Ignoring)

You don’t need to spend a fortune on bagged mulch from the big-box store. Landscaping supply companies charge $30 to $50 per cubic yard, and that adds up fast if you have any real garden beds. The truth is, your yard, your kitchen, and even your local government are producing perfectly good mulch every single day, and most of it gets thrown away.

Start With Your Own Yard Waste

Before you buy anything, look at what you are already raking, mowing, and pruning. This is the absolute cheapest source of mulch because the cost is just your time.

Grass clippings are a fantastic free mulch, but you have to use them correctly. They break down quickly and add nitrogen to the soil. Spread them in a thin layer, no more than one to two inches thick. If you pile them on too thick, they turn into a slimy, smelly mat that blocks water. Let them dry out for a day or two before spreading if you can, which prevents that matting issue.

Fallen leaves are arguably the best free mulch on the planet. They are nature’s own ground cover. Run your lawn mower over a pile of dry leaves to shred them into smaller pieces. Whole leaves can form a barrier that repels water, but shredded leaves break down into rich, crumbly organic matter that worms love. A thick layer of shredded leaves will suppress weeds all winter and feed your soil as it decomposes.

Pine needles are another excellent free option, especially if you have pine trees. They knit together into a breathable mat that stays in place on slopes better than almost anything else. They are slightly acidic, but the effect on your soil pH is minimal for most gardens. They are perfect around acid-loving plants like blueberries, azaleas, and rhododendrons.

Wood chips from pruned branches work too, but only if you have a chipper or a very patient neighbor with one. If you are pruning trees or shrubs, chip the branches and use the result as a woody mulch. This is a higher-effort option, but it is completely free.

Check With Local Arborists and Tree Services

This is the hidden gem of cheap mulching. Tree removal and trimming companies generate massive piles of wood chips every single day. They have to pay to dump those chips at a landfill or recycling facility, so they are usually thrilled to give them away.

Call a few local tree services and ask if they have a "chip drop" list. Most will deliver a full truckload of freshly chipped wood to your driveway for free, especially if you live within their normal service area. You are saving them a trip to the dump, so it is a win-win.

There are a few things to know before you take a free chip drop:

  • The pile is huge. We are talking about a full dump truck load, which is roughly 10 to 15 cubic yards. That will cover a large area, but it is a lot of physical labor to move it by wheelbarrow.
  • The chips are fresh. Fresh wood chips can temporarily tie up nitrogen in the soil as they decompose. Do not dig them into your garden beds. Use them only as a top dressing, and the nitrogen issue disappears because the decomposition happens at the soil surface.
  • You don't control the mix. You might get pure pine, a mix of hardwoods, or a pile full of leaves and twigs. It is all fine for mulch, but it will not be uniform.

If you don't want to make phone calls, you can use a service like ChipDrop, which connects homeowners with local arborists. It is a free service, although you can offer a small "tip" to the driver to get your drop prioritized faster.

Get Free Wood Chips From Utility Companies

Utility companies that maintain power lines and roadways constantly cut down trees and brush. They often chip the wood on-site and leave the pile behind for the landowner, or they haul it to a central location.

Contact your local electric cooperative, city public works department, or highway department. Ask if they have a free mulch pile that residents can access. Many municipalities maintain a compost and mulch yard where they process collected yard waste and give it back to residents at no cost. You just have to bring your own shovel and truck.

Hit Up Your Neighbors

Your neighbors are a goldmine of free mulch, even if they don't know it. Most homeowners bag up their leaves and grass clippings and set them on the curb for trash pickup. That is prime mulching material going to waste.

Post a message on your neighborhood social media group or Nextdoor app. Say something simple like, "I will take your bagged leaves and grass clippings off your hands for free. Just leave them by the curb." You will be surprised at how many people are happy to have you haul away their yard waste.

This method requires a bit more patience because you are collecting bag by bag, but it is completely free and often yields high-quality, shredded leaves that are perfect for vegetable gardens and flower beds.

Check Your Local Recycling Center

Many cities and counties run a yard waste recycling program. They collect curbside yard waste, compost it or chip it on a massive scale, and then offer the finished product back to residents for free or at a very low cost.

Call your local solid waste district or public works department and ask these specific questions:

  • Do you offer free compost or mulch to residents?
  • Is there a limit on how much I can take per visit?
  • Do I need to bring my own container or truck?
  • What is the source of the mulch (wood chips vs. composted leaves)?

This is often the easiest and cleanest option because the material is professionally processed. It might not be free in every town, but it is almost always dramatically cheaper than buying bagged mulch at a garden center. Expect to pay a nominal fee, like $10 for a truckload, or nothing at all.

Repurpose Cardboard and Newspaper

This is the ultimate budget trick for suppressing weeds. Cardboard and newspaper are not traditional mulch, but they work incredibly well as a base layer, and they are free.

Cardboard is the best weed barrier you will ever use. Break down boxes, remove all tape and plastic shipping labels, and lay the cardboard flat over your garden bed. Overlap the edges by at least six inches so weeds can't poke through the seams. Soak it thoroughly with water, then cover it with a thin layer of any other mulch you have (leaves, grass clippings, or wood chips) to hold it down and hide it.

The cardboard blocks all light, which kills existing weeds and prevents new ones from germinating. As it gets wet, it breaks down into cardboard pulp, which worms will happily eat and turn into castings.

Newspaper works the same way, but you need to layer it about four to six sheets thick. Skip the glossy inserts, which don't break down well. Just like cardboard, it smothers weeds and adds organic matter to the soil as it decomposes.

You can get free cardboard from local businesses. Ask at grocery stores, liquor stores, bike shops, or appliance stores. They all receive massive shipments in boxes and usually have a recycling bin out back. Ask the manager if you can take the flattened boxes; most will say yes.

The Bottom Line

Cheap mulching is not about finding a bargain at the garden center. It is about recognizing that you are surrounded by free resources. Your own lawn, your neighbor's trash pile, and your local tree service are all producing high-quality mulch that they are practically paying to get rid of.

Start small. Rake your leaves, make a few phone calls to local arborists, and ask around your neighborhood. You will save hundreds of dollars, keep waste out of the landfill, and improve your garden soil at the same time. All it takes is a little effort and a willingness to look at a pile of wood chips as the treasure it actually is.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does mulch from a landscaping supply company cost?

Landscaping supply companies typically charge between $30 and $50 per cubic yard, and that cost climbs quickly if you have large garden beds to cover. Buying bagged mulch from a big-box store is another paid option the article suggests avoiding in favor of free sources.

How thick should grass clippings be spread as mulch?

Grass clippings should go down in a thin layer of no more than one to two inches. Piling them on thicker than that creates a slimy, smelly mat that blocks water from reaching the soil. Letting the clippings dry for a day or two before spreading also helps prevent matting.

How much mulch comes in a free chip drop from a tree service?

A full truckload from a tree service is roughly 10 to 15 cubic yards, which covers a large area but requires significant physical labor to move by wheelbarrow. The chips arrive fresh, so they should be used only as a top dressing rather than dug into garden beds, and the mix of wood types is not something you can control.
