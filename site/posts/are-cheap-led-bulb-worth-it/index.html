Are Cheap LED Bulbs Worth It? We Did the Math

The question that decides everything with cheap LED bulbs is not "how long do they last?" It is "does the light actually fit where I need it?"

If you are standing in the bulb aisle with a toddler on your hip and eleven minutes before daycare pickup, you do not have time to compare lumens-per-watt charts. You want to know one thing: will the four-dollar bulb do the same job as the nine-dollar bulb, or am I going to be back here in March buying replacements?

Here is the honest answer, and it depends almost entirely on where the bulb is going.

The question that decides everything

For most fixtures, a cheap LED bulb is fine. For tight spaces, it is often not.

The reason is heat. LEDs do not burn out the way old incandescent bulbs did — the light-emitting part can run for tens of thousands of hours. What kills them is heat building up behind the bulb, and cheap bulbs cut costs partly by using smaller heat sinks and cheaper driver electronics. In an open table lamp with air moving around it, that is not a big deal. Crammed inside a sealed glass globe, a recessed can with insulation packed over it, or a tiny enclosed ceiling fixture, that heat has nowhere to go.

This is why the footprint angle matters more than the price tag. A bulb that is a quarter-inch too wide will not fit inside a small shade or a shallow recessed housing. A bulb that runs hot in an enclosed fixture will dim, flicker, or die early — and then you have paid twice.

So the real test is: open fixture or enclosed fixture? If it is open, buy cheap. If it is enclosed or tight, buy carefully.

What it actually costs

Let's do the first-year math on a single bulb, because that is what makes the decision concrete.

A typical cheap LED bulb — the store-brand multi-pack kind, usually sold in packs of four — runs somewhere around $1.50 to $3 per bulb when you buy the pack. Call it $2. A mid-tier name-brand LED in the same brightness typically runs $4 to $8 per bulb. That is a $2 to $6 gap per bulb, which sounds trivial until you multiply it across the eight or ten fixtures in a typical house.

Running cost is where cheap and expensive LEDs barely differ. A bulb rated at 800 lumens — roughly the old 60-watt incandescent replacement — draws about 8 to 12 watts in most LED designs. At a US average electricity rate in the range of 15 to 17 cents per kilowatt-hour, running that bulb three hours a day costs roughly $1.50 to $2.50 per year. Both the cheap bulb and the expensive bulb land in that same band. You are not saving meaningful electricity by spending more.

The consumable cost is the replacement. This is the part people forget. A cheap bulb in a hot, enclosed fixture may last a year or two instead of the 10-plus years the package implies. If you replace it twice while a better-built bulb would have lasted once, the cheap bulb just cost you $4 to $6 plus two trips to the store — more than the name brand, plus your time.

The other hidden cost is compatibility. Cheap LEDs are more likely to buzz or flicker on dimmer switches, and more likely to interfere with a dimmer that was designed for incandescent loads. If you have to swap a dimmer to fix flicker, that is a $20-plus part and an hour of your evening. That single problem wipes out the savings on an entire houseful of cheap bulbs.

If you want to know exactly what a given fixture is costing you, a plug-in meter takes the guesswork out. The P3 P4460 Kill A Watt EZ is the standard tool for this — you plug the lamp into it and read actual watts and cumulative kilowatt-hours. It will not tell you anything about bulb quality, but it settles the "is this costing me money" question in about a minute.

What you get for that money

At the cheap end, what you are buying is a bulb with a shorter warranty, looser quality control, and a wider spread in real-world color. Two bulbs from the same four-pack may look slightly different from each other. The color temperature label — 2700K for warm white, 5000K for daylight — is usually accurate enough, but the actual light quality varies more than it does on premium bulbs.

You also typically get less accurate color rendering. On cheap bulbs the CRI (color rendering index) is often unlisted, and when it is listed it may be in the low 80s. That matters in a bathroom mirror or over a kitchen counter where you are judging whether meat is cooked. In a hallway or a closet, it does not matter at all.

What you give up in exchange is mostly consistency and lifespan, not brightness. A cheap 800-lumen bulb is generally as bright as an expensive 800-lumen bulb. Lumens are lumens.

At the mid-tier, you are paying for a longer warranty (often three to five years), tighter color consistency across a multi-pack, better dimming behavior, and a physical design that manages heat better. That last point is the one that actually connects to your small-space problem.

When it is NOT worth it

Be honest with yourself about these cases, because this is where cheap bulbs cost you more:

Enclosed fixtures. Recessed cans with a glass trim, ceiling globes, and enclosed vanity bars trap heat. Owner reviews on cheap bulbs frequently mention early failure specifically in these spots.

Dimmers. If the circuit is on a dimmer, especially an older one, cheap LEDs are the most common cause of flicker and buzz complaints. Check that the bulb is marked dimmable, and expect to pay a little more for one that dims smoothly.

Fixtures you cannot easily reach. A bulb in a vaulted ceiling fixture or a stairwell that needs a ladder is not where you want to save two dollars. Pay for the one that lasts.

Small shades and tight sockets. This is the footprint issue. Some LED bulbs have a noticeably wider base or a taller profile than the old incandescent they replace, and they simply will not fit under a small lampshade or inside a shallow sconce. Measure your fixture before you buy, and check the listed bulb dimensions rather than assuming.

Color-critical spots. Bathroom mirrors, makeup areas, and kitchen task lighting are worth spending on.

The cheaper alternative

Here is the thing worth knowing: the biggest savings in home lighting usually do not come from the bulb at all. They come from controlling when the light is on.

A smart plug lets you put a lamp on a schedule or turn it off from your phone, which cuts the hours a bulb runs in the first place. That is a real reduction in the electricity line of the math above, and it costs less than replacing a houseful of bulbs.

The Kasa Smart Plug HS103 is a single-outlet Wi-Fi plug that works with Alexa and Google Assistant without a hub. If you would rather buy a two-pack and cover two lamps at once, the Kasa Smart Plug HS103P2 is the same plug sold as a pair.

Neither one changes the bulb quality question. They just reduce the running hours, which is the one number in this whole calculation you actually control.

The bottom line

Yes, cheap LED bulbs are worth it — with one condition. Buy them for open fixtures, lamps, hallways, closets, and anywhere you can reach the bulb easily. In those spots the math works clearly in your favor, and the electricity savings are identical to what you would get from a premium bulb.

No, they are not worth it in enclosed fixtures, on dimmers, in hard-to-reach spots, or anywhere you need accurate color. There, the replacement cost and the flicker risk eat the savings, and you end up paying more in money and in trips to the store.

If you only remember one thing: check whether the fixture is enclosed and measure the space before you buy. That single step decides the answer for every bulb in your house.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do cheap LED bulbs use more electricity than expensive ones?

No. A bulb rated at 800 lumens draws roughly 8 to 12 watts whether it is cheap or premium, and at typical US electricity rates that runs about $1.50 to $2.50 per year at three hours a day. The difference between cheap and expensive LEDs shows up in lifespan and color consistency, not in the power bill.

Why do cheap LED bulbs fail early in some fixtures?

Heat. LEDs die from heat buildup rather than from the light-emitting part wearing out, and enclosed fixtures — recessed cans with trim, ceiling globes, and enclosed vanity bars — trap that heat. Owner reviews frequently mention early failure in exactly those spots, while the same bulbs last much longer in open lamps.

Can I use a smart plug to cut what my lighting costs?

Yes, and it is often the bigger win. A smart plug like the Kasa Smart Plug HS103 puts a lamp on a schedule or lets you shut it off remotely, which reduces the hours the bulb runs. Since running cost is roughly $1.50 to $2.50 per bulb per year, cutting those hours is a real saving — and it does not depend on which bulb you bought.
