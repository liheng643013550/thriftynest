How to Clean a Washing Machine Cheaply (Yes, It Needs It)

Your washing machine works hard. Every week, it tackles muddy soccer uniforms, greasy work clothes, and the mystery stain that appeared on your favorite shirt. But here’s the thing nobody tells you: the machine needs a bath too. Over time, detergent scum, fabric softener residue, and hard water minerals build up inside the drum and hoses. This gunk can trap bacteria, which leads to a musty smell that clings to your "clean" clothes. You don't need a fancy $20 cleaning pod or a plumber to fix this. You probably have everything you need in your pantry right now.

To be fair, here is my step-by-step guide to getting your washer sparkling clean for less than the cost of a gallon of milk.

Why Your Washer Gets Gross (And Why You Should Care)

As a rule of thumb, i used to think the machine cleaned itself with every cycle. I was wrong. The reality is that a typical wash cycle leaves behind about a cup of stagnant water and a thin film of residue. When you close the door, you’re creating a dark, damp, warm environment—a perfect petri dish for mold and mildew, in practice.

Honestly, that funky smell isn't just annoying. It can cause skin irritation for sensitive folks and make your towels smell sour no matter how much detergent you use. The good news? You can fix it with two household staples: white vinegar and baking soda. A 64-ounce jug of distilled white vinegar costs about $1.50 at my local grocery store, and a box of baking soda is under a buck. That's your entire cleaning budget.

What You'll Need

Before we start, gather your supplies. You don't need a special brush or a chemical cocktail. Here is my exact shopping list:

  • 2 cups of white distilled vinegar (the cheap stuff in the plastic jug)
  • 1/2 cup of baking soda
  • An old microfiber cloth or an old toothbrush
  • A spray bottle (optional, but helps for the door seal)

That's it. Seriously. If you have a front-loader, you might also want to grab a few paper towels to soak up the water in the rubber gasket, believe it or not.

Step 1: Run the Vinegar Cycle

The first step is to tackle the mineral buildup and kill the odor-causing bacteria. Vinegar is a natural acid that breaks down soap scum and dissolves hard water minerals. It’s also a natural deodorizer.

Pour 2 cups of white vinegar directly into the detergent dispenser drawer. If your dispenser has a separate compartment for bleach, you can put it there instead. Set your machine to the hottest, longest cycle available. For my standard top-loader, that's the "Heavy Duty" setting with the temperature cranked to hot. Press start and let it run empty.

I know it smells like salad dressing while it runs, but that smell fades completely by the end of the cycle. Don't skip the hot water. The heat is what activates the vinegar and helps dissolve the gunk that cold water leaves behind, believe it or not.

Step 2: The Baking Soda Scrub

Once the vinegar cycle finishes, you're halfway there. The vinegar loosened the grime; now we need to scrub it away. Baking soda is a mild abrasive that won't scratch the metal drum, but it will lift the residue.

Add 1/2 cup of baking soda directly into the empty drum (not the dispenser—pour it right into the tub). Run another hot cycle. This time, you can choose a shorter cycle, like "Normal" or "Quick Wash," just as long as it uses hot water.

This second cycle helps neutralize the vinegar smell and gives the drum a gentle polishing. The water will look cloudy and might have some little flecks floating in it. That's the gunk leaving your machine. It's gross, but it means it's working, in practice.

Step 3: Wipe Down the Door and Gasket

This is the step most people skip, and it's the number one reason machines smell bad again within a week. The rubber gasket on a front-load washer is a notorious mold trap. Water pools in those folds and never dries out.

Take your old microfiber cloth and dip it in a little white vinegar. Wipe down the entire rubber seal, pulling the folds back to get every crevice. You will likely find some black or pinkish slime. That's mold and bacteria. Use an old toothbrush dipped in vinegar to scrub those stubborn spots.

For my machine, I also check the detergent drawer. I pull it all the way out and rinse it in the sink. There's always a little soap sludge hiding in the back of it. A quick rinse with hot water and a scrub with the toothbrush fixes it right up. Do this every time you clean the drum, in practice.

Step 4: Don't Forget the Filter (The Grossest Step)

I have to be honest: I ignored this step for two years, and my washer started taking forever to drain. Most modern washers, especially front-loaders, have a lint trap or drain pump filter. It catches coins, hairpins, and that one sock that always escapes.

Usually, it’s a small panel at the bottom front of the machine. Place a towel underneath it, because opening it will release water. Twist the cap off slowly. You'll probably find a nasty wad of hair and lint. Pull it out, rinse the filter under the tap, and pop it back in.

This filter is the reason your machine drains slowly. Cleaning it takes two minutes and prevents costly pump repairs down the line. Check your owner's manual if you can't find the panel—it's a standard feature on most brands like Samsung or LG, honestly.

Step 5: Freshen Up the Inside (Optional but Nice)

If you have a top-loader, you might notice a bit of buildup along the top edge of the drum, right at the waterline. I use a spray bottle filled with a 50/50 mix of white vinegar and water for this. Spray the rim, let it sit for five minutes, and wipe it down with a cloth.

For a quick refresh in between deep cleans, I sometimes run an empty cycle with just 1 cup of vinegar every month. It keeps the smell away without the full two-cycle routine. It’s a cheap habit that saves you from buying those expensive washing machine cleaner tablets that cost $8 for a pack of three.

The Bottom Line

You don't need a professional or a pricey chemical cleaner to keep your washer fresh. A dollar's worth of vinegar and baking soda does the job just as well. I clean my machine this way every three months, and it takes about an hour of my time (mostly waiting for the cycles to finish). The result is a machine that drains faster, smells fresh, and gets my clothes genuinely clean, honestly.

Set a reminder on your phone for the first of every quarter. Your nose—and your laundry—will thank you.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I clean my washing machine with vinegar?

I do a deep clean like this every three months. If you live in an area with very hard water, or if you wash a lot of heavily soiled clothes, bump it up to once a month. A quick vinegar rinse cycle in between deep cleans also helps keep the smell at bay.

Is it safe to mix vinegar and baking soda in the washer?

Yes, but not in the same cycle. Mixing them in a closed space can cause a fizzy overflow, and they neutralize each other. That's why I run the vinegar cycle first, drain it, and then run the baking soda cycle. They work together sequentially to clean, but they don't need to be mixed in one load, in practice.

Can I use this method on a high-efficiency (HE) washer?

Absolutely. In fact, HE washers need this more often because they use less water, which means residue is more concentrated. Just make sure you are using the correct amount of product. Two cups of vinegar is fine for a standard size drum, but if you have a huge 5.0 cu. ft. machine, you might want to add an extra half cup to ensure it reaches all the surfaces.
