The one question that decides everything here is this: will the blender survive a hot, thick, fibrous load every week for a year without the motor quitting or the jar cracking? Soup is harder on a blender than a smoothie ever will be — no ice, no frozen fruit, just heavy, hot, fibrous work. If you hate buying things twice, that question matters more than wattage bragging rights.

What actually matters

  • Motor wattage and duty cycle. Budget blenders typically run 300–700 watts. For soup, what matters is whether the motor can run 60–90 seconds without smelling hot. Owner reviews of cheap blenders often mention a burning smell during thick blends — that is the failure mode to avoid.
  • Jar material. Plastic scratches and holds onto tomato and curry stains. Glass resists staining and handles heat better, but it is heavier and can chip. For soup specifically, glass or Tritan is the safer long-term choice.
  • Heat tolerance. If you blend hot soup, the jar and lid need to handle near-boiling liquid, and you need a vented lid or you will get a steam explosion. Many budget blenders say "do not blend hot liquids" in the manual — check before buying.
  • Easy-clean design. A removable blade assembly and a wide jar mouth mean less scraping. This is the difference between a blender you use weekly and one that sits in the cabinet.
  • Replacement parts and warranty. A 1-year warranty on a $35 blender is a warning, not a feature. Brands that sell replacement jars and gaskets keep a cheap blender alive for years.
  • Real first-year cost. Add the purchase price, any replacement jar or gasket, and the value of your time if it dies mid-winter. That number is the one to compare.

Cuisinart Smart Stick CSB-75

Rough price band: typically $35–$50.

Immersion blenders are the sleeper pick for soup because you blend directly in the pot — no transferring hot liquid, no second vessel to wash. The CSB-75 is a two-speed stick with a stainless shaft, and owner reviews consistently mention it handling pureed vegetable soups and tomato sauces without complaint. Fewer parts means fewer things to break.

Downsides: it will not give you a silky, restaurant-smooth texture the way a high-powered jar blender can. Fibrous things like leeks or tough kale stems may stay slightly stringy. It also cannot crush ice or handle smoothie bowls well, so if you want one appliance for both jobs, this is not it.

Who should buy it: someone who makes soup two or three times a month, wants minimal cleanup, and would rather spend $40 once than $40 every year on a jar blender that burns out.

The bottom line

If you want the lowest total cost over the first year and you mostly make soup, buy the Cuisinart Smart Stick CSB-75 and a 5-quart Dutch oven. That combination typically lands under $120, has almost no failure points, and will still be working in five years. The stick blender has one moving part and no jar to crack.

If you insist on a jar blender, the Cuisinart Perfectemp is the safer bet because replacement parts exist. Skip the absolute cheapest options unless you genuinely treat them as disposable — a $30 blender that dies in eight months is a worse deal than a $70 one that lasts three years, every time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a cheap blender handle hot soup?

Only if the manual says it can. Many budget blenders warn against hot liquids, and blending near-boiling soup in a sealed jar can cause a steam burst. An immersion blender like the Cuisinart Smart Stick CSB-75 lets you blend in the pot, which sidesteps the problem entirely.

What is the cheapest way to make soup at home?

Pair a budget immersion blender in the $35–$50 range with a 5-quart Dutch oven. That combination usually costs less than a single mid-range countertop blender and has far fewer parts that can fail.

How long do cheap blenders typically last?

It varies by model and how hard you push it. Owner reviews of budget-tier units often report failures within a year, while mid-range models with available replacement parts tend to last several years. That is why first-year cost matters more than sticker price.
