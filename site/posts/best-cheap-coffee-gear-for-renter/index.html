What's the one thing that decides whether a coffee setup works for a renter? Not the flavor. Not the price. It's whether you can fix it, refill it, and eventually replace the parts without begging a landlord for permission to drill a hole in the countertop.

If you hate buying things that die in six months, the real question isn't "which brewer makes the best coffee." It's "which brewer can I keep alive for years in a rental kitchen?" That means filters you can buy at any grocery store, carafes and gaskets that are sold separately, and nothing that needs a plumber or a security deposit on the line.

This guide focuses on cheap gear that's easy to live with, easy to repair, and easy to replace one piece at a time instead of the whole machine.

What actually matters

Before you spend a dollar, run every option through these filters. They matter more than brand prestige at this price point.

  • Replacement parts availability. Can you buy a new carafe, filter basket, gasket, or pod holder on its own? If the answer is no, the machine is disposable by design.
  • Filter type. Cone filters and flat-bottom basket filters are sold in every supermarket and big-box store for a few dollars per hundred. Proprietary pods lock you into one supplier and one price.
  • No permanent installation. Skip anything that needs drilling, hardwiring, or under-cabinet mounting. Countertop only.
  • Footprint and clearance. Rental kitchens are often small, and cabinets sit lower than you think. Measure your counter and your cabinet gap before buying.
  • Power draw. Most drip machines and single-serve brewers pull somewhere in the 600 to 1,500 watt range. A kettle is typically 1,200 to 1,500 watts. On a shared circuit, that matters.
  • Consumable cost per year. Filters, descaler, and pods add up. A cheap machine with expensive pods can cost more over two years than a slightly pricier machine with cheap paper filters.

Mr. Coffee 5-Cup Mini Brew

Rough price band: under $30.

This is the classic small-drip answer for a rental. It's a 5-cup (roughly 25-ounce) machine, which is about right for one or two people. The specs show a simple on/off switch, a reusable or paper basket filter, and a glass carafe.

What makes it a good pick is the parts ecosystem. Owner reviews consistently report that the carafe and filter basket are easy to find as separate replacements, and the machine takes standard small basket filters you can grab anywhere. Nothing is proprietary. If the carafe cracks, you're out a few dollars, not a whole appliance.

The real downsides: no timer, no auto-shutoff sophistication on the base models, and the coffee quality is fine but not remarkable. The warming plate can scorch coffee if it sits for an hour. It's also not built for a decade of daily use.

Who should buy it: someone who wants a $25 machine, cheap filters, and zero commitment.

Black+Decker 12-Cup Programmable Coffee Maker

Rough price band: $30 to $50.

If you want a full-size drip machine with a timer, this is the budget standard. The specs typically list a 12-cup glass carafe, a programmable brew time, and a sneak-a-cup pause feature. Wattage on this class of machine usually lands around 900 to 1,000 watts.

The replacement-part story is the selling point here. Black+Decker carafes and filter baskets are widely stocked, and the machine uses standard basket filters. Reviewers often mention that the carafe is the first thing to break and that a replacement is cheap and easy to order.

Downsides: the build is plastic-heavy, the warming plate runs hot, and the "12 cups" is really about 60 ounces, not 12 mugs. Some owners report the display dimming over time.

Who should buy it: a household of two or more that wants a timer and doesn't want to think about it.

AeroPress Original

Rough price band: $35 to $40.

No electricity, no carafe, no filter basket to replace. The AeroPress uses small paper micro-filters, and a pack of 350 typically runs around $8 to $10. It also ships with a reusable metal filter option in some bundles.

This is arguably the most renter-proof brewer on the list. There's almost nothing to break. The only real wear item is the rubber plunger seal, and replacement seals are sold separately. Owner reviews frequently mention people using the same AeroPress for many years with only a seal swap.

The downsides: it brews one or two cups at a time, so it's slow for a crowd. It also requires a separate kettle for hot water, which is an extra purchase. And it's a hands-on process, not a push-button one.

Who should buy it: a solo renter or a couple who wants near-zero failure points and cheap filters forever.

Bodum Chambord French Press

Rough price band: $20 to $40 depending on size.

A French press has exactly one wear part: the mesh filter and plunger assembly. Bodum sells replacement filter and plunger sets separately, and they're inexpensive. The carafe itself is the fragile piece, and on the glass models, a drop on tile ends it. Bodum also makes a version with a more durable carafe material.

The specs are simple: coarse ground coffee, hot water, four minutes, press. No filters to buy at all if you use the included mesh. That makes the long-run consumable cost close to zero.

Downsides: glass carafes break, cleanup takes a minute, and you get sediment in the cup unless you grind coarse. It also doesn't keep coffee hot.

Who should buy it: someone who wants a $25 brewer with essentially no ongoing filter cost.

Keurig K-Mini

Rough price band: $60 to $90.

Single-serve has a bad reputation among people who hate buying things that break, and for good reason. But the K-Mini deserves a mention because it's tiny, it's the entry point to the Keurig ecosystem, and Keurig sells replacement parts and a reusable My K-Cup filter.

That reusable filter is the key detail for a renter watching costs. It lets you use your own ground coffee instead of pods, which cuts the per-cup cost dramatically. Owner reviews often mention descaling being the main maintenance task, and Keurig's descaler is sold separately.

Downsides: pods are expensive if you don't use the reusable filter, the reservoir is small (about 12 ounces on the K-Mini), and these machines are known to develop issues after a few years. It's the least repairable pick on this list.

Who should buy it: someone in a tiny space who wants one cup at a time and will commit to the reusable filter.

OXO Brew Conical Burr Coffee Grinder

Rough price band: $80 to $100.

A grinder isn't coffee gear you drink, but it's the piece that makes cheap brewers taste better, and it's the piece most likely to outlast everything else. The OXO Brew uses conical burrs with 15 settings.

The replacement-part angle: burrs eventually wear, and OXO sells replacement burr sets. That's rare at this price. Most budget blade grinders are throwaway items with no serviceable parts.

Downsides: it's the priciest item here, it's louder than you'd like, and it takes up counter space. Some owners report static cling making a mess.

Who should buy it: someone who plans to keep a cheap brewer for years and wants the coffee to actually taste good.

Why replacement parts should drive your decision

Here's the math renters usually miss. A $25 drip machine with a $10 replacement carafe every two years costs less over five years than a $120 machine with a proprietary pod system that gets discontinued.

Paper filters are the cheapest consumable in coffee. A 100-count pack of basket or cone filters typically runs a few dollars. Over a year of daily brewing, that's often under $15. Pods, by contrast, can run $0.50 to $1.00 each, which is $180 or more per year at one cup a day.

So the honest ranking for a renter who hates replacing things is: French press and AeroPress first (almost no consumables), standard drip machines second (cheap filters, stock parts), single-serve last (convenience tax, weakest repairability).

The bottom line

If you want the safest bet, get the AeroPress Original or a Bodum Chambord French Press. Neither needs drilling, neither has a carafe to shatter on the AeroPress side, and both use filters or parts you can buy for years without hunting.

If you want push-button convenience, the Mr. Coffee 5-Cup Mini Brew and the Black+Decker 12-Cup Programmable are the picks that respect your budget and your ability to find a replacement carafe at short notice.

Skip anything proprietary unless you've done the pod math and you're fine with it. In a rental, the gear that survives is the gear you can fix.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a landlord's permission to use these coffee makers?

No. Every product here is a countertop appliance that plugs into a standard outlet. None of them require drilling, mounting, or plumbing changes, so nothing touches your lease or your security deposit.

How often do I need to replace coffee filters and parts?

Paper filters get replaced every brew, but a pack of 100 typically lasts a few months. Wear parts like French press plungers, AeroPress seals, and drip machine carafes usually last one to three years with normal use, and all of the picks above have replacements sold separately.

Is a cheap coffee maker worth it compared to a more expensive one?

It depends on whether you can get parts. A cheap machine with widely available filters and carafes often outlasts a pricier machine with proprietary pods. If repairability matters more to you than features, the budget picks here are the smarter long-term buy.
