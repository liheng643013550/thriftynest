What actually matters

  • Footprint and opening width. In a dorm, the gate has to span a doorway or a narrow room divider, usually somewhere between 28 and 38 inches. Measure the opening before you buy anything, and check whether the gate needs a wall cup or pressure mount — many cheap gates do not come with hardware for both.
  • Mount type. Pressure-mounted gates need two flat, sturdy surfaces and no drilling, which is ideal for a rental or a dorm where you can't put holes in the wall. Hardware-mounted gates are more secure but require screws.
  • Swing versus walk-through. A gate with a walk-through door is worth the extra few dollars in a small room, because you'll be stepping over it twenty times a day otherwise. A fixed panel with no door gets old fast.
  • Height. Most budget gates run about 23 to 30 inches tall. That's fine for a small dog or a cat; it is not going to hold a determined 60-pound dog.
  • Weight and storage. If you're moving the gate between a doorway and a closet, lighter is better. Metal gates in this range typically weigh 8 to 15 pounds.
  • What it's actually rated for. Manufacturer specs list a recommended pet height or weight range, and owner reviews will tell you whether that rating holds up. Read both, because the spec is the marketing claim and the reviews are the reality.

North States Supergate

Rough price band: typically $25 to $40 depending on the model and finish.

What makes it a good pick: North States has been making pressure-mounted gates for years, and the Supergate line is the one most often recommended for tight doorways and odd-shaped openings. The standard versions are pressure-mounted, so no drilling, and the narrower configurations are designed for exactly the kind of opening you find in a dorm or apartment. Owner reviews frequently mention that it installs in a few minutes without tools.

Real downsides: the cheaper models are basic — usually a simple swing panel with a latch, no walk-through door on every version, and the plastic components feel light. The height is modest, generally in the 26 to 30 inch range depending on the model, so it's a barrier for small pets, not a containment system for a large dog. Also, the sub-$35 versions tend to be the narrower ones, so measure carefully.

Who should buy it: a student or renter who needs a no-drill gate for a doorway and has a small dog or a cat.

Summer Infant Multi-Use

Rough price band: usually $30 to $50 depending on the configuration.

What makes it a good pick: the Multi-Use line is a pressure-mounted gate that can also be configured as a freestanding play yard in some versions, which is genuinely useful in a small room. The walk-through door and one-hand latch are the features people actually use every day. Specs generally put these around 30 inches tall, which is a reasonable middle ground.

Real downsides: this is a baby gate first, so the opening width and the accessory pieces are designed around child safety standards, and some configurations push past the $35 mark. The freestanding setup takes up more floor space than a plain doorway gate. Owner reviews sometimes note that the pressure mounts need a solid, flat surface — a hollow dorm door frame can be fussy.

Who should buy it: someone who wants a walk-through door and might use the freestanding mode in a small room.

Richell Wood Pet Gate

Rough price band: often $60 and up, so this is a stretch pick, not a budget one.

What makes it a good pick: Richell's wood gates are the ones to look at if you care about how the gate looks in a small space. A wood gate reads as furniture rather than baby gear, which matters when your living room is also your bedroom. These are typically freestanding or hardware-mounted panels with a walk-through door, and the build quality is a step up from plastic.

Real downsides: price, clearly. It's well above $35 in most sizes. The wood construction also means more weight, so it's not something you'll move around casually. And freestanding wood panels take up real floor footprint — the opposite of what a tiny room wants.

Who should buy it: someone who can stretch the budget and wants a gate that doesn't look like a gate.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to drill holes to install a pet gate in a dorm?

Not necessarily. Pressure-mounted gates like the North States Supergate are designed to hold in place without screws, which is why they're the usual choice for renters and dorm rooms. They do need two flat, solid surfaces to press against, so a flimsy or hollow door frame can be tricky — check that the frame is sturdy before you commit.

How wide an opening can a cheap pet gate cover?

It varies a lot by model. The budget-friendly configurations in the North States Supergate and Summer Infant Multi-Use lines are generally built for standard doorways and narrow openings, while wider spans often require extension pieces that can push the price up. Measure your opening first, then confirm the gate's listed width range before buying.

What's the cheapest way to keep a cat and a dog apart in a small room?

Vertical and flat space is usually cheaper than a barrier. The Amazon Basics Cat Scratching Post takes up roughly a square foot of floor, and the PetFusion Ultimate Cat Scratcher is flat enough to slide under a bed, so both give a cat a zone the dog can't reach without fencing off any floor space at all.
