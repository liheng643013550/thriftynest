Smart Plug: Worth It or Waste? Honest Budget Math

You keep eyeing the smart plugs on the endcap, then remember the grocery budget is already tight and the kitchen counter has about four inches of free outlet space. So is a $15-ish gadget that turns a lamp on a schedule actually worth it, or is it just another app you'll stop opening by March? The honest answer depends on what you're plugging in and whether the thing even fits where you need it.

The biggest mistake buyers make

The single most common regret in owner reviews isn't that the plug failed. It's buying a plug that's too bulky for the outlet. A lot of budget models are chunky blocks that hog the whole wall plate, so the second outlet becomes useless — a real problem in a small kitchen where the coffee maker and the toaster are fighting for the same duplex.

The other version of this mistake: buying a Wi-Fi plug for a spot where the Wi-Fi barely reaches. These things run on 2.4 GHz networks, and a kitchen at the back of an old house is often the deadest zone in the place. Reviewers routinely mention plugs that drop offline or take ten seconds to respond, and it's almost always signal, not the plug.

Before you spend a dollar, do two things. Hold a phone at the outlet and check your signal bars. Then look at the outlet itself — is it behind a cabinet, under a lip, in a tight corner? A plug that sticks out an inch and a half may not fit at all.

What it actually costs

The purchase price is the easy part. Budget Wi-Fi plugs typically run anywhere from about $8 on sale to $25 for a name brand, and two-packs usually land cheaper per plug.

Running costs are close to nothing. A smart plug itself draws a fraction of a watt — call it well under a dollar a year in electricity at typical US rates, though it varies by model. What matters more is what the plug lets you control. If it's switching a 1,500-watt space heater, that's real money per hour, and the plug isn't saving it — the schedule is.

There's no real consumable cost. No filters, no batteries in most models. The hidden cost is your time and attention: setup, firmware updates, and the occasional re-pairing when the router reboots. Budget 20 minutes to set up the first one and a few minutes a year after that.

One more first-year cost people forget: if your outlet is cramped, you may end up buying a short extension cord or a slim power strip to make the plug fit. That's another $8 to $15.

What you get for that money

A working smart plug buys you three things, and only three.

Scheduling. Coffee maker starts at 6:40 a.m. so it's ready when you stumble in. A slow cooker turns off after four hours instead of running until you remember. Owner reviews consistently cite the coffee maker and the holiday lights as the two uses people actually keep.

Remote off. You left the iron or the space heater on. You can kill it from your phone. This is the feature that quietly justifies the purchase for a lot of people.

Energy awareness. Some plugs report how much a device draws. That's genuinely useful for finding the thing in your kitchen that's quietly eating watts.

What you don't get: a smart plug does not make a dumb appliance smart in any meaningful way. It can't preheat an oven, change a temperature, or tell you the toast is done. It switches power on and off. That's it.

For a small kitchen specifically, look at the physical size before the feature list. Slim plugs that sit flush against the wall are the ones that don't block the second outlet. The Kasa Smart Plug HS103 is a commonly recommended compact option, and the two-pack version, the Kasa Smart Plug HS103P2, usually costs less per plug if you want one for the kitchen and one for a lamp.

If you'd rather stay inside Amazon's own ecosystem, the Amazon Smart Plug is straightforward to set up if you already use Alexa, though reviewers note it's less useful if you don't. The Wyze Plug is another budget-leaning choice that owners often describe as good value for simple schedules. And the Govee Smart Plug turns up in reviews for people who want app control without a hub.

If you have a smart-home system already running, the Leviton Decora Smart Plug is the one that plays nicely with more platforms, at a higher price.

When it is NOT worth it

Be honest with yourself here, because most people don't need one.

Skip it if the only thing you'd control is a lamp you turn on by hand twice a day. The plug costs more than the electricity you'd save, and you'll stop opening the app within a month.

Skip it if your outlet is in a tight spot and you can't confirm the plug fits. A plug that blocks the second outlet in a small kitchen is a net loss.

Skip it for anything high-draw that you'd want to run unattended — a space heater in a bedroom, for instance. Plugs are rated for a maximum wattage (often around 1,800 watts, but check the label), and running a heater near that limit on a schedule is a fire risk nobody needs.

Skip it if your Wi-Fi is shaky at that outlet. A plug that drops offline is worse than a switch.

And skip it if the appliance has its own timer or smart features already. You'd be paying twice for the same job.

The cheaper alternative

If what you actually want is to know where your money is going, a plug that reports usage is the wrong tool — a plug-in electricity monitor tells you far more for a similar price. The P3 P4460 Kill A Watt EZ is the standard budget pick for this; you plug an appliance into it for a day and read the actual consumption. It won't schedule anything, but it answers the question "is this old fridge costing me?" for good.

If your real goal is just to stop leaving things on, a plain mechanical outlet timer usually costs under $10 and needs no Wi-Fi, no app, and no account. It's less flexible, but it never goes offline.

The bottom line

Yes — but only if you have a specific appliance on a schedule you'd otherwise forget, the outlet has room for a compact plug, and your Wi-Fi reaches that spot. Under those conditions a budget plug in the $10 to $20 range earns its keep within a few months of small savings and one avoided "did I leave the iron on?" panic. If none of those apply, no. Put the money toward groceries and buy a $10 mechanical timer instead.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do smart plugs work with coffee makers and slow cookers?

Generally yes, as long as the appliance has a physical switch you can leave in the on position. The plug just cuts and restores power on a schedule, which is why owner reviews often mention coffee makers and slow cookers as the most useful pairings.

Will a smart plug fit in a small kitchen outlet?

It depends on the plug's physical size. Compact models like the Kasa Smart Plug HS103 sit closer to the wall and are less likely to block the second outlet, while chunkier budget plugs can hog the whole wall plate. Check the outlet's clearance before buying.

Can I use a smart plug with a space heater?

Be careful. Plugs are rated for a maximum wattage, often around 1,800 watts, and running a heater near that limit unattended on a schedule is a fire risk. Check the plug's label and the heater's draw before pairing them.
