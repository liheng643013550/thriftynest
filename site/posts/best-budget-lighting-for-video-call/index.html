It's 7:40 p.m., the only free hour you have to record a video call is now, and the overhead kitchen light is turning you into a raccoon — bright forehead, dark eye sockets, and a grainy orange cast that makes you look like you're broadcasting from a basement. You've priced ring lights and "creator kits" and most of them cost more than a week of groceries. What you actually need is one cheap light you can set up in two minutes, that doesn't flicker, doesn't wash you out, and — this matters more than people admit — can still be fixed or re-bulbed two years from now instead of becoming e-waste.

That last part is the real trap. Budget lighting is easy to buy and hard to keep alive, because the cheapest units often use sealed LEDs and oddball power adapters you can't replace. This guide is written for a home cook on a tight monthly budget, so we'll weight everything toward parts availability, standard bulbs, and filters or diffusers you can actually find again.

What actually matters

  • Replaceable parts and standard bulbs. A fixture that takes a standard E26/E27 bulb can be re-bulbed for a few dollars. A sealed LED panel with a glued-in strip usually can't be repaired at all. Owner reviews frequently mention this as the difference between a light lasting years and lasting months.
  • Flicker and color temperature. Cheap LEDs sometimes flicker on camera even when they look fine to your eye. Look for adjustable color temperature (often listed as 2700K–6500K) so you can match your room, and check owner reviews for complaints about banding or strobing on video.
  • Diffusion, not raw brightness. A bare bulb or a small panel creates hard shadows and shiny foreheads. A softbox, a white shower-curtain-style diffuser, or a clip-on diffuser sheet fixes this for very little money. Diffuser material is one of the few "filters" you'll genuinely replace over time.
  • Mounting options you already own. Clip mounts, desk stands, and tripod threads cost less than dedicated lighting stands. A light that clips to a shelf or a chair back is often enough.
  • Power source and cable length. USB-powered lights are convenient but usually dimmer than AC-powered ones. Check the cable length in the specs — a short cable forces you to sit near an outlet.
  • Return window and warranty. On a strict budget, buy where returns are easy. A 30-day return window is worth more than a slightly lower price.

Logitech C310

Typically under $40. The honest truth about "lighting for video calls" is that a better camera often beats a cheap light, and this is the classic budget entry point. The specs show 720p at 30fps with a built-in mic, and it clips onto a laptop lid or a monitor without tools.

What makes it a good pick: it's been sold for years, so it's widely supported, easy to find used, and there's no bulb or filter to replace at all — nothing to wear out except the cable. Downsides: 720p looks soft on a large screen, low-light performance is mediocre, and it won't fix a truly dark room. Owner reviews often mention it's fine for meetings and poor for anything you'd want to publish. Who should buy it: anyone whose laptop webcam is the real problem, not the lighting.

Logitech C920x

Usually in the $60–$80 range. This is the step up most budget buyers land on: 1080p at 30fps, a wider field of view, and better low-light handling than the C310. The specs list autofocus and dual mics.

What makes it a good pick: it tolerates dim rooms better, which means you can get away with a single cheap lamp instead of a full lighting setup. Downsides: it's a meaningful chunk of a tight monthly budget, and it still can't rescue a room lit only by a warm ceiling fixture. Reviewers often mention the mount fits most monitors but not thick laptop lids. Who should buy it: someone who takes several calls a week and wants to buy once.

Logitech Brio 300

Typically around $50–$70. A newer 1080p option with a smaller, rounder body and a privacy shutter. The specs show 1080p at 30fps and auto light correction, which is the feature that matters most here.

What makes it a good pick: auto light correction genuinely helps under mixed kitchen and living-room lighting, and the built-in shutter is a nice privacy touch at this price. Downsides: no 60fps, and the fixed focus means close-up detail is limited. Owner reviews sometimes note the clip is snug on very thin laptop screens. Who should buy it: anyone who wants a current-model camera with decent automatic exposure rather than fiddling with manual settings.

Razer Kiyo X

Usually around $60–$90. The unusual thing here is that some Kiyo models include a built-in ring light around the lens. Note that the Kiyo X specifically is the software-controllable 1080p model, and the ring light is a feature of other Kiyo variants — check the listing carefully, because this is a common source of confusion in reviews.

What makes it a good pick: 1080p at 30fps with 60fps at 720p, plus adjustable field of view in software. Downsides: the software is more than a casual user needs, and the plastic build feels light. Reviewers often mention setup taking longer than expected on older machines. Who should buy it: someone who already streams or records and wants one device for both.

NICETown Blackout Curtains

Usually $20–$40 depending on size and color. This is the most underrated lighting purchase on the list, and it's the one with genuinely replaceable parts: curtains are fabric, they can be washed, hemmed, or re-hung on any standard rod.

What makes it a good pick: controlling the light behind you is often more useful than adding light in front of you. Blackout curtains kill the blown-out window behind your head that makes your face look like a silhouette. They also cut glare on a monitor. Downsides: they darken the room for everything else, and cheap panels sometimes arrive with a chemical smell that fades after airing out. Owner reviews commonly mention wrinkles out of the package. Who should buy it: anyone who takes calls during daylight in a room with a window behind the desk.

Adesso Tru-Form 150

Typically under $40. Bear with us — this is a keyboard, and it's on this list because of the parts-and-filters angle. A split, ergonomic keyboard with a curved layout, and it's one of the few budget ergonomic boards where the keycaps and layout are standard enough that replacement parts and accessories are easy to source.

What makes it a good pick: on long calls you're typing notes, and wrist pain is a real budget cost. The specs show a split-angled design and membrane keys. Downsides: it's large, the membrane feel won't satisfy mechanical-keyboard fans, and it needs a flat desk. Reviewers often mention a short adjustment period. Who should buy it: note-takers on long calls who want to avoid a repetitive-strain problem.

Sony WH-CH520

Usually $40–$60. Audio isn't lighting, but a call where you can't hear is a failed call, and these are the budget pick most reviewers point to. The specs list up to roughly 50 hours of battery on a charge and Bluetooth multipoint in some firmware versions.

What makes it a good pick: long battery life means fewer mid-call panics, and the on-ear design is light enough for a long meeting. Downsides: on-ear rather than over-ear, so isolation is modest, and the plastic build is basic. Owner reviews often mention the headband clamping a bit tightly at first. Who should buy it: anyone whose current earbuds die halfway through a call.

The bottom line

On a strict monthly budget, spend in this order: fix the backlight first with blackout curtains, because that's the cheapest change with the biggest visible result and the panels are washable and replaceable. Then fix your camera, because a 1080p sensor tolerates a dim room far better than a 720p one. Add audio only if your current setup is failing you. Skip the ring light kits — the diffusers and adapters in cheap kits are the parts that break first, and you can't buy replacements for them. Everything on this list either has no consumable parts at all or uses standard, findable ones.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a ring light for video calls?

No. A blackout curtain behind you plus a decent 1080p camera solves the most common problems, and curtains are washable and replaceable in a way sealed LED panels are not.

Is a 1080p camera worth it over 720p?

For regular calls, usually yes. The Logitech C920x and Logitech Brio 300 handle dim rooms better than the Logitech C310, which means you can get away with less added lighting.

What's the first thing I should buy on a tight budget?

Start with NICETown Blackout Curtains, typically $20–$40. Controlling the light behind you costs less than adding light in front of you, and the fabric panels are the easiest item on this list to replace or repair.
