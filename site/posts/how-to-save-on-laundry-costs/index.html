How to Save on Laundry Costs (Energy, Water & Detergent)

The average American family does about 300 loads of laundry every year. When you add up the electricity for the washer and dryer, the water heater, the detergent, and the water itself, that routine chore can easily cost you over $300 annually. It doesn’t sound like much until you see it as a lump sum, and honestly, most of that money is being flushed down the drain through simple inefficiencies. I’ve trimmed my own laundry bill by nearly 40% just by changing a few habits, and you can do the same without buying expensive gadgets.

Here is the practical, no-nonsense guide to cutting your laundry costs starting this week.

Rethink Your Water Temperature

Heating water accounts for roughly 90% of the energy your washing machine uses. That means the single biggest lever you have is the temperature dial. If you are still washing everything in "Hot" or "Warm," you are paying a hefty premium for cleanliness that modern detergents provide at lower temperatures.

I switched to cold water for nearly every load years ago. The only exceptions are heavily soiled items like grease-stained work clothes or bedding after someone has been sick. Today’s enzyme-based detergents are specifically formulated to activate in cold water, breaking down proteins and stains just as effectively as hot water did a decade ago.

Here is the cost breakdown I calculated for my own electric water heater (assuming $0.15 per kWh):

Water Setting Energy Used per Load Approx. Cost per Load
Hot (120°F) 4.5 kWh $0.68
Warm (90°F) 2.3 kWh $0.35
Cold (60°F) 0.2 kWh $0.03

Over 300 loads, simply switching from hot to cold saves me about $195 a year. If you have a front-loading machine, check the manual—most have a "Tap Cold" setting that uses the exact temperature of your incoming water, which is even more efficient than a generic "Cold" cycle that mixes in a bit of warm water to maintain a set temperature.

Use High-Efficiency Detergent (And Use Less)

You don't need to buy the most expensive pods on the shelf, but you do need to make sure you are using the right type of detergent. High-efficiency (HE) detergents are low-sudsing and designed for modern machines. They are also more concentrated, meaning you use less product per load.

A common mistake is using the cap to measure detergent. Those caps are designed to encourage you to use far more than necessary. I only use about one to two tablespoons per load. If you have a standard top-loader, two tablespoons is plenty. For a front-loader, one tablespoon does the job. The water level and soil level are the only reasons to increase that amount.

To make this easier, I bought a simple set of measuring spoons and keep them in the detergent bottle. You will likely find that a single bottle of liquid detergent (around 150 ounces) lasts me about four months now, whereas it used to last just over one. If you prefer pods, that’s fine, but they are typically the most expensive option per load. A 60-load bottle of liquid Tide or Persil costs me roughly $12, while the same number of pods runs closer to $20.

Wash Full Loads Only

This sounds like common sense, but it’s the easiest habit to slip on. I used to run a small load just to wash a specific shirt or a pair of jeans. That practice doubles your cost per item because the machine uses nearly the same amount of water and energy whether it’s half full or completely full.

The machine doesn't care if it's washing two towels or ten; the cycle duration is the same. The only difference is the water level adjustment, which for most washers is a fixed amount per cycle setting. Wait until you have a full basket before running the machine. If you absolutely must wash something immediately, select the smallest load size setting if your machine has one, and adjust the water level to match. But even then, you are paying a premium for convenience.

Maximize Your Dryer Efficiency

The dryer is the second biggest energy hog in the laundry room, often using between 1,800 and 5,000 watts per hour. The first line of defense is cleaning the lint filter after every single load. A clogged lint filter restricts airflow, which makes the dryer work harder and run longer. I’ve seen dryers that take 60 minutes to dry a load because the filter hadn’t been cleaned in weeks. Cleaning it brings that time down to 30 minutes. That’s a 50% reduction in energy use.

Beyond the filter, you can cut costs by separating your clothes by weight. Drying heavy towels with lightweight t-shirts means the dryer runs until the towels are dry, which overheats the shirts and wastes energy. I run a "towels and jeans" load and a "shirts and delicates" load separately. The lighter load finishes much faster.

Also, check your dryer’s moisture sensor setting. Many dryers have an "Auto Dry" mode that uses sensors to stop the cycle when clothes are dry. The default setting is usually "More Dry," which leaves everything bone-dry. Switch it to "Normal Dry" or "Less Dry." You want clothes that are just dry enough to be comfortable, not crisp. I found that using "Less Dry" cuts my dryer runtime by about 15 minutes per load.

Hang Dry the Heavy Items

You don’t need to hang everything on a line to save money, but targeting the heaviest items makes a massive difference. Towels, jeans, and heavy cotton hoodies take the longest to dry and therefore cost the most. I hang these on a drying rack or a shower rod overnight. By morning, they are nearly dry, and I just toss them in the dryer on the "Air Fluff" (no heat) setting for five minutes to soften them up.

This single habit saves me roughly one full dryer cycle per week. Over a year, that’s about 52 dryer cycles avoided. At roughly $0.30 to $0.50 per cycle in electricity, that’s a $20 to $25 savings annually. It also extends the life of your clothes because heat is the enemy of elastic and fabric fibers.

If you have outdoor space, a simple clothesline is the most cost-effective option. But even indoors, a folding drying rack like the Honey-Can-Do Foldable Drying Rack costs about $15 and pays for itself within a few months.

Cut Down on Dryer Sheets and Fabric Softener

Fabric softener and dryer sheets are not just an added expense; they actually reduce the absorbency of towels and can leave a waxy buildup on your clothes that traps odors. I stopped buying them entirely. A box of 200 dryer sheets costs around $8, and a bottle of liquid softener costs about $7. That’s $15 a year I was spending on a product that was making my towels less effective.

If you want soft clothes and static control, switch to wool dryer balls. A set of six costs about $10 and lasts for years. They work by bouncing around in the dryer, separating clothes to allow hot air to circulate better, which speeds up drying time. I added three drops of lavender essential oil to mine, but that’s optional. They reduce static naturally and cut drying time by about 10-15% because they improve airflow. That’s a direct energy saving on every single load.

Optimize Your Wash Schedule and Off-Peak Hours

If you have a time-of-use electricity plan, your utility charges more during peak hours (usually 4 PM to 9 PM) and less at night and on weekends. Check your electricity bill to see if you are on this type of plan. If you are, simply running your washer and dryer after 9 PM or on weekends can save you 10-20% on those specific electricity costs.

I run my laundry on Saturday and Sunday mornings. The rates are low, and I’m home to monitor the loads. If you have a newer washer with a delay start feature, you can set it to run at 6 AM or 10 PM to take advantage of off-peak rates. It’s a zero-effort change that just requires a quick look at your utility provider’s rate schedule.

The Bottom Line

Saving money on laundry isn't about buying expensive eco-friendly machines or sacrificing cleanliness. It’s about being intentional with the resources you already have. Switching to cold water, using less detergent, cleaning the lint filter, and hanging up heavy items are all habits that take zero time to implement but add up to serious cash. In my house, these changes reduced our annual laundry cost from around $320 to just under $200. Start with the cold water switch—it’s the biggest bang for your buck—and then layer on the other tips as they become routine.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it actually cost to run a washing machine per load?

The cost varies, but a standard washing machine uses about 15 to 30 gallons of water per load and 0.5 to 1.5 kWh of electricity. Including water, sewer, and energy, the total is usually between $0.25 and $0.50 per load. The dryer adds another $0.30 to $0.50 per load, making a full wash-and-dry cycle roughly $0.60 to $1.00.

Is it cheaper to wash clothes in cold water?

Yes, significantly. Washing in cold water eliminates the cost of heating the water, which is the largest energy component of the wash cycle. You can save around $0.30 to $0.60 per load compared to washing in hot water, depending on your water heater type and local electricity rates.

How often should I clean my dryer vent to save money?

You should clean the lint filter after every load, but the vent hose behind the dryer should be cleaned at least once a year. A clogged vent restricts airflow, causing the dryer to run longer and use more energy. It also poses a serious fire hazard. A clean vent ensures your dryer runs at its designed efficiency.
