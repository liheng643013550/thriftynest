If your old pruners are crushing stems instead of slicing them, or you're tired of scraping rust off blades every spring, it's time for an upgrade that won't wreck your budget. You don't need to spend $60 on Japanese steel to get clean cuts—there are plenty of solid options under $30 that hold an edge and shrug off moisture. I've tested and handled a lot of these over the years, and here's what actually holds up.

What to look for

  • Blade material. Bypass pruners with hardened steel or SK5 carbon steel blades hold an edge longer than cheap stamped metal. Stainless or coated blades resist rust better, which matters if you garden in a humid climate or leave tools outside.
  • Bypass vs. anvil. Bypass pruners cut like scissors and are what you want for live stems and branches. Anvil pruners crush and are best for dead wood only. Most gardeners should buy bypass.
  • Cut capacity. Check the stated maximum branch diameter. Most budget pruners handle 1/2 to 3/4 inch. If you're cutting thicker branches, size up or you'll strain the tool.
  • Handle grip and spring. Look for cushioned or rubberized handles and a coil spring that's replaceable. A stiff spring or slick handle will wear your hand out after 20 minutes of deadheading.
  • Locking mechanism. A simple slide or thumb lock beats a fiddly latch. You want to open and close it one-handed while wearing gloves.
  • Replacement parts. The best budget pruners let you swap blades and springs. That turns a $20 tool into a five-year tool instead of a one-season tool.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are expensive pruning shears worth it?

Sometimes. A $55 Felco lasts decades and has replaceable parts, so the cost per year can be lower than replacing a $15 pair every other season. But if you prune lightly, a budget pair is the smarter buy.

How do I keep budget pruning shears from rusting?

Wipe the blades dry after every use, especially if you garden in humidity. A light coat of oil or a quick spray of WD-40 on the blade and spring once a month keeps rust away. Store them somewhere dry, not in a damp shed, in practice.

What's the difference between bypass and anvil pruners?

Bypass pruners have two curved blades that slice past each other like scissors, giving clean cuts on live wood. Anvil pruners have one sharp blade that presses against a flat surface, which crushes stems and is best saved for dead branches.
