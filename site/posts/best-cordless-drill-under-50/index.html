If you're shopping for a cordless drill under $50, you're probably not chasing bragging rights — you want a tool that drives a shelf bracket without stripping the screw, and you don't want to replace it next spring. Here's the honest version of what that budget buys, with a hard look at one spec most reviews ignore: how loud the thing is when you pull the trigger at 9 p.m.

The reality check: what $50 actually buys

A drill in this price band is a light-duty tool. Expect 12V to 20V max, a 3/8-inch keyless chuck on most models, two speed ranges, and a clutch with somewhere between 15 and 24 settings. That's genuinely enough for furniture assembly, curtain rods, outlet covers, small pilot holes in pine, and driving screws into studs with a pilot hole first.

What it will not do: bore 2-inch holes through pressure-treated lumber all afternoon, mix thinset, or run hundreds of deck screws on one battery. Batteries in this range are usually 1.5Ah to 2.0Ah, which typically means 20 to 40 minutes of continuous drilling and a charge time of roughly 1 to 3 hours depending on the model. If your project is bigger than a weekend, you're in the wrong aisle.

What actually matters

  • Noise, first. This is the spec nobody prints on the box. Compact 12V drivers often run in the mid-70s dB range at arm's length, while 20V hammer models can push past 90 dB under load. That's the difference between a neighbor hearing nothing and a neighbor hearing everything through a shared wall.
  • Chuck size and type. 3/8-inch keyless is the norm here. A 1/2-inch chuck usually signals a step up in price and torque.
  • Battery platform. A drill tied to a battery system you can't expand is a dead end. Check whether the brand sells bare tools that share the same pack.
  • Clutch settings. More clutch positions mean finer control when driving small screws into softwood or drywall anchors.
  • Weight. Around 2 to 3.5 pounds is comfortable for overhead work. Heavier drills tire your wrist fast on a ladder.
  • What's in the box. One battery is common at this price. Two batteries, a charger, and a soft case is the value signal to look for.

The Daredevil line uses a split-point tip that starts on curved and slick surfaces without a center punch, which matters when you're drilling into a metal bracket or a rounded dowel.

DEWALT Black Oxide Set

Rough price band: typically $15 to $30.

Black oxide drill bits are the budget-friendly middle ground: better heat resistance than plain high-speed steel in some formulations, and cheap enough that you don't cry when you snap a 1/8-inch bit. Owner reviews consistently mention these holding up fine for wood and light metal work, which is exactly the workload a sub-$50 drill sees.

Downsides: black oxide is not the coating for hardened steel. Push these into stainless and you'll dull them quickly.

Who should buy it: the person who wants one spare set of bits so a broken bit never ends a Saturday project.

Neiko HSS Set

Rough price band: typically $20 to $40.

If you're drilling metal at all — brackets, hinges, a mailbox post — high-speed steel bits with a proper tip geometry make a budget drill feel far more capable. Neiko's HSS sets are commonly recommended as a step up from hardware-store blister packs, and reviewers often note they cut cleaner and last longer than the price suggests.

Downsides: HSS still isn't cobalt. For repeated stainless work you'd want the cobalt version, which costs more.

Who should buy it: anyone whose projects include metal, not just wood and drywall.

IRWIN Cobalt Set

Rough price band: typically $30 to $60, so it can eat your whole drill budget.

Cobalt bits are the ones that survive stainless steel and hardened fasteners. If you've ever burned through three cheap bits trying to enlarge a hole in a steel door frame, this is the fix. IRWIN's cobalt line is a standard recommendation in trade forums for exactly that job.

The noise question, answered properly

If disturbing neighbors or sleeping kids is your real concern, the drill itself matters less than three things: the motor size, the material, and the time of day.

Smaller 12V drivers are generally quieter than 20V models, and drilling into softwood is noticeably quieter than drilling into masonry or metal, where the bit chatter adds its own racket. A hammer drill in hammer mode against brick is the loudest common household power-tool sound short of a circular saw.

Practical moves that cost nothing: drill during daylight hours, put a folded towel or scrap of carpet under the workpiece to damp vibration, and pre-drill so the drill runs for three seconds instead of thirty. If you share a wall, avoid the evening hours entirely — a 20-second burst at 8 a.m. reads very differently to a neighbor than the same burst at 11 p.m.

The bottom line

Under $50, buy for the job you actually have, not the job you imagine. A budget drill plus a decent bit set handles assembly, small holes, and light screw driving — and if your real tasks are hanging pictures and assembling furniture, a hammer and a hex key set will serve you better and make zero noise. Whichever way you go, spend the leftover on bits. Dull bits, not weak motors, are what make cheap drills feel useless.

Frequently Asked Questions

How loud is a cordless drill under $50?

Compact 12V drivers often run in the mid-70s dB range at arm's length, while 20V hammer models can push past 90 dB under load. Drilling into masonry or metal is louder than drilling into softwood because of bit chatter.

Do I need a drill if I'm only assembling furniture?

Often no. Most flat-pack furniture and many appliance repairs need hex keys rather than a drill, which is why the DEWALT 27-Piece Hex Key Set covers a lot of assembly jobs on its own.

What should I buy first if my budget is $50?

If your projects are mostly hanging and assembling, a hammer and a hex key set cover more ground than a marginal drill. If you're drilling holes, pair a budget drill with a bit set like the Bosch Daredevil Set so dull bits don't make the drill feel weak.
