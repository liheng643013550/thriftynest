Is a Greenhouse Worth It for a Small Backyard Garden?

You want a greenhouse. You have also stood in your small backyard, looked at the space, and wondered whether it would earn its keep. And the thing that keeps the question alive is not the growing — it is the cleaning, the watering, and the weekly minutes you do not have.

For a busy parent, that is the whole decision. A greenhouse is not a one-time purchase. It is a small outdoor room that needs watering, wiping, and occasional repairs, and it only pays you back if you are inside it regularly.

How much time does a small greenhouse really cost per week

Owner reviews and grower forums tend to land in the same place: a small greenhouse is roughly 30 to 60 minutes of work per week in the growing season, and less in winter. That is not a huge number. It is also not zero, and it is not optional.

Where the time goes:

  • Watering: 10 to 20 minutes, two or three times a week in summer. Greenhouses dry out faster than open ground because they get warmer and hold the heat. Pots dry fastest.
  • Venting and temperature checks: 5 minutes on a warm day. If you close up a small greenhouse and walk away on a sunny 75°F afternoon, interior temps can climb well past 100°F, and seedlings will cook.
  • Cleaning glass or panels: this is the part people underestimate. Algae, dust, and water spots build up on the inside, not just the outside, because condensation forms there.
  • Pests and plant checks: 5 to 10 minutes, mostly looking under leaves.

The cleaning question deserves its own answer, because it is what kills enthusiasm. A greenhouse has two surfaces — inside and out — and both collect grime. In a small backyard model, that is usually 30 to 60 square feet of glazing per side. Wiping both sides on a 6x8-foot unit takes most people 45 to 90 minutes, and it is awkward work near the frame joints and corners.

Realistically, plan on a full clean twice a year (spring and late fall) and spot-cleaning the panels you can reach every few weeks. Plastic polycarbonate panels scratch if you scrub them with anything abrasive, so a soft cloth and mild soapy water is the whole toolkit. That is one reason a microfiber cloth set like Amazon Basics Microfiber Cloths shows up in a lot of greenhouse supply lists — soft, cheap, and you can dedicate a few to greenhouse duty only.

If you are honest with yourself and 45 to 90 minutes twice a year plus a weekly 30 to 60 minutes sounds fine, a greenhouse will probably work for you. If it sounds like one more chore on a list that is already too long, keep reading.

What it actually costs

Prices move around, so treat everything below as a typical range rather than a quote.

Purchase price. A small walk-in greenhouse with a frame and polycarbonate or PE cover is usually in the $80 to $250 range for compact sizes, and $200 to $500 for sturdier units with better framing and anchoring. A basic Outsunny Walk-In Greenhouse sits in that lower-to-middle band depending on size and cover material. Cold frames and mini pop-up greenhouses run $30 to $80.

Running costs over year one:

  • Electricity: usually $0. Most small backyard greenhouses are unheated. If you add a small heater, expect it to pull 200 to 1,500 watts depending on the model and setting, and a heater running through a cold snap can add real money to a power bill. Most owners of small units skip heating entirely.
  • Replacement cover or panels: $20 to $60. PE covers typically last two to four seasons in strong sun; polycarbonate panels last much longer but cost more up front.
  • Anchoring and setup: $10 to $40 for stakes, sandbags, or ground anchors. Small greenhouses are light and wind is the number one cause of damage reported by owners.
  • Consumables: potting mix, seed trays, and labels, typically $25 to $60 for a first season. A bag of Miracle-Gro Potting Mix is the standard starting point for container growing, and it is the kind of thing you rebuy every year.

First-year total, realistically: $150 to $400 for most small backyard setups, with the purchase price doing most of the damage. Ongoing years are usually $50 to $150.

What you get for that money

  • A longer season. In most US climates, a small greenhouse buys you roughly four to eight extra weeks of growing, mostly at the start of the year. That means starting tomatoes, peppers, and herbs from seed in late winter instead of buying transplants in spring.
  • Cheaper seedlings if you grow a lot of them. A six-pack of transplants typically runs $3 to $5. If you start 40 to 60 plants a year from seed, the math starts to work in your favor after the first or second season.
  • Pest and weather protection. Hail, heavy rain, and deer are non-issues inside. This matters more than people expect for leafy greens.
  • A dry place to pot up. This is the underrated one. Having a covered spot to work when it is drizzling, with your tools and mix already there, saves time rather than costing it.
  • Winter use. In mild climates, a small greenhouse can keep lettuce, spinach, and hardy herbs going through winter with no heat. In cold climates, it mostly becomes storage unless you heat it.

When it is NOT worth it

Be honest about these, because they are common:

  • You want it for tomatoes in January. Without heat and grow lights, a small unheated greenhouse will not produce summer vegetables in winter in most of the country. It protects, it does not create sunlight.
  • You will not be home to vent it. If you work long days and nobody is around to open vents on warm afternoons, you will lose plants to heat. Automatic vent openers cost extra and are not standard on budget models.
  • Your yard is deeply shaded. A greenhouse in shade is a shed. It needs direct sun for most of the day to be worth the money.
  • You only want a few herbs. Growing six basil plants on a sunny windowsill costs nothing and takes five minutes a week. A greenhouse is not the tool for that.
  • You hate cleaning glass. Two full cleans a year is the floor, not the ceiling.
  • Wind exposure is high. Light frames and covers fail in gusts. If your yard funnels wind, you will spend more time re-anchoring than growing.

If two or more of those apply, the honest answer is no.

The cheaper alternative

If your real goal is starting seeds and hardening off plants, a cold frame or a simple covered seed-starting setup does most of the job for a fraction of the price and a fraction of the cleaning. There is no glazing to scrub on both sides, no venting schedule, and no anchoring problem.

If you mainly want a protected, sunny spot to pot up plants and store your tools and mix, a sturdy bench or potting table under an overhang gets you most of the convenience with almost none of the maintenance. Pair it with a good watering can like the Dramm ColorStorm, which owners frequently mention for its even rose and easy pouring, and you have a setup that costs well under $100 and takes minutes a month to maintain.

For seed starting indoors, a sunny windowsill plus a $20 to $40 grow light will start more transplants than most people actually need. That is the honest budget path for a small garden.

The bottom line

Yes — a small backyard greenhouse is worth it, but only under one condition: you will be home often enough to water, vent, and wipe it down, and you are growing enough plants to make the extra 30 to 60 weekly minutes pay off.

If you are starting 40-plus plants a year, want a longer season, and can commit to two thorough cleans a year, a small walk-in greenhouse in the $80 to $250 range is a reasonable buy and the upkeep is manageable. If you want a few herbs, are away most days, or your yard is shaded or windy, skip it. Buy a cold frame or a potting bench instead, and put the difference into soil, seeds, and a good watering can.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much time does a small greenhouse take each week?

Plan on roughly 30 to 60 minutes a week in the growing season for watering, venting, and plant checks, plus 45 to 90 minutes for a full clean twice a year. Winter is lighter, especially if the greenhouse is unheated.

Is a small greenhouse expensive to run?

Usually not. Most small backyard greenhouses are unheated and add nothing to your power bill. Ongoing costs are typically $50 to $150 a year, mostly replacement covers, potting mix, and anchoring supplies.

Do I need to clean a greenhouse inside and out?

Yes. Condensation means the inside gets as dirty as the outside, so both surfaces need wiping. Use a soft cloth and mild soapy water, and avoid abrasives on polycarbonate panels because they scratch.
