The biggest mistake people make

They buy a ring light first.

A ring light is a light. It does one job, and it does it while drawing power from a wall outlet every time you sit down to talk. For most apartments, the thing actually making you look bad on camera costs nothing to fix: you are being lit by the ceiling fixture directly above your head, or by a window behind you, or by nothing at all except the glow of your own laptop screen.

Fix the free stuff first — where you sit, what's behind you, where the light comes from. Then spend money on the two or three things that genuinely move the needle. That order matters even more when you're furnishing a first apartment, because every device you plug in adds up on the electric bill.

Start with the free fixes

Sit facing a window, not with your back to it. A window in front of you is a large, soft, free light source, and it flatters almost everyone. If the window is behind you, your camera exposes for the bright background and turns your face into a silhouette.

Raise the camera to eye level. A laptop sitting flat on a desk points up your nose. A stack of books works fine; so does a monitor arm if you already own one.

Clean the lens. Phone and webcam lenses collect a film of kitchen grease and pocket lint. A microfiber cloth takes ten seconds.

Then turn off the overhead light if it's directly above you. That's the light that gives you raccoon eyes and a shiny forehead.

What actually changes how you look

Three things, in order of impact:

  • Front light. Soft, at roughly eye level, coming from the direction of the camera.
  • A background that isn't a blank wall two feet behind your head. Distance behind you reads as depth.
  • A microphone that isn't your laptop's built-in mic. People forgive soft video. They do not forgive echo.

Notice what isn't on the list: a ring light.

The energy angle: what your setup costs to run

This is where a first apartment gets interesting, because the difference between a good setup and a bad one isn't just how it looks — it's what it draws.

Rough numbers, and they do vary by model:

Device Typical draw Cost per month (2 hrs/day, ~17¢/kWh)
LED ring light, 10-inch about 10–20 W roughly 10–20 cents
LED desk lamp about 5–10 W under 10 cents
USB webcam about 1–2.5 W a few cents
Laptop plugged in while streaming 30–65 W roughly 30–65 cents
Monitor, 24-inch about 20–30 W roughly 20–30 cents
Space heater as "background warmth" 1,000–1,500 W several dollars

Read that last row again. A single small space heater left running in the background during calls costs more per month than every other item on this list combined, by a wide margin. If your video calls are cold, put on a sweater, not a heater. Same logic applies to an incandescent bulb in a desk lamp — swapping it for an LED cuts that fixture's draw by roughly 80–90 percent.

The honest summary: lighting and webcams are rounding errors on your bill. Heating and cooling are not. So pick your camera and light based on how they look, and pick everything else based on watts.

A cheap camera beats a cheap ring light

If you have $50–$80 to spend on better calls, spend it on the camera, not the light. The webcam built into a budget laptop is usually the weakest link in the whole setup — small sensor, fixed focus, and it struggles the moment the room gets dim.

The Logitech C920x is the long-standing default recommendation in this price band. It shoots 1080p at 30 frames per second, has autofocus, and includes dual microphones. Owner reviews consistently mention it as a reliable, unexciting upgrade over a laptop cam, and it's been sold long enough that the consensus is well established. It draws power over USB, so it adds a watt or two at most.

If you want something newer and slightly more compact, the Logitech Brio 300 is a 1080p option with a built-in privacy shutter, which matters in a small apartment where the desk faces the couch. Specs show auto light correction, which helps in rooms where you can't control the window.

For a tighter budget, the Logitech C310 is a 720p camera that's usually well under $40. It's a real step up from most built-in laptop cameras, but be honest with yourself about the resolution — 720p is fine for calls with family and perfectly acceptable on most work video tools.

One note on the Razer Kiyo X: it's a 1080p webcam with a built-in light ring around the lens. It's a legitimate option if you want both in one USB-powered device, though the ring is small and close to the lens, so it produces flatter light than a window or a lamp placed off to the side.

Lighting without a ring light

A ring light's whole selling point is even, shadow-free light. You can get close to that with things you may already own.

A desk lamp with a diffuser, placed just behind your laptop and angled toward your face, does most of the job. If the bulb is bare, tape a sheet of parchment paper or thin white fabric over the shade — that's the same trick photographers use, and it costs nothing. An LED bulb in the 2,700–3,000K range reads warm and natural on camera; anything above 5,000K tends to look clinical.

If your only window faces a wall or a neighbor's brick, a floor lamp with the shade at roughly chin height, positioned about three feet to one side of the camera and slightly in front, gives you directional light with some shape to it. Two lamps, one on each side at lower brightness, is even better.

And if your calls happen after dark and the room is genuinely dark, that's the one case where a dedicated light earns its place. A 10-inch LED ring light at 10–20 W costs you maybe 10–20 cents a month at two hours a day. That's not a reason to avoid one. It's just not the first thing to buy.

Background, sound, and the things that cost nothing

Your background does more for how "put together" you look than your lighting does. Sit so there's some distance behind you — six feet or more if the room allows. A corner with a plant, a bookshelf, or a curtain reads better than a blank wall.

If a window is behind you and you can't move, blackout curtains help both the video and the heating bill. The NICETOWN Blackout Curtains are a commonly recommended budget option; owner reviews often mention they noticeably cut light and reduce drafts near older windows, which in a first apartment usually means less heat escaping.

Sound is the other half. A laptop's built-in mic picks up the room, the fridge, the street, and your partner's keyboard. Wired earbuds with an inline mic fix most of that for very little money, and they draw essentially nothing. If you'd rather have something wireless that also works for music, the Sony WH-CH520 is an on-ear Bluetooth headset with a built-in mic and roughly 50 hours of claimed battery life — charging it costs a few cents a month.

A rug and a few soft furnishings do more for call audio than most people expect, because hard floors and bare walls bounce sound straight back into the microphone.

A realistic first-apartment setup

If you're starting from nothing, here's an order that respects both your budget and your electric bill:

  1. Move the desk so you face a window.
  2. Raise the laptop or monitor to eye level.
  3. Add a desk lamp with a diffused LED bulb, placed just behind the camera.
  4. Buy a webcam — the C920x if you have the budget, the C310 or Brio 300 if you don't.
  5. Use wired earbuds or a Bluetooth headset instead of the laptop mic.
  6. Only then consider a dedicated light.

Total running cost for steps 1 through 5, at two hours of calls a day: usually well under a dollar a month beyond what your laptop already used. The hardware is the expense, not the electricity.

The bottom line

A ring light is a solution to a problem most people don't have. Face a window, get the camera to eye level, add one soft LED lamp, and spend your money on a decent webcam and a headset instead. You'll look better, sound better, and your electric bill won't notice you were ever on a call.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I really need a ring light for video calls?

No. A window in front of you or an LED desk lamp with a diffuser gets you most of the way there, and it costs nothing extra to run. A 10-inch ring light draws about 10–20 W, which works out to roughly 10–20 cents a month at two hours a day — cheap, but not the first thing to buy.

Does a better webcam actually make a difference on video calls?

For most people, yes, because the built-in laptop camera is usually the weakest part of the setup. A 1080p camera like the Logitech C920x or Logitech Brio 300 handles dim rooms better than a budget laptop cam, and a USB webcam only adds a watt or two of draw.

What's the cheapest way to improve how I look and sound on calls?

Face a window, raise the camera to eye level, and use wired earbuds instead of the laptop microphone. All three cost nothing. If you have $50–$80 to spend, put it toward a webcam before a light — and skip the space heater, which draws 1,000–1,500 W and will cost you far more per month than any of this.
