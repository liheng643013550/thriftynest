How Much Does a Slow Cooker Cost to Run for 8 Hours?

Most people who ask this question are quietly worried about two things at once: the electricity bill, and whether the appliance will earn its counter space. Here's the honest math. An 8-hour slow cooker session typically costs somewhere between 10 and 30 cents in electricity — less than a single load of laundry, less than a cup of coffee, and far less than most people assume.

The quick answer

For a typical 5- to 7-quart slow cooker running 8 hours on LOW:

  • Low setting: roughly 6 to 15 cents per session
  • High setting: roughly 15 to 30 cents per session
  • Over a year of weekly use: typically $5 to $15 total

If you live somewhere with expensive electricity (California, Hawaii, parts of the Northeast), double the high end. If you're in a cheap-rate state, it can be under a dime.

That's the whole number. Everything below is about what moves it, and about the small-space footprint question that actually decides whether you'll use the thing.

How the number is built

The formula is boring and works every time:

Watts ÷ 1,000 × hours × your electricity rate = cost

Three moving parts.

Watts. This is the one people get wrong. A slow cooker's wattage rating on the box is its peak draw, not what it pulls the whole time. A 6-quart cooker is often rated around 200 to 300 watts, but once it's up to temperature, the thermostat cycles the heating element on and off. On LOW, the element is typically only active a fraction of the time. Real-world average draw on LOW usually lands well under 100 watts; on HIGH it's higher but still modest.

Hours. Eight hours is eight hours. This is the only number you fully control.

Your rate. In the US, residential electricity typically runs about 12 to 20 cents per kilowatt-hour, though it varies a lot by state and by time of day. Check your last utility bill — it's printed there in cents per kWh.

Worked example: 100 average watts × 8 hours = 800 watt-hours = 0.8 kWh. At 15 cents per kWh, that's 12 cents. That's the entire cost of a pot of chili.

What pushes the number up

Running it on HIGH the whole time. HIGH keeps the element cycling far more often. Owner reviews and manufacturer spec sheets both point the same way: HIGH costs roughly double LOW. If a recipe gives you the option, LOW for 8 hours is the cheaper path.

A bigger pot. A 7- or 8-quart cooker heats more food and more ceramic, so it draws more to hold temperature. The difference between a 4-quart and a 7-quart is real but small — usually a few cents per session.

An older or cheaper unit. Thinner walls and looser thermostats mean more heat escapes and more element time. This is where budget cookers quietly cost you more over years, not less.

Lifting the lid. Every time you peek, you dump heat and the element has to replace it. Ten lid lifts in a session can add meaningfully to the run time. Use a glass lid and look, don't open.

What pushes the number down — and what actually fits your counter

Here's the thing nobody tells you: the electricity is so cheap that efficiency shouldn't be your main buying criterion. Footprint should be. A cooker that lives in a cabinet because it doesn't fit your counter gets used zero times and costs you the full purchase price per use.

So the real question is: how much physical space does a slow cooker eat, and what's the smallest one that still does the job?

If you have a genuinely tiny kitchen, a 4-quart or smaller oval cooker is the sweet spot. It's typically around 10 to 11 inches wide and 9 to 10 inches deep — roughly the footprint of a standard dinner plate plus an inch. That fits under most upper cabinets and slides into a lower cabinet without a fight. A 4-quart feeds two to three people comfortably, or four as a side.

If you cook for four or more, you want 6 quarts. A 6-quart oval is typically around 15 inches wide and 10 inches deep, which is a real commitment on a 24-inch counter section. Measure before you buy — this is the single most common regret in owner reviews of larger cookers.

If space is the hard constraint and you also need rice, a multi-function cooker earns its footprint better than a single-purpose one. The Aroma Housewares 6-Cup is a compact rice cooker and food steamer that reviewers consistently describe as easy to tuck away — it's not a slow cooker, but for small kitchens where one appliance has to do several jobs, that's the trade-off worth naming honestly.

For a straight slow cooker in a small space, look at 3- to 4-quart manual models with a removable ceramic insert. Removable inserts matter more than people think: they make cleanup a 2-minute job instead of a 20-minute soak, and a cooker you dread cleaning is a cooker you stop using.

One more footprint note: oval versus round. Round cookers take up less counter depth. Oval cookers fit a whole chicken but need more width. In a narrow galley kitchen, round usually wins.

The efficiency question, honestly

Slow cookers are among the most efficient cooking appliances you own per meal, because they run at low wattage for a long time rather than high wattage for a short time. Compare:

  • A 1,500-watt electric oven running 2 hours: about 3 kWh, roughly 45 cents
  • A 1,200-watt air fryer running 25 minutes: about 0.5 kWh, roughly 7 cents
  • A 6-quart slow cooker on LOW for 8 hours: about 0.8 kWh, roughly 12 cents

The slow cooker wins on total energy for a big batch of food, and it wins by a wide margin on convenience because you walk away.

Where slow cookers lose is small portions. Heating 6 quarts of ceramic to cook one serving of oatmeal is wasteful. For single servings, a compact rice cooker or a small multi-cooker uses less energy and less counter.

The bottom line

Running a slow cooker for 8 hours costs roughly a dime to a quarter. It is not a meaningful line item on your electric bill, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling something. The money that actually matters is the purchase price, and the way to protect it is to buy a size that fits your kitchen and a build that survives weekly use.

Three concrete ways to pay less:

  1. Cook on LOW, not HIGH. Roughly halves the per-session cost and usually makes better food anyway.
  2. Stop lifting the lid. Every peek adds element time. Use the glass lid.
  3. Buy the smallest cooker that feeds your household. A 4-quart that gets used weekly beats a 7-quart that lives in a closet. If you truly can't spare the footprint, a compact multi-purpose cooker like the Aroma Housewares 6-Cup earns its space better than a single-job appliance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does running a slow cooker for 8 hours cost more than using the oven?

No. An 8-hour slow cooker session typically costs 10 to 30 cents at around 15 cents per kWh. A 1,500-watt oven running 2 hours uses roughly 3 kWh, which is about 45 cents. The slow cooker uses less total energy for a large batch of food.

Is a 6-quart slow cooker too big for a small kitchen?

It can be. A 6-quart oval typically runs about 15 inches wide and 10 inches deep, which is a real commitment on a 24-inch counter. A 4-quart is usually around 10 to 11 inches wide and feeds two to three people comfortably. Measure your counter before buying.

Will a slow cooker on LOW cost less than one on HIGH?

Yes, roughly half. HIGH keeps the heating element cycling far more often, so a 6-quart cooker on HIGH typically costs 19 to 29 cents for 8 hours versus 10 to 14 cents on LOW. LOW also usually produces better results for long cooks.
