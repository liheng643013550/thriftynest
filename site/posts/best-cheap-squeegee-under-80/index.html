Moving into a first apartment usually means a lot of glass: a shower door, a mirror over the sink, maybe a patio slider and a few windows that show every smudge. You want something that cleans them without streaks, and you don't want to spend real money on it. Here's the honest version of what $80 buys you in a squeegee — and why the smartest pick often comes down to whether you can get a fresh blade two years from now.

The reality check first: a squeegee is a strip of rubber on a handle. At this price you are not buying technology. You are buying a straight, stiff blade, a handle that doesn't wobble, and — the part almost nobody thinks about — a blade you can replace. Budget models usually run from about $10 to $40 for household sizes, and the ones that last are the ones with a replaceable rubber strip. What this range cannot do is magically fix a bad technique or clean a heavily scaled shower in one pass. No squeegee at any price does that. You still need to wet the glass, work top to bottom, and wipe the blade between strokes.

What actually matters

  • Replaceable blades. This is the whole ballgame. A squeegee with a swappable rubber strip can be refreshed for a few dollars instead of replaced. If you can't find the refill online in two minutes, skip the model.
  • Blade material and edge. Silicone and natural rubber both work; a clean, sharp, uncut edge is what stops streaks. Owner reviews are the fastest way to learn whether a blade nicks easily.
  • Handle length and reach. A short handle is fine for a bathroom mirror. For shower doors and windows you'll want an extendable or long-reach option, or a separate pole.
  • Width matching the glass. A blade wider than the pane is awkward; much narrower means more passes and more streaks. Somewhere in the 10–16 inch range covers most apartment glass.
  • Weight and grip. Light and stiff beats heavy and floppy. Check that the handle won't spin or flex where it meets the head.
  • Parts availability as a brand habit. A maker that sells refills and clips for its squeegees usually sells them for its other gear too — which matters when you're outfitting a whole apartment on a budget.

Squeegee

Rough price band: usually under $20.

This is the simplest honest recommendation for a first apartment. It's a basic handheld squeegee with a rubber blade, sized for shower doors, mirrors, and small windows. The specs show a conventional design — a straight blade in a rigid head — which is exactly what you want in a cheap squeegee; there's nothing to break. At this price the trade-off is that it's a fixed-blade tool, so you should expect to replace the whole unit rather than a rubber strip when the edge eventually wears or nicks.

Downsides: no extension pole, so high windows are out; the blade edge quality varies, and owner reviews on budget squeegees often mention streaking once the rubber hardens. Store it hanging, not pressed against a wall, and it'll stay true longer.

Who should buy it: a couple who mostly needs a shower door and a bathroom mirror cleaned, and who wants to spend the leftover budget on the rest of the apartment.

Bona Hardwood Floor Spray Mop

Rough price band: typically $30–$45.

Here's the parts angle. The Bona system is a spray mop, not a squeegee, but it belongs in this conversation because it's the clearest example of a budget brand built around refills: cartridges, pads, and cleaning solution are all sold separately and widely stocked. If you're setting up a first apartment and want one brand whose consumables you can actually reorder, this is the pattern to look for. For glass, pair it with a handheld squeegee; for floors, it does the job without a bucket.

Downsides: it's a floor tool, so it won't clean your shower door. The spray cartridge runs out and needs replacing, which is a recurring cost — usually a few dollars per refill.

Who should buy it: anyone who wants a mop whose pads and solution will still be on sale in three years.

O-Cedar EasyWring Spin Mop

Rough price band: typically $35–$50.

Same logic, different tool. The EasyWring is a spin mop with a widely available replacement head, and the mop head refills are one of the most commonly restocked items in this category. It's not a squeegee, but if the goal is "cheap tools I can keep running," a mop with a $10-ish replacement head beats a mop you throw away. Owner reviews consistently praise the wringer mechanism; the common complaint is that the bucket takes up closet space.

Downsides: bulky for a small apartment, and the spin mechanism is the part that eventually wears.

Who should buy it: couples with hard floors and a closet to spare.

Rubbermaid Reveal Spray Mop

Rough price band: typically $25–$40.

The Reveal's selling point is a refillable bottle — you mix your own cleaning solution instead of buying cartridges. That's a genuine money-saver over a couple of years, and the microfiber pads are replaceable and machine washable. Again, this is a floor tool, and again the reason it's here is parts: washable pads mean near-zero consumable cost.

Downsides: the spray trigger is the weak point according to many owner reviews, and the bottle can drip if you over-tighten it.

Who should buy it: budget-focused couples who'd rather mix cleaner than buy it.

E-Cloth General Purpose Pack

Rough price band: usually $15–$25.

For glass and mirrors, a good microfiber cloth is the partner a squeegee needs — one to wet, one to buff the edges. E-Cloth's general-purpose pack is a well-known option with a long lifespan; the cloths are washable hundreds of times according to the manufacturer, which is the closest thing to a "filter" in cloth form. There's nothing to replace, which is the point.

Downsides: no blade, so it won't do the squeegee's job on a wet shower door. Cheap cloths pill; these generally don't.

Who should buy it: everyone. Buy this alongside whichever squeegee you pick.

Amazon Basics Microfiber Cloths

Rough price band: typically $10–$15 for a multipack.

The value pick for the same job. Amazon Basics microfiber is the budget standby, widely reviewed as absorbent and durable enough for everyday glass and counter wiping. As with any budget cloth, the edges can fray over time, and the very cheapest packs run thin.

Downsides: not as plush as premium cloths; expect to replace them every year or two.

Who should buy it: a couple furnishing a whole apartment who wants cloths in bulk without thinking about it.

Squeegee

Rough price band: usually under $15.

A second, cheap handheld option worth naming because it's the kind of low-cost squeegee you can buy two of — one for the bathroom, one for the kitchen window — and not care. The bottom line

Buy the Squeegee for the glass, add a pack of E-Cloth General Purpose Pack or Amazon Basics Microfiber Cloths for buffing, and you're out well under $40. If you have hard floors too, put the remaining budget toward a mop whose replacement parts you can actually reorder — that's the Bona Hardwood Floor Spray Mop, the O-Cedar EasyWring Spin Mop, or the Rubbermaid Reveal Spray Mop. The cheap squeegee is the easy part; the refills are what keep the whole setup cheap for years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do cheap squeegees have replaceable blades?

Most fixed-blade squeegees under $20 do not — you replace the whole unit. If replaceable blades matter to you, check the listing for a refill before buying.

What's the best way to avoid streaks with a budget squeegee?

Wet the glass first, work top to bottom, and wipe the blade between strokes. Pair it with a microfiber cloth like the E-Cloth General Purpose Pack or Amazon Basics Microfiber Cloths to buff the edges.

Why does replacement parts availability matter for a first apartment?

Because consumables — mop heads, pads, spray cartridges — are the recurring cost. A mop like the Rubbermaid Reveal Spray Mop with washable pads, or the O-Cedar EasyWring Spin Mop with cheap replacement heads, costs less to keep running over a couple of years.
