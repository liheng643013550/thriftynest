Electric Kettle vs Stovetop: Which Is Actually Cheaper?

If you’re trying to cut costs in the kitchen, the way you heat water matters more than you might think. Whether you are making your morning pour-over or a quick bowl of oatmeal, the appliance you choose affects your electric bill. We did the math on the classic debate—electric kettle vs. stovetop—to see which one actually saves you money.

The Short Answer: Electric Kettles Win on Efficiency

In almost every scenario, an electric kettle is cheaper to operate than a stovetop kettle. The reason comes down to physics and heat transfer.

An electric kettle sits directly on top of its heating element, which is submerged in the water. This design wastes almost no heat. The energy goes straight into the water. A stovetop kettle, on the other hand, sits on a burner. The burner heats the air, the metal of the pan, and the glass or metal of the kettle before the water gets hot. That lost heat is money leaving your kitchen.

According to energy efficiency studies, electric kettles are about 80% efficient. Stovetop kettles are roughly 70% efficient on a gas stove, and slightly worse on an electric coil stove. That 10% difference adds up, especially if you boil water daily.

Breaking Down the Actual Cost Per Boil

Let’s get specific. We’ll use the average US electricity rate of 16 cents per kilowatt-hour (kWh). We’ll also assume you are boiling 4 cups of water (about 1 liter), which is a standard amount for tea or a small pasta pot.

Here is the rough math for each method.

Electric kettle: - Power: 1500 watts (1.5 kW) - Boil time: 3 to 4 minutes - Energy used: 0.08 to 0.10 kWh - Cost per boil: 1.3 to 1.6 cents

Stovetop on gas: - Power: 10,000 BTU burner (about 2.9 kW) - Boil time: 5 to 6 minutes - Energy used: 0.12 to 0.15 kWh equivalent - Cost per boil: 2.0 to 2.5 cents

Stovetop on electric coil: - Power: 2,000 watts - Boil time: 6 to 7 minutes - Energy used: 0.20 to 0.23 kWh - Cost per boil: 3.2 to 3.7 cents

The numbers are small per boil, but they compound. If you boil water once a day, an electric kettle saves you around 3 to 8 dollars per year compared to a gas stovetop. Compared to an electric coil stove, the savings are closer to 7 to 10 dollars per year.

Gas vs. Electric Stoves: A Special Note

Many people assume gas is cheaper because the utility rate per BTU is often lower than electricity. That is true in some regions. However, gas stoves are incredibly inefficient at heating a kettle. A lot of the flame’s heat wraps around the sides of the kettle and escapes into the room. You are paying to heat your kitchen, not just your water.

If you have a gas stove and an electric kettle, the electric kettle is almost always the cheaper choice. The only exception is if you live in an area with extremely high electricity rates (above 30 cents per kWh) and very cheap natural gas. Even then, the difference is pennies per month.

The Hidden Costs of Each Method

The purchase price matters, too. You are not just paying per boil—you are paying for the device itself.

Electric kettles range from $20 to $60 for a reliable model. A basic Hamilton Beach Electric Kettle costs around $25 and lasts 3 to 5 years with daily use. A premium model like the Cuisinart Perfectemp costs $80 but has temperature presets that save time, not energy.

Stovetop kettles are cheaper upfront. A basic OXO Good Grips Stovetop Kettle costs about $35. A simple stainless steel one from Farberware can be found for $15 to $20. These last a long time—often 10 years or more—because they have no electronics to fail.

But here is the catch: stovetop kettles are slower. Time is a hidden cost. If you value your time at even a minimum wage level, waiting an extra 2 to 3 minutes per boil adds up to several hours a year. An electric kettle boils faster because it uses concentrated power directly in the water.

When a Stovetop Kettle Makes Sense

There are a few situations where a stovetop kettle is the better buy.

  • You are on a tight budget right now. A $15 stovetop kettle beats a $30 electric kettle if you only have $20 in your pocket.
  • You have no counter space. Electric kettles take up permanent space on your counter. A stovetop kettle lives on the stove and disappears when not in use.
  • You frequently cook for large groups. If you are boiling 3 liters of water for a huge batch of pasta, a stovetop kettle on a large burner can handle bigger volumes than most electric kettles, which top out at 1.7 liters.
  • You are off-grid or use solar power. A stovetop kettle works with any heat source, while an electric kettle needs a reliable 120V outlet.

In these cases, the stovetop kettle is not "cheaper" per boil, but it is the lower-cost entry point. You are trading efficiency for simplicity and upfront savings.

Temperature Control and Waste

If you are a tea drinker, temperature control can save you money in a sneaky way. Electric kettles with temperature presets let you heat water to exactly 175°F for green tea or 200°F for black tea. This avoids boiling water to 212°F and then waiting for it to cool—a process that wastes electricity and time.

The Zwilling Enfinigy Electric Kettle has precise temperature settings. Stovetop kettles with a whistle do not. You have to watch and guess. If you overshoot, you have wasted energy.

Also, consider the "reboil" habit. Many people boil a full kettle, use a cup, and then reboil the rest later. Reboiling costs the same as the first boil. An electric kettle lets you boil exactly the amount you need. A stovetop kettle often gets overfilled because you cannot see the water level easily.

Maintenance and Long-Term Costs

Electric kettles need descaling. If you have hard water, mineral buildup reduces heating efficiency over time. A kettle with limescale takes longer to boil, which means higher energy costs. You can fix this by using a 50/50 water and vinegar solution monthly. That costs pennies.

Stovetop kettles have no electronics, but they can develop rust or pitting on the interior if you leave water in them. This shortens their lifespan. You need to empty and dry them after every use. If you forget, you might be buying a new one sooner than expected.

In terms of repairs, neither is worth fixing. When an electric kettle dies, you recycle it and buy a new one. When a stovetop kettle rusts through, you recycle the metal. Both have similar lifespans in practice, despite the stovetop kettle’s simpler design.

The Verdict on Total Cost of Ownership

Let’s put together a 5-year cost projection for daily use (one boil per day, 4 cups each time).

Electric kettle: - Purchase price: $30 - Energy cost: 1.5 cents per boil x 365 days x 5 years = $27.38 - Total: $57.38

Stovetop kettle (gas stove): - Purchase price: $20 - Energy cost: 2.25 cents per boil x 365 days x 5 years = $41.06 - Total: $61.06

Stovetop kettle (electric coil stove): - Purchase price: $20 - Energy cost: 3.5 cents per boil x 365 days x 5 years = $63.88 - Total: $83.88

The electric kettle wins over 5 years against gas, and it wins big against electric coil stoves. The only way a stovetop kettle wins is if you buy the absolute cheapest model and you have natural gas at a rock-bottom price.

The Bottom Line

An electric kettle is the cheaper option for daily boiling. It uses less energy, wastes less heat, and saves you time. The upfront cost is slightly higher, but you recoup that within the first year of regular use.

If you already own a stovetop kettle and it works fine, do not run out and buy an electric one. You will not save enough money to justify the purchase unless you boil water multiple times a day. But if you are buying fresh, or your old kettle is failing, go electric. Your wallet and your schedule will thank you.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does one boil actually cost with each method?

Using the average US electricity rate of 16 cents per kWh and 4 cups of water, an electric kettle runs about 1.3 to 1.6 cents per boil. A stovetop kettle on gas costs roughly 2.0 to 2.5 cents, and on an electric coil stove about 3.2 to 3.7 cents. The gap is small for a single boil but grows with daily use.

Is a gas stove cheaper than an electric kettle?

Usually not. Gas rates per BTU can be lower than electricity in some regions, but a gas burner loses a large share of its flame heat around the sides of the kettle, so much of what you pay for warms the room instead of the water. The exception is an area with electricity above 30 cents per kWh combined with very cheap natural gas, and even then the difference is only pennies per month.

What does a five-year cost comparison look like?

For one daily boil of 4 cups, an electric kettle at $30 plus $27.38 in energy comes to about $57.38. A $20 stovetop kettle on gas totals roughly $61.06, and the same kettle on an electric coil stove reaches about $83.88. The electric kettle comes out ahead against gas and by a wide margin against electric coil.
