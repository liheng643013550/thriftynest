Drill Bits vs Hammer: Which Is Worth It?

The choice between drill bits and a hammer isn't really a fair fight — they do different jobs. But if you're downsizing into a smaller home and only have room (and budget) for one tool kit, the question becomes practical: which one earns its footprint? A hammer suits someone hanging shelves, tapping in picture hooks, and doing light demolition. A drill-bit set suits anyone who needs clean holes in wood, metal, or drywall — and a surprising number of small-home jobs fall into that category.

Here's the reality check first: at the budget end — typically under $40 — neither a basic bit set nor a mid-weight hammer is a precision instrument. Cheap bits dull faster and can wander off-center in hard materials. Inexpensive hammers can have poorly seated heads or handles that loosen with heavy use. What this price band can do is cover everyday household tasks reliably for years if you buy from a known brand and match the tool to the material. What it can't do is substitute for a rotary hammer on concrete or a pro-grade bit set on stainless steel.

Quick verdict

  • Tight on space, mostly hanging things: Go with a hammer. A 16 oz curved-claw model like the Vaughan 16 oz Curved Claw hangs on a peg or tucks in a drawer and handles picture hooks, small nails, and light pry work.
  • Renting or in a condo where you drill occasionally: A compact bit set such as the Bosch Daredevil Set covers wood, drywall, and light metal in a case smaller than a paperback.
  • One tool only, and you'll be mounting things: A hammer wins on versatility for a downsizer — but only if you also keep a small bit set for the actual holes.

Cost to own

Neither tool is expensive up front, but they age differently. A basic high-speed-steel bit set like the Neiko HSS Set typically runs $15–$25 and covers the common sizes for wood and drywall. Owner reviews often note that budget HSS bits dull after a dozen or so holes in hardwood — you'll replace the small sizes within a year or two. Cobalt bits, such as the IRWIN Cobalt Set, cost more (usually $30–$50) but hold an edge longer in metal.

A hammer, by contrast, is a near one-time purchase. A 16 oz framing hammer like the Milwaukee 16 oz Framing or the Vaughan curved-claw model will last decades with no consumables. Budget roughly $0 per year after purchase versus $10–$20 per year replacing worn bits.

Verdict: The hammer wins on long-term cost. The bit set wins on jobs-per-dollar if you actually drill.

Space and footprint

This is the downsizer's real question. A 16 oz hammer is about 12–14 inches long and weighs roughly a pound — it hangs on a nail, fits in a kitchen drawer, or slides into a toolbox corner. A bit set in a molded case is often 6–10 inches wide and an inch thick, so it stacks under a sink or in a closet shelf.

The tricky part: a bit set is useless without a drill. If you don't already own one, you're storing a drill plus bits — that's the bigger footprint. The hammer stands alone.

Verdict: For pure footprint, the hammer is the smaller commitment. If you already own a drill, the bit set is nearly free in space terms.

Cleaning and upkeep

Bits need a little care: wipe them dry after use, keep them out of damp drawers, and store them in the case so the flutes don't chip against other tools. Rust is the main enemy of budget steel, and it shows up fast in a humid bathroom drawer. Hammers need almost nothing — a wipe of the head and an occasional check that the handle isn't cracked.

Verdict: Hammer, easily, on maintenance.

Speed on the job

For hanging a picture with a small nail, a hammer takes seconds; a drill is overkill. For putting a clean 1/4-inch hole through drywall for an anchor, a drill with the right bit takes under a minute; a hammer just makes a mess. Owner reviews of the DEWALT Black Oxide Set frequently mention clean, fast cuts in wood and plastic — but the same reviewers note these bits are not for masonry.

Verdict: Depends entirely on the task. Speed is a wash; it's a job-matching question.

Noise

Both are brief and both are loud. A cordless drill typically runs around 70–80 dB at arm's length; a hammer strike peaks higher but lasts a fraction of a second. In a small apartment with shared walls, the drill's sustained whine is usually more noticeable to neighbors than a few hammer taps.

Verdict: The hammer is marginally friendlier in close quarters — but only for a handful of strikes.

Versatility

A hammer drives nails, sets hooks, taps joints together, and pries light trim. It cannot make a clean hole. A bit set makes holes in wood, drywall, and light metal — but only with a drill attached, and it can't drive a nail. For a downsizer doing picture hanging, small repairs, and occasional furniture assembly, the hammer covers more of those jobs on its own.

Verdict: Hammer for standalone versatility; bit set for anyone who already owns a drill.

When to choose each one

Choose a hammer if your to-do list is mostly hanging, tapping, and light prying, you're short on storage, and you want one tool that works without a power source. The Vaughan 16 oz Curved Claw is a sensible small-space pick.

Choose drill bits if you already own a drill, you're mounting shelves or anchors into drywall, or you assemble flat-pack furniture regularly. The Bosch Daredevil Set covers the common sizes in a compact case; step up to the IRWIN Cobalt Set only if you drill metal often.

The bottom line

For a downsizer with limited shelf space and a general handyman list, the hammer is the better single purchase — it's cheaper to own over time, needs no upkeep, and works alone. But be honest about your list: if it includes shelves, anchors, or furniture, a small bit set alongside it is worth the few inches of drawer space. Buy the hammer first, add bits when a job actually calls for them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need both a hammer and drill bits?

Not necessarily. If your list is mostly hanging and tapping, a hammer alone covers it. Add a bit set when you have a drill and a job that needs clean holes, like drywall anchors or furniture assembly.

Which is cheaper to own over time?

The hammer. It's a near one-time purchase with no consumables, while budget high-speed-steel bits typically dull after a dozen or so holes in hardwood and need replacing every year or two.

Will a basic bit set work on concrete?

No. Standard high-speed-steel and black-oxide bits are for wood, drywall, and light metal. Masonry needs a dedicated masonry bit and, for anything hard, a rotary hammer — not a budget set.
