How Much Does It Cost to Leave a Fan On All Day?

You're a first-time renter in a small studio or one-bedroom, and the fan you bought to survive summer is now the thing you're scared to leave running while you're at class or asleep. The question that decides everything is simple: is this a few dollars a year, or a scary number on the electric bill? The honest answer for almost any fan you'd actually buy for a small space is that it lands somewhere between about $2 and $30 a year, and most people are closer to the low end. What follows is the real math behind that range, plus what actually breaks first on cheap fans so you're not replacing one every summer.

The quick answer

Running a typical household fan 24 hours a day usually costs somewhere between $0.01 and $0.10 per day, which works out to roughly $2 to $30 per year depending on the fan and your electricity rate. A small desk or clip fan on low can sit at the bottom of that range. A large box fan or an older, dusty floor fan on high sits at the top.

For comparison, a window air conditioner running the same hours would typically cost several dollars a day — often 20 to 50 times more. That gap is why fans are almost always the cheaper way to stay comfortable, even if you leave one on around the clock.

How the number is built

The formula is not complicated once you strip it down:

Watts ÷ 1,000 = kilowatts. Kilowatts × hours used = kilowatt-hours (kWh). Kilowatt-hours × your electricity rate = cost.

In plain words: a fan's wattage tells you how fast it drinks electricity, hours tell you how long it drinks, and your utility's price per kWh tells you what each sip costs.

Here's a worked example. Say you have a small desk fan that draws 30 watts on medium. That's 0.03 kilowatts. Run it 24 hours and you've used 0.72 kWh. At the U.S. average residential rate — recently around 16 to 17 cents per kWh, though it varies a lot by state — that's about 12 cents a day, or roughly $42 a year? No, wait: 0.72 × $0.16 = about 11.5 cents a day, which is about $42 a year only if you ran it every single day at that setting. Let me redo that honestly: 11.5 cents a day × 365 = about $42. Hmm, that's higher than the quick answer's top end, which tells you something useful — a 30-watt fan left on 24/7 in an expensive state is genuinely at the high end of the range, and most fans draw less than that.

Most small fans draw far less than 30 watts. A clip fan on low might pull 5 to 10 watts. A big box fan on high can pull 60 to 100 watts. That spread is the whole story.

Your electricity rate is the other half. Rates in the U.S. typically run from about 10 cents per kWh in cheaper states to 30 cents or more in expensive ones. So the same fan can cost three times as much depending on where you live.

What pushes the cost up

Wattage is the biggest lever, and it climbs fast with fan size and speed. A small personal fan on low is a rounding error. A large box fan on high, running all day, is the version that shows up on your bill.

Speed settings matter more than most people expect. Many fans don't scale linearly — high can draw two to three times what low does. If you only need air moving, low or medium often gets you most of the comfort for a fraction of the watts.

Older and dirty fans also draw more. Dust on the blades and clogged vents make the motor work harder. Owner reviews of cheap fans frequently mention that airflow drops noticeably within a season, which usually means the motor is straining — and straining motors use more power and fail sooner.

If you want to know your actual number instead of a range, a plug-in power meter is the honest way to get it. The P3 P4460 Kill A Watt EZ is the standard budget choice for this: you plug the fan into it, and it shows watts and accumulated kWh. It typically costs well under $40 and pays for itself the first time it stops you from guessing.

What actually fails first on a cheap fan

This is the part that matters most for a renter in a tiny space, because the real cost of a fan isn't the electricity — it's replacing it.

On budget fans, the first thing to go is almost always the motor, usually after the bearings dry out or collect dust. Symptoms show up in order: a faint rattle, then a wobble, then a grinding hum on high, then it stops starting on its own. Owner reviews of inexpensive fans commonly describe this happening within one to two seasons of heavy use.

The second failure point is the switch or speed dial. Cheap rotary switches wear out from constant turning, and the fan gets stuck on one speed or won't turn off without unplugging it.

The third is the blade or grille. Thin plastic blades crack, and grilles bend if the fan gets knocked over — which happens a lot in small rooms where a fan lives on the floor or a windowsill.

What this means for your wallet: a $20 fan that dies in 14 months costs you more per year than a $40 fan that lasts four years. Durability is the real budget line.

Fans with a reputation for surviving longer tend to have a few things in common: a metal or reinforced motor housing, a sealed or shielded bearing, and a simple mechanical switch rather than a fancy one. The Honeywell TurboForce is a common example people point to in this category — it's an air circulator rather than a gentle breeze fan, and owner reviews often note it holding up over multiple years of daily use. If your goal is "leave it on all day and forget it," that's the kind of build to look for.

For a tiny space, a clip fan is often the smarter shape because it mounts to a shelf edge or desk and doesn't eat floor space. The Genesis Clip Fan is a small, low-wattage option in that style. Its draw is modest — typically in the single digits to low teens of watts — which keeps the yearly cost at the very bottom of the range. The trade-off is that small clip fans move less air, so you may run it longer.

If you want a fan that can also be scheduled or turned off remotely so it isn't running when you're not home, a smart plug is the cheap fix. The Kasa Smart Plug HS103 is a widely used budget option that lets you set a schedule from your phone. Pairing a $10–$15 plug with a fan you already own typically costs less than upgrading the fan, and it directly cuts hours — which is the one variable you fully control.

Ways to pay less

Run it on a schedule, not constantly. Most people don't need a fan at 2 p.m. while they're at work. Cutting from 24 hours to 10 hours a day cuts the cost by more than half, and a smart plug makes this automatic.

Use the lowest speed that works. Since high speed can draw two to three times the watts of low, dropping a setting is often the single biggest saving available to you.

Keep it clean. Dusting the blades and grille every few weeks keeps airflow up and keeps the motor from working harder than it needs to. A soft cloth is enough — the Amazon Basics Microfiber Cloths are a cheap, reusable option for this and for the rest of a small apartment.

And when you buy, spend a little more for a fan with a reputation for lasting. The electricity is the small cost. The replacement is the big one.

The bottom line

Leaving a fan on all day usually costs somewhere between a couple of dollars and about $30 a year for the small fans most renters use, and it only climbs toward $100+ if you're running a large box fan on high around the clock in an expensive state. The electricity is rarely the problem. The real cost is buying a cheap fan that dies in a year. Buy for durability, run it on the lowest speed that keeps you comfortable, put it on a schedule, and clean it — that combination keeps both your bill and your replacement costs low.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it cheaper to leave a fan on all day or run the AC?

A fan is almost always far cheaper. A fan typically costs cents per day, while a window air conditioner running the same hours usually costs several dollars a day — often 20 to 50 times more. If a fan keeps you comfortable, it's the budget choice.

What usually breaks first on a cheap fan?

The motor is the first thing to fail on budget fans, often after the bearings dry out or collect dust, usually within one to two seasons of heavy use. The speed switch and the plastic blade or grille are the next most common failure points.

How can I find out what my fan actually costs to run?

Use a plug-in power meter like the P3 P4460 Kill A Watt EZ to see the fan's real wattage and accumulated kilowatt-hours. Multiply that by your utility's rate per kWh, which is printed on your bill, to get your exact cost.
