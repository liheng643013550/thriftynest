You've done the math on those little countertop herb kits and it doesn't work. The light is weak, the basil stretches toward it and goes pale, and the replacement bulbs cost more than the herbs are worth. So you start looking at "real" grow lights and find a wall of blurple panels with wattage numbers that don't mean anything and no mention of what they cost to run every month.

That's the actual problem with budget grow lights for herbs. The purchase price is the easy part. The electricity bill shows up every single month, and a cheap fixture that draws more power than it needs will quietly cost you more than a good one within a year. This guide weights everything toward running cost, because that's where the money really goes.

What actually matters

  • Real draw at the wall, not "equivalent" wattage. A light advertised as "600W equivalent" often pulls 60 to 100 actual watts from the outlet. The real number is what your meter charges you for. Look for the actual power draw in the specs, usually listed as "power consumption" or "actual wattage."
  • Cost per month. Multiply actual watts by your daily hours, divide by 1,000, then multiply by your electricity rate. At a typical US rate of roughly 12 to 17 cents per kWh, a 30-watt light running 14 hours a day costs somewhere around $2 to $3 a month. A 100-watt light costs closer to $6 to $9. Over a year that gap is real money.
  • Full-spectrum white vs. blurple. For culinary herbs you want leaf and stem growth, not flowering. White full-spectrum LEDs are easier to judge plant health by eye and are usually more efficient per watt than the old red-blue panels.
  • Heat and airflow. Herbs like basil, mint, and parsley don't want to be cooked. LEDs run cooler than fluorescents, but a fixture with no ventilation still builds heat in a small space.
  • Adjustability. A light you can raise as plants grow, and dim as seedlings mature, saves electricity and prevents leggy, stretched growth.
  • Build quality and warranty. This is the "hates spending money on things that break" part. Owner reviews are the best signal here — look for reports of drivers failing in year one versus units still running after three or four years.

The honest catch about grow lights and herbs

Here's the thing worth saying plainly: most herbs are not demanding. Basil, mint, chives, parsley, and thyme will grow acceptably on a bright windowsill for part of the year. A grow light earns its keep in winter, in north-facing rooms, and for people who want a steady supply rather than a seasonal one.

That means you don't need much wattage. A 20 to 45 watt LED fixture placed 6 to 12 inches above a small tray of herbs is genuinely enough for most culinary varieties. Buying a 200-watt panel for a windowsill of basil is the most common way people waste money on this hobby — both at checkout and every month afterward.

What to buy instead of guessing on wattage

If you're going to spend money on a grow light, spend it on a fixture from a lighting brand with a published actual-wattage figure. Then spend a small amount on the soil and plants, because that's where most beginners lose their crop.

Miracle-Gro Potting Mix

Typical price: usually around $8 to $15 for a 16-quart bag, depending on size.

This is the cheapest way to improve your odds with any light setup. Herbs in poor, compacted soil stretch and yellow no matter how good the light is. Owner reviews consistently mention it holds moisture well and drains reasonably for containers, though some reviewers note it can run a little heavy on fertilizer for seedlings.

Downsides: It's not a seed-starting mix. It's also not sterile, so you may see fungus gnats in a bag that's been sitting damp. Let it dry out between waterings and that's rarely an issue.

Who should buy it: Anyone potting up basil, mint, or parsley from a nursery start. It's the single highest-value purchase on this page.

Cast Iron Plant

Typical price: usually around $25 to $45 for a potted plant.

Wait — this isn't an herb. It's here because it's the honest answer for the low-light corner where herbs keep dying. If your space genuinely can't support basil, stop fighting it. Cast Iron Plant tolerates low light and neglect better than almost anything, and owner reviews repeatedly describe it as nearly unkillable.

Downsides: It's slow-growing and it's not edible. If you want pesto, this isn't it.

Who should buy it: People whose "grow light" plan is really just a dark apartment and wishful thinking.

Golden Pothos

Typical price: usually around $15 to $30 for a potted plant.

Same logic as above. Golden Pothos grows in moderate indirect light, tolerates missed waterings, and gives you something green and alive in a space that won't support herbs. Reviewers often mention how quickly it roots in water, which makes it a cheap way to expand your collection.

Downsides: Toxic to cats and dogs if chewed. Not edible for you either.

Who should buy it: Anyone who wants the satisfaction of a thriving plant without a lighting investment.

Snake Plant Laurentii

Typical price: usually around $20 to $40 for a potted plant.

The most forgiving plant on this list. It handles low light, dry air, and irregular watering. If you're testing whether you even enjoy indoor growing before buying a light, start here.

Downsides: Extremely slow growth. Also toxic to pets.

Who should buy it: Beginners who want to prove they can keep something alive for three months before spending on equipment.

ZZ Plant

Typical price: usually around $20 to $40 for a potted plant.

ZZ Plant stores water in its rhizomes, so it survives neglect that would kill an herb in a week. Owner reviews consistently report it doing fine in rooms with nothing but overhead lighting.

Downsides: Slow, and again, not edible and toxic to pets.

Who should buy it: The "I travel a lot" crowd.

Spathiphyllum (Peace Lily)

Typical price: usually around $20 to $40 for a potted plant.

Peace Lily is the one plant on this list that will visibly droop when it's thirsty, which makes it a surprisingly good training tool for learning to read plants. It manages in moderate indirect light.

Downsides: Toxic to pets, and it's fussy about tap water in some areas — brown tips are common.

Who should buy it: People who want feedback from their plants rather than silence.

The running cost math, spelled out

Take the two ends of the budget range. A 25-watt LED fixture running 14 hours a day uses 0.35 kWh per day, or about 10.5 kWh a month. At 15 cents per kWh that's roughly $1.60 a month, or about $19 a year. A 100-watt fixture on the same schedule uses about 42 kWh a month, costing roughly $6.30 monthly and about $76 a year.

That's a $57 annual difference between two lights that both grow herbs. If the 25-watt unit costs $30 and the 100-watt unit costs $60, the cheap-looking one is cheaper in year one and dramatically cheaper by year three — assuming it doesn't fail, which is why owner reviews about driver reliability matter more than the sticker price.

The bottom line

For herbs specifically, you almost never need more than 20 to 45 actual watts of full-spectrum LED. Buy based on the published power consumption figure, not the "equivalent" marketing number, and do the monthly math before you check out. A light that draws 30 watts and lasts four years beats a 100-watt panel that dies in eighteen months on both cost and hassle.

If you're not ready to buy a fixture at all, be honest about it. Pot up some herbs with Miracle-Gro Potting Mix on your brightest sill, and if that spot is genuinely too dark, put a Golden Pothos or a ZZ Plant there instead and save the electricity. Killing a $20 plant teaches you more than buying a light you don't need.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many watts does a grow light for herbs actually need?

For culinary herbs, 20 to 45 actual watts of full-spectrum LED is usually enough for a small tray. The number that matters is the published power consumption figure, not the "equivalent" wattage in the product name.

How much does it cost to run a grow light every month?

A 25-watt LED running 14 hours a day costs roughly $1.60 a month at 15 cents per kWh, or about $19 a year. A 100-watt fixture on the same schedule runs closer to $6.30 a month and about $76 a year.

What should I buy first if I'm not ready for a grow light?

Start with Miracle-Gro Potting Mix and a plant that tolerates your actual light levels — Cast Iron Plant, Snake Plant Laurentii, or ZZ Plant are all forgiving options in the $20 to $45 range. If those thrive, you'll know your space can support herbs.
