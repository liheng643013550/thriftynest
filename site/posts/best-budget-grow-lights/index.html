If you're starting seeds on a windowsill or trying to keep a pothos alive through a dark winter, you've probably figured out that regular room light isn't enough. The good news: you don't need to spend $150 on a pro setup. I've tested and compared lights in the $15 to $40 range, and a few of them genuinely work. Here's what's worth buying, and what to skip.

What to look for

  • Real wattage, not "equivalent" wattage. A light advertised as "100W equivalent" might draw 15 actual watts. Check the actual power draw, usually listed in the specs as "actual power" or "power consumption." For a small shelf of seedlings, 20–30 actual watts per light is a reasonable starting point.
  • Full spectrum vs. blurple. Older cheap lights use red-and-blue LEDs that cast a purple glow. They work, but they're miserable to look at in a living space. Modern white full-spectrum LEDs cost about the same now and are much easier on the eyes.
  • Coverage area. A 2-foot light typically covers a 2x2 foot area for seedlings, less for plants that need high light. If you're lighting a 4-foot shelf, you either need two lights or a 4-foot model.
  • Adjustable brightness and timers. Dimmability lets you start seeds gently and crank it up later. A built-in timer means you don't have to remember to switch it on and off, which matters more than you'd think after week three.
  • Heat output. LEDs run cooler than fluorescents, but cheap panels can still get warm. If your light is 4 inches from seedlings, you want something that won't cook them.
  • Mounting options. Look for included hanging hardware, clips, or a stand. A $30 light that needs $20 of extra rigging isn't a $30 light.

The bottom line

For one houseplant, grab the GooingTop clip-on and stop overthinking it. For seed starting, the Barrina T5 two-pack is the best value in this price range, and the Monios-L T8 is worth the extra $10 if you need 4-foot coverage. If you already own a lamp, the Sansi bulb is the easiest upgrade you can make for $25.

Skip anything that advertises "1000W" for $25. It's not 1000 watts, and the seller knows it. Buy based on actual power draw and coverage, and you'll get real results without spending real money, in my experience.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many hours a day should I run a budget grow light?

Most houseplants and seedlings do well with 12 to 16 hours of light per day. Seedlings generally want closer to 14 to 16 hours, while low-light houseplants like pothos are fine with 10 to 12. An outlet timer costs about $10 and removes all the guesswork.

Can a cheap grow light actually work, or am I wasting money?

A $20 to $40 LED will absolutely grow seedlings, herbs, and low-to-medium light houseplants. What it won't do is fruit tomatoes or flowering plants that need very high intensity. Match the light to the plant, and budget options work fine.

Do I need a purple light or a white one?

You don't need purple. Older blurple LEDs worked, but modern white full-spectrum LEDs perform just as well and are far more pleasant to have in your home. Unless you're growing in a closet where nobody sees it, buy white, honestly.
