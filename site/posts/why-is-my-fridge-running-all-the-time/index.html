Why Your Fridge Runs Constantly (and What It Costs You)

If your fridge never seems to shut off, the good news is that most causes are cheap to fix — often a clogged coil, a worn door gasket, or a thermostat set too cold. The bad news is that a fridge that runs constantly quietly adds to your electric bill every single day, so it's worth an hour of your time this weekend.

Here's the question that decides everything: is the fridge running nonstop and keeping food cold, or is it running nonstop and struggling to stay cold? If it's holding temperature, you're usually looking at an airflow, gasket, or settings problem. If it's warm inside, you may be looking at a sealed-system failure, and that's the point where repair math starts to favor replacement.

The specs on most standard top-freezer fridges put normal compressor run time at roughly 50 to 70 percent of the time in a warm kitchen. If yours is running 90 percent or more, something below is likely going on.

Cause 1: Dirty or Blocked Condenser Coils

This is the most common reason a fridge runs all the time, and it's the cheapest to fix.

How to confirm it: Pull the fridge out from the wall and look at the coils — on most older models they're the black grid on the back; on many newer ones they're a mat underneath, behind a kick plate at the bottom front. A visible felt of dust, pet hair, or lint means airflow is restricted and the compressor has to work harder to shed heat.

The fix: Unplug the fridge, then vacuum the coils with a brush attachment. A coil brush reaches between the fins. On a pet-heavy household, this may need doing every three to six months.

What it costs: Nothing if you already own a vacuum. A coil brush is typically under $15.

Cause 2: A Failing Door Gasket

A gasket that no longer seals lets cold air leak out, so the compressor runs to replace it. Owner reviews of older fridges mention this constantly.

How to confirm it: The dollar-bill test. Close the door on a dollar bill so half hangs out, then tug. If it slides out with almost no resistance, the seal is weak at that spot. Run the test at the top, middle, and bottom of both doors, and look for cracks, tears, or a gasket that's gone stiff.

The fix: Clean the gasket first with warm soapy water — sometimes grime is all that's breaking the seal. If it's genuinely worn, replacement gaskets are model-specific and usually available from the manufacturer or a parts retailer. Many are user-installable; some need a technician.

What it costs: A replacement gasket typically runs $30 to $80 for the part. Professional installation usually adds $100 or more.

Cause 3: Temperature Set Too Low, or a Faulty Thermostat

A fridge set to its coldest setting will run almost continuously, especially in summer.

How to confirm it: Check the dial or digital setting. The FDA food-safety guidance is to keep the fridge at or below 40°F and the freezer at 0°F. Many people run theirs at 34°F "to be safe," which forces near-constant running. If the setting is reasonable but the fridge still never cycles off, the thermostat or thermistor may be misreading temperature.

The fix: Set the fridge to 37°F to 38°F and the freezer to 0°F, then give it 24 hours to settle. If it still won't cycle off, a technician can test the thermostat — this is not usually a DIY part on modern digital models.

What it costs: A thermostat or thermistor part typically runs $20 to $60, with labor on top if you hire out.

Cause 4: Door Left Ajar, or a Blocked Interior Vent

Small things that keep cold air from staying put.

How to confirm it: Look for a door that doesn't sit flush — a bent hinge, an overstuffed shelf pushing the door open, or a crisper drawer not fully seated. Inside, find the air vent between freezer and fridge compartments and check that a big casserole dish or a full shelf isn't blocking it.

The fix: Rearrange shelves so air can move, and check that the fridge sits level so the door self-closes. If a hinge is bent, that's a repair.

What it costs: Free for rearranging; hinge repair typically $100 to $200 with a technician.

Cause 5: A Warm Room or Poor Placement

A fridge in a hot garage or jammed against a wall can't shed heat efficiently.

How to confirm it: Feel the wall or cabinet beside and behind the fridge. Specs generally call for at least an inch or two of clearance on the sides and back. If the fridge sits in a garage that hits 90°F in summer, the compressor will run far more than it would indoors.

The fix: Pull it out from the wall, clear the top, and if it's in a hot space, consider whether that's the right home for it. Most standard fridges aren't rated for garage temperature swings.

What it costs: Free.

Cause 6: A Worn Compressor or Sealed-System Leak

This is the expensive one, and it's why the opening question matters.

How to confirm it: The fridge runs constantly and the food isn't cold enough. You may hear clicking as the compressor tries and fails to start. Frost may be uneven, or the freezer may be fine while the fridge section warms. A technician confirms with pressure gauges.

The fix: Sealed-system repair — compressor, refrigerant leak, or evaporator — is skilled work. On a fridge more than about 10 years old, the repair often costs more than the appliance is worth.

What it costs: Typically $300 to $600 or more, depending on the part and your area.

When to stop and replace it instead

The rule most appliance technicians use: if the repair is more than half the price of a new comparable fridge, replace it. A sealed-system repair at $400 to $600 on a fridge that's already 12 years old is usually money down the drain.

At the budget end, the trade-off is durability. Budget top-freezer models typically last 10 to 13 years, and what fails first is almost always the same: the compressor and the door gaskets. Cheaper models often use thinner gaskets and less robust compressors, so the constant-running problem tends to come back sooner. Owner reviews of budget fridges frequently mention gasket wear within five to seven years, versus longer on mid-range units.

If you're replacing, look for a model with a manufacturer warranty of at least one year on parts and labor, and check whether the compressor has a longer separate warranty — some brands offer five to ten years on the sealed system, which is the single most useful spec for durability. Expect to pay roughly $600 to $900 for a basic 18-cubic-foot top-freezer, and $900 to $1,300 for one with better seals and a longer compressor warranty.

Before you buy, measure your opening — including door swing — and confirm the model is rated for your room temperature. A fridge rated only for indoor use will struggle in a garage.

Preventing this next time

  • Vacuum the coils every six months, more often with pets.
  • Wipe the gaskets monthly and run the dollar-bill test twice a year.
  • Keep the fridge at 37°F to 38°F and the freezer at 0°F, not colder.
  • Leave clearance behind and beside the unit, and don't pack shelves so tightly that air can't move.
  • If you want to know what the fridge is actually costing you, a plug-in power meter like the P3 P4460 Kill A Watt EZ lets you measure its real draw over a week. A constantly running fridge can add roughly $50 to $150 a year to your bill, depending on your rate.

The bottom line

Nine times out of ten, a fridge that runs all the time has dirty coils, a weak gasket, or a setting that's too cold — fixes that cost under $80 and an afternoon. Start with the coils and the dollar-bill test before you call anyone. If the food isn't staying cold and a technician confirms a sealed-system problem on an older unit, put the money toward a replacement with a long compressor warranty instead.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my fridge is running too much?

Normal run time is roughly 50 to 70 percent of the time in a warm kitchen. If yours runs 90 percent or more and never seems to cycle off, check the coils, gasket, and temperature setting first.

What's the cheapest thing to check first?

Dirty condenser coils. Unplug the fridge and vacuum them — it costs nothing if you own a vacuum, and a coil brush is typically under $15.

When should I replace instead of repair?

When the repair costs more than half the price of a new comparable fridge. A sealed-system repair at $300 to $600 on a unit more than about 10 years old usually isn't worth it.
