You have maybe four feet of wall space, a stack of framed photos still in the moving box, and a lease that says you can't put holes just anywhere. You hold a picture up, eyeball it, and it looks straight — until you step back and see it's an inch low on one side. So you tap a new nail, and now there are two holes and the frame still hangs crooked.

A level fixes this in about ten seconds. The catch is that a lot of cheap levels are junk, and a lot of good ones cost more than the frames you're hanging. Here's what's actually worth buying when you're renting a small place and hanging pictures on a budget.

What actually matters

You don't need a contractor's level for a 16-by-ounce picture frame. You need one that's small, light, and accurate enough that your eye can't argue with it.

  • Length. For pictures under about 24 inches wide, a 9-inch torpedo level is the sweet spot. Anything longer is awkward to hold against a frame while you're also holding a pencil.
  • Vial count and orientation. Three vials — level, plumb, and 45-degree — cover basically everything a renter does. Two vials is fine if you never need to check a vertical edge.
  • Accuracy rating. Cheap levels usually claim something in the range of 0.001 to 0.005 inch per inch. At picture-hanging distances that's a difference nobody can see, so don't pay extra for a tighter number.
  • Weight and grip. A light level you can hold one-handed against a frame beats a heavy aluminum one that pulls the frame off the wall.
  • Hang holes and a ruled edge. If the level has a hole for a nail and printed inch marks, it stops being a level and becomes a layout tool. That's the feature that actually saves you time.
  • What's in the box. A level alone won't hang anything. Hooks, nails, and a small kit matter more than the tool once the level has done its job.

One honest note before the list: a level doesn't consume energy, so there's no "running cost per month" the way there is with a vacuum or a space heater. The only ongoing cost is the hardware you use up — hooks and nails — which typically runs a few dollars a year for a renter hanging a handful of frames. What you're really optimizing here is the cost of a mistake: a bad hole means spackle, paint, and a repair you have to do before you move out.

Craftsman 9-Inch Torpedo Level

Rough price band: usually under $15.

This is the plainest possible answer to the question. It's a 9-inch torpedo with the standard three-vial layout, and it's small enough to tuck in a kitchen drawer next to the tape measure. Owner reviews consistently describe it as accurate out of the box and light enough to hold against a frame with one hand while you mark with the other.

Downsides: it's basic. No magnified vials, no ruled edge worth trusting, and the plastic body won't survive being stepped on or left in a hot car. If you want a level that doubles as a straightedge for cutting, this isn't it.

Who should buy it: the renter hanging six to ten pictures and nothing else. Craftsman 9-Inch Torpedo Level

OOK Brand 50 lb. Picture Hanging Kit

Rough price band: typically $10–$15.

Here's the thing people miss: the level tells you where to put the hook, but the hook is what actually holds the picture. This kit includes the assorted hooks, nails, and wire a renter needs, rated up to 50 lb., which is far more capacity than a typical framed print or poster requires. Reviewers often mention that the assortment covers small frames through medium mirrors without a second trip to the store.

Downsides: the hooks are the classic brass style, which leaves a slightly larger hole than a modern wire hook, and the kit doesn't include a level. You're buying this alongside a level, not instead of one. Also, 50 lb. is a rating for the hook assembly, not a license to hang a 50-pound mirror on drywall with no stud — use the stud finder for anything genuinely heavy.

Who should buy it: anyone hanging more than two things. It's the cheapest way to stop making hardware-store runs. OOK Brand 50 lb. Picture Hanging Kit

Bosch Daredevil Set

Rough price band: usually $15–$25.

This is a drill and driver bit set, and it earns a spot here for one specific reason: if your walls are plaster, brick, or concrete — common in older rentals — you cannot hang anything with a nail alone. You need to drill. The Daredevil bits are widely reported to hold up well in masonry and wood, and the set covers the common sizes.

Downsides: it's a bit set, not a level and not a hanging kit. If you live in a standard drywall apartment, you probably don't need it. It's also overkill if you own one drill and hang two pictures a year.

Who should buy it: renters in older buildings with hard walls, or anyone who already owns a drill and keeps snapping cheap bits. Bosch Daredevil Set

DEWALT 27-Piece Hex Key Set

Rough price band: typically $20–$30.

Frames with adjustable hanging hardware, floating shelves, and the brackets on cheap furniture almost all use hex screws. This set covers the common SAE and metric sizes in a folding holder that won't scatter across your floor.

Downsides: it has nothing to do with leveling. It's here because "hanging pictures" in a small rental usually turns into "also assembling the shelf the pictures sit on." If you already own hex keys, skip it.

Who should buy it: the renter who is furnishing an apartment from flat-pack boxes at the same time. DEWALT 27-Piece Hex Key Set

Milwaukee 16 oz Framing

Rough price band: usually $25–$35.

A hammer is the tool you use when the hook's nail needs two taps instead of ten, and when you need to gently persuade a stubborn hook out of drywall at move-out time. Milwaukee's 16 oz framing hammer has a longer handle and a head design meant to reduce the sting in your hand, which matters more than it sounds when you're nailing into a wall above your head.

Downsides: 16 oz is heavier than you need for picture hooks, and a framing hammer is genuinely the wrong tool for finish work. It's also the loudest thing in this article for your neighbors.

Who should buy it: anyone who wants one hammer that will also handle furniture assembly and minor repairs. Milwaukee 16 oz Framing

Vaughan 16 oz Curved Claw

Rough price band: typically $25–$35.

The Vaughan is the more traditional choice — a curved claw, a wood handle, and a weight that feels balanced rather than front-heavy. Owner reviews often describe it as a "buy it once" hammer, which is the right way to think about a tool you'll use a few times a year for decades.

Downsides: wood handles can dry out and loosen if you store it somewhere hot, and a curved claw is less forgiving than a straight one when you're pulling a nail out of a tight corner.

Who should buy it: someone who wants a single hammer that feels good in the hand and doesn't need a framing-specific design. Vaughan 16 oz Curved Claw

Neodymium Magnetic Wristband

Rough price band: usually under $15.

This is the small-space pick. When your "workshop" is a 6-by-8-foot room and the floor is carpet, dropped nails disappear forever. A magnetic wristband holds the nails, hooks, and bits while both your hands are busy holding the frame and the level.

Downsides: it only holds ferrous metal — no brass hooks, no aluminum. And it's a convenience item, not a necessity. If you hang one picture a year, you don't need it.

Who should buy it: anyone hanging a lot of things in a small space, or anyone tired of crawling around looking for a nail under the couch. Neodymium Magnetic Wristband

The bottom line

If you're a first-time renter with a small space and a box of unframed pictures, buy two things: the Craftsman 9-Inch Torpedo Level and the OOK Brand 50 lb. Picture Hanging Kit. Together that's typically under $30, and it covers the job completely. Add the hammer if you don't own one, the magnetic wristband if your floor is carpet, and the Bosch bits only if your walls are genuinely hard.

Skip anything that promises laser precision for a picture frame. Your eye is the final judge anyway, and a 9-inch torpedo level gets you close enough that your eye has nothing to complain about.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I really need a level to hang a picture?

For a single small frame, no — a tape measure and a pencil will do. For two or more frames, or anything you want aligned with something else, yes. The level is what stops you from making a second hole, and a second hole means spackle and paint before you move out.

How much does it cost to hang pictures in a rental?

The tools are a one-time cost, typically $15 to $35 for a level plus a hanging kit. The ongoing cost is negligible — a few dollars a year in hooks and nails if you hang things regularly. The real expense is repairing holes at move-out, which is why getting it right the first time matters.

Is a 9-inch level accurate enough for hanging pictures?

Yes. A 9-inch torpedo level is accurate to well within what your eye can detect at picture-hanging distances, and its short length makes it easier to hold flat against a frame than a 24-inch level. Save the longer level for shelving and tile.
