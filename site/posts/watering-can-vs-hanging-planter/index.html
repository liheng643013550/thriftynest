Watering Can vs Hanging Planter: Which Is Worth It?

It's 11:40 p.m. in a 620-square-foot apartment, and you're standing on a step stool with a two-quart watering can tilted over a pothos that hangs at eye level. Water misses the pot, runs down your wrist, and drips onto the rug. Your partner is asleep six feet away. Every trip to refill the can means another round of footsteps, another splash, another chance the plastic handle knocks the curtain rod. That's the moment this decision stops being about plants and starts being about sleep, neighbors, and how much you're willing to spend to stop the dripping.

A watering can suits anyone with plants on shelves, windowsills, or the floor — it's cheap, quiet, and forgiving. A hanging planter suits anyone whose plants live above the furniture line and who wants to water less often and with less mess. The catch for a first apartment is that both choices have a sound profile, and one of them is much friendlier to a shared wall.

Quick verdict

  • You share a bedroom wall with a neighbor and sleep light: go with a watering can. A can makes almost no noise beyond a soft pour — typically quieter than a refrigerator hum. A hanging planter means reaching overhead, and reaching overhead means step stools, rustling leaves, and the occasional thump.
  • Your plants are already hanging and you're tired of the drip: go with a hanging planter that has a built-in reservoir. Fewer refills, fewer trips, less water on the floor.
  • You're furnishing a first apartment on a tight budget and plants are an afterthought: a watering can wins. You can get a solid one for under $30 and it lasts years.

Cost to own

The watering can is the cheaper habit. A basic two-gallon can runs $15–$30, and a well-made one like the Dramm ColorStorm sits at the top of that band. Owner reviews consistently praise its balance and the fact that the rose (the sprinkler head) doesn't fall off mid-pour. The OXO Good Grips 1.75 Gallon is the pick if you have wrist issues — reviewers often mention the angled spout and comfortable handle.

Hanging planters cost less per unit but multiply. Two or three planters at $15–$25 each, plus potting mix, plus a ceiling hook or bracket, and you're at $60–$100 before you've bought a single plant. A bag of Miracle-Gro Potting Mix usually runs $10–$20 depending on size. Add a Golden Pothos or a ZZ Plant and the total climbs fast.

Verdict: Watering can wins on cost. Hanging planters are a decorating decision as much as a gardening one.

Space and setup

A watering can takes up about as much room as a large cereal box. You can tuck it under a sink, on a closet shelf, or beside the trash. For a first apartment with limited storage, that matters.

Hanging planters free up floor and shelf space entirely — a real advantage in a small unit. The trade-off is installation. You need a ceiling joist or a sturdy bracket, a drill, and a landlord who allows holes. Renters often use tension rods or adhesive hooks instead, but those have weight limits, usually 5–10 pounds, and a watered planter can exceed that.

Verdict: Hanging planter wins on floor space, but only if you're allowed to drill. Otherwise the watering can is the practical choice.

Cleaning and maintenance

Watering cans need a rinse every few weeks and a scrub if you've been adding fertilizer. A bottle brush handles the inside. Most are plastic or galvanized metal; the metal ones can rust at the seam if you leave water sitting in them.

Hanging planters are fussier. Drainage is the enemy — most hanging pots either have no drainage hole (risking root rot) or have one that drips onto whatever's below. You'll need a saucer, a liner, or a spot where a little water on the floor is acceptable. Repotting a hanging plant means taking it down, which means a step stool and a free hand.

Verdict: Watering can wins. It's a tool, not a habitat, and it asks almost nothing of you.

Speed and effort

Filling and using a can is a one-trip job for most apartments. A 1.75–2 gallon can waters roughly 8–12 small-to-medium houseplants before a refill. That's typically 1–3 minutes of actual work.

Hanging planters are slower because of the reach. You're lifting a can overhead, which is awkward, or using a step stool, which is slower and riskier. If you have five hanging plants, expect 10–15 minutes on watering day once you count setup and cleanup.

Verdict: Watering can wins on speed, especially for anyone under 5'6" or with shoulder issues.

Noise and disturbance — the factor that matters most here

This is where the two options diverge sharply, and it's the reason a lot of apartment dwellers regret their first hanging planter.

A watering can is quiet. The pour is a soft trickle, typically in the 30–40 decibel range at arm's length — quieter than a dishwasher and well below the 50 dB that most apartment leases treat as a nighttime nuisance threshold. You can water at midnight and no one will know.

Hanging planters introduce noise in three ways. First, the reach: step stools creak, and a metal stool on a hardwood floor makes a sharp tap. Second, the plant itself: leaves rustle against a wall or window, and a lightweight plastic pot swings and knocks against the ceiling or a curtain rod. Third, the drip: water hitting a saucer or a hardwood floor is a surprisingly sharp sound in a quiet room, and it's the kind of irregular noise that wakes light sleepers more reliably than steady sound.

Owner reviews of hanging planters frequently mention "swinging" and "knocking" as minor annoyances. In a shared-wall apartment, those minor annoyances become your neighbor's problem at 11 p.m.

Verdict: Watering can wins clearly. If noise and neighbor relations are your priority, this is the deciding factor.

Versatility

A watering can works for every plant you own — floor plants, shelf plants, hanging plants, seedlings. It's the one tool that covers all of them. A can with a removable rose (like the Dramm ColorStorm) handles both gentle watering for seedlings and a direct pour for larger pots.

Hanging planters are specialized. They suit trailing plants — pothos, string of pearls, ivy — and little else. A Snake Plant Laurentii or a Cast Iron Plant will survive in one, but they don't need the height. A Spathiphyllum (Peace Lily) droops dramatically when thirsty, which is hard to see from below.

Verdict: Watering can wins. It's the more useful purchase for a first apartment.

When to choose each one

Choose a watering can if: you rent and can't drill, you sleep light or share a wall, you have plants at multiple heights, or you want one purchase that solves the problem for under $40. The Dramm ColorStorm and OXO Good Grips 1.75 Gallon are both solid picks at that price.

Choose a hanging planter if: you have ceiling hooks already installed, your plants are trailing varieties, you want floor space back, and you're willing to accept a little drip and rustle. Pair it with a can for refilling — the two aren't mutually exclusive.

The bottom line

For a couple in a first apartment, especially one with a shared wall and a light sleeper, the watering can is the better first purchase. It's cheaper, quieter, more versatile, and easier to clean. A hanging planter is a nice upgrade once you've got the basics covered and you know your ceiling can take the weight — but it's a decorating choice, not a noise-friendly one. If you buy one thing this month, buy the can.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a watering can quieter than a hanging planter?

Yes. A watering can's pour is typically around 30–40 decibels at arm's length, well below the 50 dB most leases treat as a nighttime nuisance threshold. Hanging planters add step-stool creaks, rustling leaves, and dripping water — irregular sounds that tend to wake light sleepers.

How much does a watering can cost compared to a hanging planter?

A watering can typically runs $15–$40, with the Dramm ColorStorm and OXO Good Grips 1.75 Gallon at the upper end of that range. Hanging planters cost $12–$35 each, but you'll likely buy several, plus potting mix at $10–$20 a bag, so the total usually lands higher.

Can I use a watering can for hanging plants?

Yes. A 1.75–2 gallon can waters roughly 8–12 small-to-medium houseplants before a refill, and it works for hanging pots as long as you can reach them safely. Many people keep a hanging planter and a can together rather than choosing one.
