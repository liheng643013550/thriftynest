How to Clean an Oven Without Chemicals (The Baking Soda Paste)

Opening the oven door to a crusted-on mess from last month’s lasagna is never fun. The good news is that you don’t need a can of toxic foam to fix it. A simple paste of baking soda and water handles the job just as well, and it costs about 50 cents per use.

Why Skip Chemical Cleaners?

Most commercial oven cleaners rely on sodium hydroxide (lye) or harsh solvents that eat through grease. They work fast, but they come with real downsides. The fumes are strong enough to make your eyes water, and they linger in the air for hours. If you have kids or pets, that is a serious concern.

Residue is another problem. Even after a thorough rinse, chemical cleaners can leave a film that burns off the next time you preheat. That smell is not just unpleasant—it is you inhaling whatever was left behind.

Baking soda and water is the opposite. It is non-toxic, cheap, and safe to touch. It does not dissolve grease on contact like a solvent, but it does lift and loosen grime with a bit of time. I find that letting the paste sit overnight handles even the worst baked-on spills, which means I just plan ahead and let it work while I sleep.

What You Need to Get Started

The beauty of this method is that you probably have everything already. Here is the full shopping list:

  • Baking soda (a fresh box, about 16 ounces, is plenty)
  • Water
  • A spray bottle filled with plain water
  • A sponge or soft cloth
  • A plastic scraper or old credit card (metal can scratch)
  • Rubber gloves, if you want to keep your hands clean
  • White vinegar, for the final rinse
  • A microfiber cloth for drying

That is it. No special tools, no expensive sprays. If you want to make the paste application easier, a silicone brush helps spread it into corners, but a spoon works fine too.

Step 1: Remove the Racks and Loose Debris

Start by taking out the oven racks and setting them aside. You will clean them separately in the sink. Then, use a dry paper towel or a brush to sweep out any loose crumbs, burnt bits, or food chunks from the bottom of the oven. This step is quick, but it makes a big difference. You want the paste to touch the cooked-on grime directly, not a layer of loose crumbs.

If your oven has a removable bottom panel, take that out too. Many models have one, and it is usually the dirtiest part. Owner reports describe learning this the hard way—an hour spent scrubbing the top of the panel before realizing it lifted right out.

Step 2: Mix the Baking Soda Paste

The ratio is simple: about 1/2 cup of baking soda with 3 to 4 tablespoons of water. Stir it in a small bowl until it forms a thick, spreadable paste. It should be the consistency of frosting, not soup. If it is too runny, add more baking soda. If it is too dry and crumbly, add a few drops of water.

Published guidance indicates that a fresh box of baking soda works better than an old one that has been sitting in the fridge absorbing odors. The paste spreads more smoothly and clings to vertical surfaces better when the soda is fresh.

Step 3: Spread the Paste Inside the Oven

Using a sponge or your gloved fingers, spread the paste all over the interior surfaces. Focus on the bottom, the sides, and the back wall. Do not forget the door, including the glass. The paste should be thick enough to cover the grime fully—about 1/8 inch deep is ideal.

You do not need to scrub at this point. Just coat it and walk away. The baking soda does the heavy lifting over time. If you have a particularly greasy spot, press a little extra paste onto it.

Here is a tip I picked up from a friend who runs a small bakery: for vertical surfaces, the paste can slide off. To prevent that, wipe the surface with a damp cloth first so the paste has something to grip.

Step 4: Let It Sit for 12 to 24 Hours

This is the step most people skip, and it is the most important. The paste needs time to break down the carbonized grease. I usually apply it in the evening and leave it overnight. By morning, the grime has softened to the point where it wipes away with minimal effort.

If you are in a hurry, you can speed things up by turning the oven on warm (not above 200°F) for 15 minutes before applying the paste. The warmth helps the baking soda work faster, but do not leave the oven on while the paste sits—that just bakes the paste on and makes things worse.

For a very dirty oven, I have let the paste sit for a full 24 hours. The difference is night and day. The baked-on grease turns into a crumbly paste that lifts right off.

Step 5: Scrub, Wipe, and Rinse with Vinegar

After the wait, it is time to clean up. Dampen a sponge with water and start wiping the paste off. You will see the grime come up with it. For stubborn spots, use a plastic scraper to gently lift the softened mess. Do not press too hard—the goal is to remove the grime, not scratch the enamel.

Once you have wiped away the bulk of the paste, fill a spray bottle with white vinegar and mist the interior. The vinegar reacts with the baking soda residue, creating a gentle fizz that lifts the last bits. Then, wipe everything down with a clean, damp cloth.

Finally, go over the surfaces with a dry microfiber cloth. This removes any lingering streaks and leaves the oven looking new. I always do a final pass with warm water to make sure no vinegar smell remains.

How to Clean the Oven Racks

The racks are the easiest part. Fill your sink or a large tub with hot water and add about 1/2 cup of baking soda. Submerge the racks and let them soak for at least 30 minutes, or a few hours for heavily soiled ones.

After soaking, the grime should slide off with a sponge. For stuck-on bits, make a paste of baking soda and water, apply it to the spots, and scrub with a non-abrasive pad. Rinse thoroughly and dry before putting them back.

One warning: do not put oven racks in the dishwasher. The heat and detergent can strip the coating and cause rust. I ruined a set of racks this way once, and the replacement cost me $40. Soaking by hand is slower but worth it.

The Bottom Line

Cleaning an oven without chemicals is not a quick trick, but it is simple, cheap, and safe. For under a dollar in baking soda, you get the same result as a $6 can of caustic spray, minus the fumes and the worry about what you are breathing in.

The key is patience. Let the paste do its work overnight, and the scrubbing becomes a light wipe-down instead of an arm workout. This method has been used on every oven from a basic rental unit to a current gas range, according to owner reports, and it has never been reported to let anyone down. Give it a try next time an oven looks like a crime scene—the results might be surprising.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does this method cost compared to a store-bought oven cleaner?

A batch of baking soda paste runs roughly 50 cents per use, while a can of caustic spray typically costs about $6. The whole process stays under a dollar in baking soda, and it avoids the fumes that come with solvent-based cleaners.

How long does the paste need to stay on the oven walls?

Plan on 12 to 24 hours for the paste to break down carbonized grease, with overnight being the most common approach. If time is short, warming the oven to no more than 200°F for 15 minutes before applying the paste can help it work faster, but the oven should be off while the paste sits.

Can oven racks go in the dishwasher instead of being soaked?

No. The heat and detergent in a dishwasher can strip the racks' coating and lead to rust, and replacing a set can cost around $40. Soaking them in hot water with about 1/2 cup of baking soda for at least 30 minutes, then scrubbing with a non-abrasive pad, is the safer route.
