A Reality Check Before You Spend Anything

If you're heading out of town for a week or two, you don't need to spend $150 on a smart irrigation system for six houseplants. You also can't expect a $12 gadget to keep a thirsty fern alive for a month in a sunny window. The honest middle ground: most cheap watering solutions cost somewhere between $10 and $40, work fine for 5 to 14 days, and fail badly if you set them up the night before you leave.

For a first apartment, the real constraint isn't money — it's floor space. You're probably working with a windowsill, a corner shelf, or a balcony that doubles as storage. So this guide weights every option by how much room it eats, not just what it costs.

First, Figure Out What Your Plants Actually Need

Before buying anything, sort your plants into rough categories. This takes ten minutes and saves you from buying the wrong thing.

  • Thirsty, dramatic plants — peace lilies, ferns, most tropicals. These wilt within days of drying out and need steady moisture.
  • Forgiving succulents and tough houseplants — snake plants, ZZ plants, pothos. These can handle 10 to 14 days dry without much drama.
  • Anything in a tiny pot — dries out fastest. Small soil volume means small water reserve.

If your collection is mostly in that second group, you may not need a device at all. A good soak the morning you leave plus moving pots out of direct sun can carry them a week or more. Owner reviews of low-maintenance plants like the Snake Plant Laurentii and ZZ Plant consistently mention going two weeks or longer between waterings, which is exactly why they're popular with renters who travel.

The Cheapest Options Cost Almost Nothing

The bathtub trick

Line your plants up in the bathtub or a large plastic tub, water them thoroughly, and leave them. The tub catches runoff, humidity builds in the enclosed space, and evaporation slows down. Cost: $0. Footprint: your bathtub, which you're not using anyway.

The catch is drainage. Pots sitting in standing water for two weeks can rot roots. Put a folded towel or a few jar lids under each pot to lift it above the water line.

The string wick method

Cut a length of cotton string or braided twine, bury one end an inch or two into the soil, and drop the other end into a jar or bucket of water placed higher than the pot. Capillary action pulls water through slowly. Cost: a few dollars for string. Footprint: one jar per plant, which adds up fast on a small shelf.

This works well for 5 to 7 days. Beyond that, the string can clog or the water runs out. It's also fussy to set up — plan on testing it for two days before you leave.

Grouping and mulching

Push all your pots together into one cluster, away from windows and vents. Add a half-inch layer of mulch or even a sheet of cardboard over the soil surface to cut evaporation. Free, and it can stretch your window by several days.

Self-Watering Pots and Reservoirs: The Best Small-Space Pick

If you want something that actually works without daily fiddling, a self-watering pot with a built-in reservoir is the sweet spot for an apartment. You fill a chamber at the bottom, and the soil wicks moisture up as needed. Most hold enough for 7 to 14 days depending on plant size and room temperature.

Footprint is the selling point here. A self-watering pot replaces the pot you already have — it doesn't add a second object. No tubes across the floor, no buckets on the counter.

Look for these specs when shopping:

Feature What to look for
Reservoir capacity Roughly 1 to 2 quarts for a 6-inch pot
Water level indicator Lets you check without lifting the plant
Drainage overflow Prevents drowning if you overfill
Material Thick plastic or glazed ceramic holds moisture better than thin terracotta

Prices for a basic self-watering insert or pot typically run $8 to $25. That's cheaper than most drip kits and takes up less room than a bucket-and-tube setup.

Drip Spikes and Glass Globes: Cheap, But Read the Reviews

The classic terracotta spike or glass globe stuck in the soil is the cheapest commercial option, usually $8 to $15 for a multi-pack. You fill it, invert it, and it slowly releases water.

Here's the honest trade-off. Owner reviews are genuinely mixed. Many people report they work fine for 3 to 5 days in a medium pot. Others report the water dumps out in a day, or that the spike clogs with soil and releases nothing. Performance depends heavily on soil type, pot size, and how tightly you pack the spike in.

For a small apartment, the appeal is obvious — they're tiny and stack in a drawer. Just don't count on them for a two-week trip. Use them as a backup to a proper soak, not a replacement.

DIY Drip From a Bottle or Bag

A plastic bottle with a pinhole in the cap, inverted into the soil, is the zero-cost version of a drip spike. Same reliability caveats apply, and you need to test the hole size — too big and it empties in hours.

A step up: a resealable plastic bag filled with water, poked with a few tiny holes, and laid on the soil surface. It releases slowly and takes up no vertical space, which matters if your plants live on a narrow windowsill. Cost: pennies. Reliability: fine for about a week.

When It's Worth Buying a Real Drip Kit

If you're leaving for two weeks or more, or you have 10-plus plants, a proper drip system starts to make sense. These use a small pump or a gravity-fed reservoir with tubing and emitters.

Two honest warnings for apartment dwellers. First, footprint: even a small kit means a reservoir bucket, tubing running between pots, and a power outlet if it's pump-driven. That's a lot of visual clutter in a 600-square-foot space. Second, cost: budget gravity kits usually start around $25 to $40, and pump-driven models run higher.

If you go this route, a simple watering can makes filling the reservoir far less messy than a pitcher. It also stores upright in a closet, so it isn't a permanent fixture in your living room.

For most first apartments, though, a drip kit is overkill. Spend the money on self-watering pots instead.

Set It Up Right, or None of It Works

Whatever method you pick, the setup matters more than the gear.

  • Water thoroughly the day before, not the morning you leave. Let pots drain so you're not leaving them sitting in a puddle.
  • Move everything out of direct sun and away from heat vents and AC drafts. Cooler, shadier spots lose water far more slowly.
  • Test your system for 48 hours before a real trip. Every method here has failure modes, and you want to find them at home.
  • Cluster your plants together. Grouped pots create their own humid microclimate.
  • Skip the fertilizer. You don't want to push new growth while you're gone.

One more apartment-specific note: if your plants live on a balcony or in a sunroom, heat swings will drain any reservoir much faster than an indoor setup. Budget for roughly half the advertised duration in a hot, bright space.

The Bottom Line

For a first apartment and a trip of a week or two, the cheapest reliable answer is usually a thorough soak, a move out of direct sun, and a self-watering pot with a 1-to-2-quart reservoir for your thirstiest plants. That combination typically costs under $30 total and adds zero clutter, because the reservoir replaces a pot you already own.

Skip the drip kits unless you're gone longer than two weeks or you're watering a small jungle. And skip the glass globes as your only plan — they're a nice backup, not a solution. Test everything two days early, and you'll come home to plants that look like you never left.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long can self-watering pots keep plants alive while I'm away?

Most hold enough water for roughly 7 to 14 days, depending on plant size and room temperature. In a hot, bright space, expect closer to the low end of that range.

Are drip spikes or glass globes worth buying?

They're cheap, usually $8 to $15 for a multi-pack, and they take up almost no space. But owner reviews are mixed — some report 3 to 5 days of steady release, others say the water dumps out in a day. Treat them as a backup, not your main plan.

What's the cheapest way to water plants during a two-week trip?

A thorough soak the day before you leave, plants clustered away from sun and vents, plus a self-watering pot for your thirstiest plants. That setup typically costs under $30 and takes up no extra floor space.
