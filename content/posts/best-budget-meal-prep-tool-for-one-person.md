For two people setting up a first apartment, the meal prep question that decides everything is simple: how much does it actually cost to run this thing every month? A $30 appliance that pulls 1,000 watts for an hour a day can quietly add more to your electric bill than the gadget itself cost. This guide focuses on tools that are cheap to buy and cheap to live with, sized for one person so you're not cooking for four or cleaning a giant pot every night.

## What actually matters

- Running cost per month, not sticker price. Most small kitchen appliances pull somewhere between 300 and 1,500 watts. At a typical US rate of roughly 12 to 17 cents per kilowatt-hour, a 700-watt appliance used 30 minutes a day costs somewhere around $1.50 to $3 a month. A 1,500-watt one used an hour a day can run $6 to $8. Check the wattage on the label before you fall in love with the shape.
- Cooking time as an energy multiplier. A tool that finishes rice in 20 minutes beats one that takes 45, even at the same wattage, because you're paying for minutes, not for the appliance.
- Capacity matched to one person. A 3-quart pot for one person is generous. A 6-quart is a batch-cooking tool, not a nightly one. Oversized means longer heat-up times and more energy wasted heating empty space.
- Counter and cabinet footprint. In a first apartment, the tool you can leave out gets used. The one buried behind the plates does not.
- Cleanup effort. Nonstick and dishwasher-safe parts get used more often than anything that needs scrubbing. Owner reviews consistently flag cleanup as the reason a tool gets abandoned.
- Whether it replaces another appliance. A rice cooker that also steams vegetables replaces a pot and a steamer basket. Fewer appliances plugged in means a lower baseline draw.

## Aroma Housewares 6-Cup

Rough price band: typically $25 to $40.

This is the classic one-to-three-person rice cooker, and the 6-cup cooked capacity is right for a couple who eats rice a few times a week. Specs put it in the low-wattage bracket for a cooker, and a batch of white rice usually finishes in about 20 to 30 minutes, which keeps the energy math friendly. It comes with a steamer tray, so you can cook rice below and vegetables above in the same cycle.

Downsides: the nonstick inner pot is thin, and owner reviews often mention it scratching if you use metal utensils. It's not a pressure cooker, so beans and brown rice take considerably longer.

Who should buy it: a couple who wants cheap rice and steamed veg with almost no learning curve, and who doesn't need a multi-function machine.

[Aroma Housewares 6-Cup](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08KFPZ1MF?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__)

## Dash Mini Rice Cooker

Rough price band: usually $20 to $30.

If counter space is the real constraint, this is the smallest sensible option. It's a 2-cup-class cooker, which genuinely suits one person or two light eaters, and its low wattage makes it one of the cheapest things in this list to run — think well under $2 a month at typical use. It also does oatmeal and simple soups.

Downsides: the capacity is genuinely small. Cook for two hungry people and you'll be running two cycles, which wipes out the energy advantage. Owner reviews frequently note the pot is best hand-washed.

Who should buy it: one person, or a couple who mostly cooks single portions and values counter space over batch cooking.

[Dash Mini Rice Cooker](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FT46M9XR?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__)

## Crock-Pot Artisan 5-Quart Enameled Cast Iron Dutch Oven

Rough price band: typically $50 to $80.

Cast iron is the opposite of energy-efficient — it takes a long time to heat up — but it holds heat so well that you can often drop the burner to low and let it coast. For a couple, 5 quarts means a big batch of chili or soup on Sunday that covers three or four meals. That batch-cooking pattern is where the running cost per serving actually gets low.

Downsides: it's heavy, it needs hand washing, and it's slow to heat. If you're cooking one portion of pasta, this is the wrong tool.

Who should buy it: a couple who cooks in batches and wants one pot that goes from stovetop to oven.

[Crock-Pot Artisan 5-Quart Enameled Cast Iron Dutch Oven](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01FJPWNKA?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__)

## Dash Compact Air Fryer

Rough price band: usually $40 to $60.

Air fryers are the appliance people most often ask about for energy reasons, and the compact models are the honest answer. A small air fryer typically pulls in the 1,000 to 1,300 watt range and runs for 10 to 20 minutes per batch, which usually works out cheaper than heating a full-size oven for the same job. This compact model is sized for one or two portions.

Downsides: the basket is small, so a couple cooking a full dinner will do two rounds. Owner reviews often mention that frozen foods need shaking halfway for even results.

Who should buy it: a couple who reheats and crisps more than they roast, and who wants to stop firing up a full oven for two chicken thighs.

[Dash Compact Air Fryer](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B075BCVSJZ?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__)

## Black+Decker Crisp 'N Bake

Rough price band: typically $70 to $110.

This is the step up if you want one appliance that air fries and toasts. It's a toaster-oven format with air-fry capability, which means it replaces two countertop appliances rather than adding one. For a first apartment, that consolidation matters — fewer things plugged in, less cabinet space consumed.

Downsides: it draws more than the compact air fryer, and it takes up more counter depth. Preheating adds a couple of minutes to every session.

Who should buy it: a couple who wants toast, bagels, and air-fried food from one machine and has the counter space for it.

[Black+Decker Crisp 'N Bake](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CXF9LRQK?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__)

## Cuisinart Smart Stick CSB-75

Rough price band: usually $35 to $55.

An immersion blender is the sleeper pick for one-person meal prep. It runs for seconds at a time, not minutes, so its energy cost is close to negligible. You can blend soup directly in the pot, make a smoothie in a cup, or whip eggs without dragging out a full blender. For two people, it handles almost everything a countertop blender would.

Downsides: it won't crush ice or handle large batches. Owner reviews sometimes note the motor is best used in short bursts rather than continuous heavy blending.

Who should buy it: anyone who makes soup, sauces, or smoothies regularly and wants to skip a bulky blender.

[Cuisinart Smart Stick CSB-75](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00ARQVLX2?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__)

## Aroma Digital 8-Cup

Rough price band: typically $35 to $55.

If you want rice plus a bit more flexibility, the 8-cup digital model covers a couple comfortably with leftovers. The digital controls include a delay timer, which is genuinely useful: you can set it before work and come home to cooked rice. Wattage is still modest, and a typical cycle runs 30 to 40 minutes.

Downsides: 8 cups is more capacity than two people need nightly, so you'll often be cooking a batch and refrigerating the rest. The digital panel is one more thing that can fail versus a simple switch.

Who should buy it: a couple who eats rice often and wants leftovers without standing at the stove.

[Aroma Digital 8-Cup](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B007WQ9YNO?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__)

## Amazon Basics Digital Kitchen Scale

Rough price band: usually $10 to $15.

Not an appliance that draws meaningful power — it runs on batteries — but it's the cheapest way to stop overbuying and overcooking. Portioning rice, pasta, and meat by weight means you cook exactly what two people eat, which cuts both food waste and the energy spent cooking extra.

Downsides: it's basic. No app, no fancy features, and the platform is small for large bowls.

Who should buy it: every first apartment, honestly. It's the lowest-cost item here and it changes how you cook.

## The bottom line

For a first apartment, buy in this order. Start with the scale — it costs about as much as lunch and it stops you cooking more than you'll eat. Add a rice cooker sized for two; the Aroma Housewares 6-Cup is the safe default, and the Dash Mini Rice Cooker is the pick if your counter is genuinely tiny. Then add one cooking appliance based on how you actually eat: the Dash Compact Air Fryer if you reheat and crisp, the Black+Decker Crisp 'N Bake if you also want toast, or the Crock-Pot Artisan 5-Quart Enameled Cast Iron Dutch Oven if you batch cook on weekends. The immersion blender and the digital rice cooker are upgrades you add later, once you know which one you'll actually use.

On running cost, the pattern is consistent: low-wattage tools used for short bursts are the cheapest to live with, and batch cooking is what makes a higher-wattage appliance worth it. You're not saving money by buying the cheapest box — you're saving it by buying the tool that matches how often you cook.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to run a small kitchen appliance each month?

It depends on wattage and minutes used, but most small appliances in this list land somewhere around $1.50 to $3 a month at typical use, based on a rate of roughly 12 to 17 cents per kilowatt-hour. Higher-wattage tools like the Black+Decker Crisp 'N Bake cost more per session than the Dash Mini Rice Cooker.

### Which of these is cheapest to run?

The Amazon Basics Digital Kitchen Scale runs on batteries and draws essentially nothing, and the Cuisinart Smart Stick CSB-75 runs for seconds at a time. Among the cooking appliances, the Dash Mini Rice Cooker and Aroma Housewares 6-Cup are the lowest-wattage options.

### Do I need a full-size oven if I have an air fryer?

No, not for two people. The Dash Compact Air Fryer and Black+Decker Crisp 'N Bake both handle reheating, crisping, and small-batch roasting, usually for less energy than heating a full oven. You'll still want the oven for large trays, whole birds, or big batches.
