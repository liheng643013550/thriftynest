Downsizing into a smaller home usually means a smaller yard, and a smaller yard usually means raised beds instead of a full garden plot. The moment you sketch out those beds, one number decides the whole project: how much does it cost to fill them with soil? I'll give you the real ranges, the formula behind them, and the honest trade-offs. No vague "it depends" — actual dollar figures you can plug into your own plan.

## The quick answer

Filling a raised bed typically costs somewhere between $8 and $20 per cubic foot of volume if you buy bagged soil, and roughly $2 to $6 per cubic foot if you buy in bulk by the cubic yard. For a common 4 ft x 8 ft bed that's 10 to 12 inches deep — about 1 cubic yard of volume — expect to spend somewhere in the range of $150 to $500, depending on what you fill it with and whether you buy bags or bulk.

That's a wide spread, and the width is the whole point. Two gardeners can fill identical beds and one pays $120 while the other pays $600. Here's how that happens.

## How the number is built

The math is simple volume, then volume times price. In plain words:

Length x width x depth (all in feet) = cubic feet of soil you need.

A 4 ft x 8 ft bed is 32 square feet. At 12 inches deep, that's 32 cubic feet. Since there are 27 cubic feet in a cubic yard, that bed needs about 1.2 cubic yards — most people round to "a yard and a bit."

Then you multiply by your cost per unit:

- Bagged soil is usually sold in 1, 1.5, or 2 cubic foot bags. At typical big-box prices, that works out to roughly $3 to $8 per cubic foot, or higher for premium blends.
- Bulk soil (often called "garden mix" or "screened topsoil") is sold by the cubic yard and typically runs $30 to $80 per yard delivered, though delivery fees and minimum orders change that fast.

So the same 32 cubic foot bed costs about $100 to $250 in bags, or about $60 to $120 in bulk. Same bed, same dirt, very different receipt.

One trap to avoid: filling the entire bed with expensive potting mix. Most experienced growers fill the bottom third with cheaper topsoil or compost and save the premium stuff for the top 4 to 6 inches where roots actually feed. That single move often cuts the bill by a third or more.

## What pushes the cost up

Depth is the biggest lever. Going from 10 inches to 16 inches increases your soil volume by roughly 60 percent, and the cost scales right along with it. Deep beds are great for carrots and potatoes, but if you're growing lettuce and herbs in a downsized yard, 10 to 12 inches is plenty for most crops.

Bagged convenience is the second lever. Every bag you carry is a labor and packaging cost baked into the price. If you can accept a pallet delivery or a dump truck in the driveway, bulk almost always wins on price per cubic foot.

Premium amendments push it further. Blends that already contain compost, peat, perlite, and slow-release fertilizer cost noticeably more than plain topsoil. That's not wasted money — but it's a choice, not a requirement.

And if you're filling a small greenhouse or cold-frame setup rather than open beds, your volume per square foot goes up because you're often using deeper containers. Products like the [Outsunny Walk-In Greenhouse](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DP9KFZVK?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) are typically used with pots or grow bags rather than filled solid, which keeps soil costs down — you're filling containers, not the footprint.

## What brings the cost down

Buying in bulk is the single biggest saver. A cubic yard of decent garden mix delivered often lands at $40 to $70 in many markets, which is roughly a third of what the same volume costs in 1 cubic foot bags.

Blending your own is the second. Plain topsoil plus your own compost plus a bag or two of a quality potting mix to lighten the texture usually costs less than a pre-mixed raised bed blend, and you control what's in it. A product like [Miracle-Gro Potting Mix](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B087F7GNFG?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) is a reasonable choice for that top layer, since it's widely available and reviewers consistently describe it as a dependable general-purpose mix.

Filling the bottom with free or cheap material is the third. Logs, branches, cardboard, and yard waste at the base of a deep bed — often called hugelkultur or just "core filling" — displace soil you'd otherwise pay for. It settles over a season or two, so plan to top off the following year, but the upfront savings are real.

Finally, check local sources. Municipal compost programs, landscape supply yards, and even some farms sell screened compost cheaply by the truckload. Prices vary a lot by region, so a phone call beats a guess.

Most of the gear in this category is about moving and mixing soil, not the soil itself, but a few items genuinely change the math.

It's not the cheapest per cubic foot, but it's consistent, and for a downsized garden where you're filling a few pots and the top few inches of a bed, consistency matters more than bulk pricing.

## The bottom line

Filling a raised bed costs most people somewhere between $60 and $250 for a standard 4 ft x 8 ft bed, and the difference almost always comes down to bags versus bulk and how deep you go. In a smaller yard, you're usually filling fewer beds, so it's worth doing the volume math once and then buying the cheapest source that still gives you decent quality.

Three concrete ways to pay less: buy bulk instead of bags wherever delivery is possible, fill the bottom third of deep beds with yard waste or cheap topsoil, and make your own compost so next year's top-off is free. Do those three things and a downsized garden can be filled for well under $150.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much soil does a 4 ft x 8 ft raised bed need?

At 10 inches deep, that bed holds about 27 cubic feet, or roughly 1 cubic yard. At 12 inches deep it's about 32 cubic feet, and at 16 inches it climbs to roughly 43 cubic feet. Most standard beds land between 27 and 43 cubic feet depending on depth.

### Is bagged soil or bulk soil cheaper for filling a raised bed?

Bulk is almost always cheaper per cubic foot. Bagged soil typically runs $3 to $8 per cubic foot, while bulk garden mix sold by the cubic yard often works out to roughly $2 to $6 per cubic foot once delivery is factored in. For a full 4 ft x 8 ft bed, that difference is often $50 to $100.

### Can I fill the bottom of a raised bed with something other than soil?

Yes. Logs, branches, cardboard, and yard waste at the base of a deep bed displace soil you'd otherwise buy, and the cost can drop to $50 to $110 for a 4 ft x 8 ft bed. Expect the material to settle over a season or two, so plan on topping off the following year with compost or a mix like Miracle-Gro Potting Mix.
