Setting up a first apartment usually means a short list of furniture to assemble, a few shelves to hang, and maybe a wobbly IKEA dresser that needs a brace. At that point, the saw question comes up fast: buy a cheap hand saw and save money, or spend more on a circular saw and save time?

If you only cut a few pieces of trim or a couple of 2x4s a year, a hand saw is usually enough. If you're planning to build shelves, cut plywood, or take on weekend projects regularly, a circular saw tends to pay for itself in time saved. The choice matters most when you look at the total cost over the first year, not just the sticker price.

## The biggest mistake beginners make

The single most common mistake is buying the saw before buying the blade. A circular saw with the stock blade that comes in the box will cut, but on plywood and 2x4s it often burns, binds, and leaves splintered edges. A $12 to $20 replacement blade can change the whole experience.

The second mistake is the opposite: spending $150 or more on a circular saw that gets used three times in twelve months, then sits on a garage shelf while the battery slowly dies. For a couple in a first apartment, the honest question isn't "which saw is better" but "how many cuts will I actually make this year."

## Quick verdict

- Small apartment, occasional furniture assembly and trim work: buy a quality hand saw. A Stanley FatMax Hand Saw covers most first-year jobs for under the cost of a single circular saw blade set.
- Planning to build shelves, a desk, or cut plywood: buy a corded circular saw. A DeWalt DWE575 with a decent 24-tooth blade handles sheet goods cleanly.
- Cordless convenience matters and you already own a battery platform: a Makita 5007MG or a comparable corded model is still the better first buy than a bare cordless tool with no batteries.

## Cost to own over the first year

This is where the two options separate most clearly. A quality hand saw like a Stanley FatMax Hand Saw typically runs $20–$35, and most owners report the original blade lasting well beyond a year of light use. Total first-year cost: roughly $20–$40 including a cheap sharpening file.

A corded circular saw like the DeWalt DWE575 usually lands between $90 and $130. Add one or two blades at $15–$25 each, plus a $10 extension cord, and you're looking at $130–$180 in year one. Cordless models like the Makita 5007MG push higher once you factor in batteries and a charger, often $200–$300 for a full kit.

Verdict: if cost is the deciding factor, the hand saw wins by a wide margin in year one. If you're cutting more than about 20 pieces of lumber, the circular saw's time savings start to justify the gap.

## Space and storage in a small apartment

Hand saws are about 20–26 inches long and an inch thick. They hang on a nail, slide behind a door, or sit in a drawer. For a couple in 600 square feet, that matters.

Circular saws are more awkward. A corded model is roughly 12–14 inches long with a cord that needs coiling. Cordless versions need a charger and spare batteries, which adds a small shelf or bin. Owner reviews frequently mention that cords tangle and cases take up more room than expected.

Verdict: hand saw wins for tight spaces. If you buy a circular saw, plan where it lives before it arrives.

## Cleaning, dust, and mess

Neither saw is clean, but they fail differently. Hand saws produce coarse sawdust that mostly falls straight down. A circular saw throws fine dust backward and sideways, and without a shop vac or dust bag attachment, it coats a room fast. Many circular saws have a dust port, but the included bag on budget models fills quickly.

Verdict: hand saw is easier to contain indoors. If you cut inside with a circular saw, tape a plastic sheet around the work area and open a window.

## Speed and quality of cut

A hand saw on a 2x4 takes most beginners one to two minutes per cut, and the first few cuts are usually crooked. Technique takes practice; a miter box helps.

A circular saw cuts the same 2x4 in seconds. With a sharp 24-tooth framing blade, edges are cleaner and repeatable, which matters if you're building shelves that need to match. Reviewers often note that a circular saw plus a straight edge gives near-table-saw results on plywood.

Verdict: circular saw wins clearly on speed and repeatability. If your project has more than a handful of cuts, this is the factor that tips the decision.

## Noise and apartment living

Hand saws are quiet. You can cut at 9 p.m. without a complaint.

Circular saws typically run above 90 dB at the operator's ear. That's loud enough to annoy neighbors through shared walls and to require hearing protection. Cordless models are not noticeably quieter than corded ones; the motor noise is similar.

Verdict: if you live in an apartment with thin walls or quiet hours, the hand saw is the more considerate tool. Save the circular saw for daytime weekend work.

## Versatility and what you can actually build

Hand saws handle crosscuts and simple angles. They struggle with long rip cuts in plywood and can't easily cut a bevel.

Circular saws adjust depth and bevel, handle sheet goods, and pair with a straight edge or track for long cuts. The DeWalt DWE575 and similar models bevel up to 45 degrees, which is enough for most beginner furniture and shelving.

Verdict: circular saw wins on range. If your project list includes plywood, bevels, or anything larger than a 2x4, this is the deciding factor.

## When to choose each one

Choose a hand saw if you're furnishing a first apartment, cutting occasional trim, assembling flat-pack furniture, or working in a space where noise is a problem. A Stanley FatMax Hand Saw plus a miter box covers most of it for under $50 total.

Choose a circular saw if you're building shelves, a desk, a headboard, or anything involving plywood or repeated cuts. Budget for a good blade on day one, not the stock one. The DeWalt DWE575 is a common first pick, and the Makita 5007MG is worth the step up if you expect years of use.

If you can only buy one and you're genuinely unsure, start with the hand saw. It costs less than a single circular saw blade replacement, and it will tell you quickly whether you enjoy cutting wood at all.

## The bottom line

For a couple in a first apartment, the hand saw is the smarter first purchase in most cases. It costs a fraction of a circular saw, stores anywhere, stays quiet, and handles furniture assembly and light trim without fuss. The circular saw earns its place the moment your projects involve sheet goods, repeated cuts, or anything you want to look square and consistent. If you expect that to happen within the first year, buy the circular saw now and budget for a real blade. If not, start with the hand saw and add the circular saw later when a specific project demands it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I cut plywood with a hand saw?

Yes, but it's slow and hard to keep straight. A hand saw with a fine-tooth blade works for small pieces; for full sheets, a circular saw with a straight edge is far easier and gives cleaner edges.

### Do I need a circular saw if I already own a hand saw?

Only if your projects involve repeated cuts, sheet goods, or bevels. For occasional trim and furniture assembly, a hand saw covers it, and the circular saw mostly sits unused.

### What blade should a beginner put on a circular saw?

A 24-tooth carbide blade is the usual starting point for framing lumber and general cuts. For plywood and finish work, a 40-tooth or higher blade leaves cleaner edges. The stock blade that ships in the box is usually the weakest part of the tool.
