You have a kitchen counter with a coffee maker, a toaster, a phone charger, and a slow cooker all fighting for the same two wall outlets. The cheap power strip you grabbed at the grocery store is already yellowing, the switch feels mushy, and one outlet has stopped gripping plugs altogether. You do not want to spend $40 on a strip — you want the one that will not burn your house down and will still work two years from now.

That is the real question at this price: not which strip has the fanciest features, but which one survives. Here is what actually goes wrong on budget strips, and which specific models hold up.

## What actually matters

- Surge protection rating in joules. This is the total energy the strip can absorb before the protection is used up. Budget strips usually run 600 to 1,200 joules. Under about 400 joules, you are mostly buying a fancy extension cord.
- Clamping voltage. Typically 330V to 500V. Lower is better because it means the strip reacts sooner. Many cheap strips do not publish this number at all, which tells you something.
- UL listing. Look for a UL 1449 listing on the label (or ETL). This is the difference between a surge protector and a plastic bar with outlets.
- Outlet grip and spacing. Wide-spaced outlets fit bulky adapters. A strip that cannot hold a plug firmly is a fire risk, not just an annoyance.
- Switch and cord quality. The rocker switch and the strain relief where the cord enters the housing are the two parts that fail first on cheap strips. Owner reviews mention both constantly.
- Realistic wattage. A 15-amp strip on a 120V circuit is rated around 1,800 watts total. Do not stack a space heater and a microwave on the same strip.

## What fails first at this price

On a budget strip, the failure order is fairly predictable. First the outlet contacts loosen, so plugs sag and lose grip. Then the rocker switch gets crunchy or stops latching. Then the strain relief where the cord meets the housing cracks. The surge component itself — the metal oxide varistor — degrades silently and gives no warning when it is spent. If a strip has taken a big hit, it may still pass power with zero protection left.

That is why the joule rating and the UL listing matter more than the outlet count. A strip with eight outlets and no real surge rating is an extension cord with marketing on the box.

For a kitchen, the practical move is often to skip the strip entirely and control individual appliances with smart plugs, keeping high-draw appliances like a space heater on their own wall outlet. If you do buy a strip, spend in the $15 to $25 range, check for UL 1449, and replace it after any major surge or if the outlets stop gripping.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I plug a space heater into a power strip?

No. A small ceramic heater draws roughly 1,500 watts on high, which is most of the roughly 1,800 watts a 15-amp strip can handle. Heaters like the Amazon Basics Ceramic Space Heater and the Dreo Space Heater should go directly into a wall outlet.

### Are smart plugs the same as a surge protector?

No. Smart plugs such as the Kasa Smart Plug HS103, Amazon Smart Plug, Govee Smart Plug, and Wyze Plug control power and scheduling. They do not absorb a surge, and none of them carry a joule rating.

### What should I check before buying a budget power strip?

Look for a UL 1449 listing, a joule rating of at least 600, and a published clamping voltage. Skip strips that list neither, and replace any strip whose outlets stop gripping plugs.
