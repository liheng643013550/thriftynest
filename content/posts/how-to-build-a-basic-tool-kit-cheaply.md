Starting a tool collection can feel like a big investment, especially when you see those massive sets with 300 pieces that cost as much as a weekend getaway. But the truth is, you don’t need any of that to hang a picture, fix a leaky faucet, or assemble a bookshelf. Most everyday home repairs only require a handful of basic tools that will last you for years.

To be fair, the trick is to buy smart: know exactly what you need, skip the gimmicks, and focus on quality where it actually counts. I’ve put together a list of the 12 essentials that cover 90% of the small jobs around a typical home, and I’ve priced it out so you can get started for under $60. I’ve used these exact tools for years, and they’ve paid for themselves many times over in avoided repair calls.

From real-world use, here is the exact list you need to grab, why you need it, and how to do it without blowing your budget, in my experience.

## Start With the Right Foundation: The Tool Bag or Box

Before you buy a single wrench, you need a place to put it. A cardboard box works in a pinch, but it falls apart fast and makes finding the right tool a treasure hunt. You don’t need a rolling chest; a simple tool bag or a plastic toolbox is perfect.

Worth noting, i prefer a soft-sided tool bag over a hard plastic box. It’s lighter, easier to carry up a ladder, and usually has more pockets for smaller items. Look for one that is at least 15 inches long to fit a hammer and a standard drill. You can find perfectly good options from Stanley or Husky for around $15. This is your home base, so don’t skip it.

## The 5 Tools You’ll Use Every Single Time

These are the workhorses. If you only bought five things, this would be the list. They handle the vast majority of quick fixes, believe it or not.

### 1. Claw Hammer (16 oz.)

A 16-ounce curved claw hammer is the gold standard for general home use. It’s heavy enough to drive a nail into a stud but light enough for hanging small frames. You don’t need a titanium wonder-tool. A solid steel head with a comfortable rubber grip will do the job. I’ve had my Estwing for over a decade, and it’s still going strong. If you want to save a few bucks, the generic brands from Home Depot or Lowe’s are fine for occasional use.

### 2. Phillips and Flathead Screwdrivers (4-Piece Set)

Do not buy a single screwdriver. Buy a set of four: a #2 Phillips, a #1 Phillips, a 3/16-inch flathead, and a 1/4-inch flathead. These four sizes will handle almost every screw you encounter in furniture assembly, outlet covers, and appliance panels. Look for a set with a rubber handle for grip. A good set costs about $10. I prefer the ones with a magnetic tip, it saves you from dropping screws into tight spots.

### 3. Adjustable Wrench (8-inch)

An 8-inch adjustable wrench is the perfect size for plumbing work, like tightening a supply line on a toilet or fixing a leaky sink trap. It’s also great for assembling furniture with hex nuts. The 8-inch size is light enough to maneuver in tight spaces under a sink. You only need one wrench to start. A decent Crescent wrench or a store brand will run you about $8, in my experience.

### 4. Tape Measure (25-foot)

Get a 25-foot tape measure with a locking mechanism. The extra length is helpful for measuring rooms or long pieces of lumber. The standout—how far the tape can extend without bending—should be at least 7 feet, which is standard on most quality tapes. A 25-foot Stanley or Milwaukee tape is a classic for a reason. You’ll use this more than you think, for hanging curtains, spacing shelves, and checking if that new couch will fit.

### 5. Utility Knife

A retractable utility knife is the single most satisfying tool to use. It’s perfect for opening boxes, cutting drywall, trimming carpet, and stripping wire in a pinch. The retractable feature is crucial for safety. Get one that takes standard snap-off blades. They are dirt cheap, usually under $5. I buy a pack of replacement blades at the same time because they go dull fast when you’re cutting through cardboard.

## The Power Tools You Can’t Skip

You don’t need a cordless drill immediately, but it is the one power tool that will change how you do projects. It’s worth the money, believe it or not.

### 6. Cordless Drill/Driver (12V or 18V)

This is your biggest single expense, but it’s non-negotiable. A cordless drill/driver is used for assembling furniture, drilling pilot holes, and driving screws. You don’t need a heavy 20V model for basic home use; a 12V model is lighter and easier to handle for hanging curtains and shelves. Brands like DeWalt, Ryobi, and Bosch all make excellent options. You can often find a kit with a battery and charger for around $40 on sale. I recommend getting a kit that comes with a set of drill bits and screwdriver bits to save money.

### 7. Drill Bit Set (Cobalt or Titanium)

You can’t use a drill without bits. Buy a basic set of high-speed steel (HSS) twist bits in sizes from 1/16-inch to 1/4-inch. These are for drilling into wood and plastic. Avoid the super cheap sets that bend; they are a waste of money. A 20-piece set from Irwin or Bosch will cost you about $10. You’ll also want a set of Phillips and flathead driver bits that fit your drill. Many drill kits include these, so check before you buy.

## The Hand Tools for Specific Jobs

These three items are essential for when things go slightly wrong, which they always do, in practice.

### 8. Needle-Nose Pliers (6-inch)

Needle-nose pliers are perfect for grabbing small screws, pulling out broken nails, and bending wire. They are also great for fishing a wire through a tight spot. Get a pair with a wire cutter built into the base of the jaws. This saves you from buying a separate pair of cutters. A 6-inch pair from Channellock is a solid choice for around $10.

### 9. Stud Finder (Magnetic or Electronic)

Hanging a heavy mirror or TV without a stud finder is a gamble. A magnetic stud finder is cheap and reliable—it finds the screws in the drywall that attach to the stud. They cost about $10 and require no batteries. An electronic one is fancier and can detect live wires, but the magnetic ones are foolproof. I use a magnetic one for most jobs because it never gives a false reading.

### 10. Level (24-inch)

You might think you can eyeball it, but you can’t. A level is essential for hanging shelves, pictures, and curtain rods. A 24-inch level is a good middle ground—long enough for a shelf but short enough for a picture frame. You can get a basic aluminum torpedo level for under $7. I always check mine against a known flat surface before I trust it, honestly.

## The Final Pieces to Round Out Your Kit

These last two items are the ones people forget, but they make the difference between a frustrating job and a smooth one.

### 11. Electrical Tape

This is a cheap insurance policy. A roll of black electrical tape is useful for temporarily insulating a wire, marking a cord, or wrapping a leaky hose connector. It’s also great for labeling keys or tools. It costs $1.50 at the hardware store. Just don’t use it as a permanent fix for electrical issues.

### 12. A Set of Assorted Screws, Nails, and Wall Anchors

You will always need the right fastener. Instead of running to the store every time, buy a small assorted box of drywall anchors, screws, and picture-hanging nails. You can get a plastic case with a few hundred pieces for about $8. This small investment will save you a trip to the hardware store more often than any other item on this list, in practice.

## How to Save Even More on This List

The prices I mentioned are approximate, but you can do better. Here are a few tricks I use to keep my tool budget lean.

### Shop the Store Brands

Husky (Home Depot), Kobalt (Lowe’s), and Craftsman (Lowe’s) are all excellent house brands. They usually cost 30% less than the name-brand equivalents and come with a lifetime warranty. For hand tools, they are just as good as the expensive stuff for home use.

### Wait for Sales and Holiday Bundles

Memorial Day, Labor Day, and Black Friday are prime times to buy power tools. You can often find a drill/driver kit with two batteries for the price of the bare tool. If you aren’t in a rush, wait for these sales, honestly.

### Buy Used for the Big Stuff

Check Facebook Marketplace or garage sales for cordless drills. People often sell them because they upgraded. Just make sure the battery holds a charge and isn’t from a discontinued line. If the batteries are old, they might be dead, and replacements can cost as much as a new drill.

### Skip the 200-Piece Sets

It’s tempting to buy that massive case with every socket and hex bit imaginable, but the quality is usually terrible. The metal is soft, and the bits strip out quickly. You will ultimately end up rebuying the essential pieces in better quality. Stick to the specific items on my list.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I really build a useful kit for under $60?

Yes, absolutely. The key is to prioritize. If you buy the drill as a kit with bits, you can get the hammer, screwdrivers, wrench, tape measure, and utility knife for around $15 total by choosing store brands. It’s tight, but it’s doable. If you can stretch to $80, you have a lot more room to breathe and buy better quality, in practice.

### What is the most important tool to spend money on?

The cordless drill is the most important tool to buy new and from a reputable brand. It’s the workhorse of the kit. A cheap drill will have a weak battery and poor torque, making simple tasks frustrating. Spend your money here, and save on the hand tools.

### Do I need a socket set right away?

No, you don't. The adjustable wrench covers most basic fasteners you'll encounter on furniture and appliances. Socket sets become necessary when you start working on cars or plumbing that has tight access. You can always add one later; it’s not a core item for a starter kit.

## The Bottom Line

Building a basic tool kit is not about buying everything at once. It’s about getting the right 12 items that cover your daily needs without breaking the bank. Start with the drill, hammer, and screwdrivers, and you can handle most jobs, in my experience.

The rest of the items on this list are the ones that will save you time and frustration down the road. You don’t need a professional-grade workshop to fix a squeaky door or hang a shelf. You just need a small, smart collection of tools that you actually know how to use. Start small, buy quality where it counts, and you’ll be surprised how much you can do yourself.
