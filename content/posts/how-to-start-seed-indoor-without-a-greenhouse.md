It's the middle of February, the seed catalogs have been sitting on your kitchen table for three weeks, and every packet of tomatoes and peppers you bought is still sealed. You don't have a greenhouse, you don't have a sunny south-facing window worth bragging about, and you're not about to spend $60 on a grow tent setup when the grocery budget is already tight. Here's the good news: starting seeds indoors without a greenhouse is mostly about light, warmth, moisture, and timing — and you can do all four on a card table.

## What You Actually Need (and What You Don't)

Greenhouses solve two problems: they extend the season and they protect plants from weather. Indoors, you've already solved the weather problem. What's left is giving seedlings enough light and consistent warmth, which is a much cheaper problem to fix.

Skip these unless you find them secondhand for a few dollars:
- Heated propagators with digital controllers
- Grow tents and ventilation kits
- Commercial seed-starting racks

You do need:
- Containers with drainage (yogurt cups with holes punched in the bottom work fine)
- A seed-starting mix, not garden soil
- A light source you can raise as plants grow
- A way to keep the mix damp without drowning it

One thing worth buying properly is the potting mix. Garden soil from outside compacts in small containers and often carries damping-off fungus. A bag of [Miracle-Gro Potting Mix](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B087F7GNFG?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) runs in the mid-single digits to low teens depending on bag size, and a standard bag fills dozens of small cells. Owner reviews consistently mention it drains well enough for seedlings, though you'll still want to thin your watering.

## The Light Problem, Solved Cheaply

A windowsill alone is usually not enough. Seedlings that don't get strong light in their first two weeks grow tall, pale, and floppy — the classic "leggy" look — and leggy seedlings never fully recover.

The cheap fix is a shop light or a pair of LED tube fixtures hung from a shelf, a closet rod, or a wooden frame. Budget LED shop lights typically draw somewhere between 20 and 40 watts and cost roughly $20 to $40 for a two-foot or four-foot fixture. You do not need a "grow light" label. You need:
- Light within 2 to 4 inches of the top of the plants
- 14 to 16 hours on per day
- A timer, so you're not the timer

A $10 mechanical outlet timer is the single best purchase in this whole project. Set it for 6 a.m. to 10 p.m. and forget it.

If you'd rather buy one fixture that does the job, a two-foot LED shop light generally covers a standard 1020 nursery tray (about 10 by 20 inches). Hang it from chain so you can raise it an inch at a time as seedlings grow — that adjustability matters more than raw wattage.

## Warmth: The Part People Get Wrong

Tomatoes, peppers, and eggplant germinate fastest around 75 to 85°F. Most homes sit at 65 to 70°F in winter, which means those seeds take twice as long to sprout — and slow, cold germination is where rot sets in.

Cheap ways to add bottom heat:
- Put trays on top of the refrigerator
- Set them on a shelf above a radiator or heat vent (not directly on it)
- Use a seedling heat mat, typically $15 to $30 for a single-tray size

Once seedlings have their first true leaves, take them off the heat. Warm roots plus weak light is how you get stretched, weak stems.

## Watering Without a Greenhouse's Humidity

A greenhouse holds moisture in the air. Indoors, your air is dry, so trays dry out fast — but overwatering is still the number one way beginners kill seedlings.

Practical rules:
- Water from the bottom by setting trays in a shallow pan for 10 to 15 minutes
- Let the surface dry slightly between waterings
- Use a spray bottle for the first week, before roots are established

A small watering can with a narrow spout makes this far less messy than a measuring cup. The [OXO Good Grips 1.75 Gallon](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07ZDRTFYN?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) is a common pick in this size range because the spout gives you control at low volume, which matters when you're dribbling water into 2-inch cells.

## Timing: Counting Backward From Your Last Frost

Work backward from your average last frost date, which your local extension office publishes for free.

| Crop | Start indoors before last frost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tomatoes | 6 to 8 weeks | Fast growers; don't start too early |
| Peppers | 8 to 10 weeks | Need the most warmth |
| Broccoli, cabbage | 4 to 6 weeks | Tolerate cooler rooms |
| Basil | 4 to 6 weeks | Germinates fast, hates cold |
| Squash, cucumbers | 2 to 4 weeks | Usually better direct-sown |

Starting too early is the most common mistake. A tomato started in January becomes a root-bound, light-starved plant by May, and it will not outproduce one started in March.

## Potting Up and Hardening Off

When roots show at the drainage holes, move seedlings into a larger container — a 3- to 4-inch pot is plenty for most starts. Use the same potting mix. A [Fiskars Transplanter](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004BDNHTI?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) is handy here because the narrow blade lifts a seedling with its root ball intact instead of tearing it. It's typically under $15.

Two weeks before your last frost, start hardening off. Carry trays outside for one hour on day one, two hours on day two, and build up over 7 to 10 days, avoiding full midday sun at first. This is the step people skip, and it's why greenhouse-grown transplants sometimes scorch within a day of planting out.

## Making Your Gear Last (and Easy to Replace)

This is where a no-greenhouse setup actually beats a greenhouse: almost everything you're using is a commodity item you can replace at any hardware store or online in two days.

Shop lights use standard LED tubes. Timers use standard outlets. Nursery trays and cell inserts are sold in packs of 5 to 10 for a few dollars, and the 1020 tray size is effectively universal, so inserts from one brand fit trays from another. Heat mats are simple resistive pads with no moving parts — owner reviews rarely report failures, and when one does die it's usually the cord, not the mat.

The one thing worth keeping clean and reusing for years is your potting mix container. Store it sealed and dry between seasons and a single bag lasts two or three springs. If you're starting seeds every year, buying trays and inserts in bulk packs usually costs less per unit than buying singles each February.

## The Bottom Line

You can start a full season of tomatoes, peppers, and herbs indoors for roughly $40 to $80 in first-year gear: a light fixture, a timer, a heat mat, trays, and a bag of potting mix. Skip the greenhouse, skip the grow tent, and put the money into light and a timer instead. Start your peppers and tomatoes 6 to 10 weeks before your last frost, keep the light 2 to 4 inches above the leaves, water from the bottom, and harden the plants off slowly. Everything else is details you can fix next year.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to start seeds indoors without a greenhouse?

First-year gear usually runs about $40 to $80, covering a light fixture, a timer, a heat mat, trays, and a bag of potting mix. After that, most years cost only a few dollars for mix and tray inserts.

### How long before my last frost should I start tomato and pepper seeds?

Tomatoes go in 6 to 8 weeks before your last frost date, and peppers 8 to 10 weeks before. Starting earlier than that usually produces root-bound plants that don't outperform later-started ones.

### Do I really need a heat mat to germinate seeds indoors?

Not for every crop, but peppers and tomatoes germinate fastest around 75 to 85°F, and most winter homes sit at 65 to 70°F. A seedling heat mat typically costs $15 to $30, and you take plants off it once the first true leaves appear.
