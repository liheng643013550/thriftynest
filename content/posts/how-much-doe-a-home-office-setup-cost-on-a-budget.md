## The quick answer

For most people, a genuinely usable budget home office costs **between $250 and $600 upfront**. The running cost is smaller than you'd think: **roughly $3 to $8 a month in electricity** for a typical setup. Your internet bill is almost always the biggest monthly number, and it's the one people forget to count.

The reason that range is so wide is that most buyers spend their money on the wrong things. That's the mistake worth fixing before you buy anything.

## The biggest mistake buyers make

They buy a "home office bundle" — desk, chair, lamp, and accessories sold together — and assume the bundle saves money.

It usually doesn't. Bundles tend to cheap out on the two things you actually touch all day (the chair and the desk) while padding the box with items you'd never buy separately. A $300 bundle often contains a $60 chair and a $40 desk with a $200 label on the box.

The second half of the mistake is ignoring running costs entirely. A cheap desk lamp and a cheap monitor can quietly add a few dollars a month to your power bill, and over a year that's real money — money that could have gone toward a better chair.

So the honest approach is this: spend on the chair first, then the desk, then the screen, then everything else. Power draw matters, but it's a tiebreaker, not the main event.

## How the number is built

There are two numbers here, and they work differently.

**Upfront cost** is simple addition:

    desk + chair + monitor + lamp + keyboard/mouse + surge protector + cables

**Running cost** uses the same formula for every device:

    watts ÷ 1000 × hours used per day × your electricity rate = cost per day

Multiply by 30 for a month. The US average residential rate sits somewhere around 16 to 17 cents per kilowatt-hour, though it varies a lot by state — Hawaii and parts of New England run well above that, and some states sit closer to 10 cents. Check your own bill; the math changes with it.

Here's the part that surprises people. A monitor that draws 25 watts for 8 hours a day costs roughly 3 to 4 cents a day at a typical rate. That's about a dollar a month. A laptop at 45 watts for the same hours lands in a similar place. Your whole office, minus heating and cooling, is usually a few dollars a month.

A space heater is the exception. A 1,500-watt heater running 4 hours a day can add $25 to $35 a month on its own. That single appliance can cost more to run than everything else in the room combined.

## What pushes the upfront cost down

Buying used or open-box is the single biggest lever, and it's the one busy parents skip because it takes time. Facebook Marketplace and local buy-nothing groups regularly have desks in the $20 to $50 range. A desk is a flat surface with legs — there's very little to go wrong.

For the chair, don't buy used unless you can sit in it first. This is the one item where new-and-decent beats cheap-and-mysterious.

On the electronics side, budget picks that owner reviews consistently describe as reliable include:

- Logitech K380 — a compact Bluetooth keyboard that runs on two AAA batteries. Owner reviews often mention getting months of use per set of batteries, which means near-zero running cost.
- Logitech M185 — a basic wireless mouse with a single AA battery. Specs list roughly a 12-month battery life, though that varies with use.
- Anker PowerStrip Surge Protector — surge protection matters more than people think, and a basic strip costs less than replacing one dead laptop.

The pattern with all three: they sip power rather than drink it. Battery-powered peripherals cost essentially nothing to run.

## What pushes the running cost up

Three things, in order of impact.

**Heating and cooling.** If your office is a spare bedroom with a door you can close, you can heat or cool one room instead of the whole house. That's a genuine saving, but a plug-in space heater or portable AC is the most expensive thing you'll plug in. A 1,500-watt heater at 4 hours a day is roughly $25 to $35 a month at a typical rate. A window AC unit can be similar or worse in summer.

**An old desktop tower.** A modern laptop typically draws 45 to 65 watts under normal office work. An older desktop tower with a separate monitor can pull 150 to 250 watts doing the same job. Same work, three to four times the power. If you're choosing between the two and you don't need the horsepower, the laptop wins on running cost every time.

**Leaving everything on.** A monitor on standby still draws a small amount — often under 1 watt, sometimes more in older models. Individually it's pennies. Across a monitor, a printer, speakers, and a charger brick, it adds up to a couple of dollars a month. A switched surge protector makes this a non-issue.

For lighting, BenQ ScreenBar monitor light bars are popular in owner reviews because they light the desk without lighting the whole room, and they draw only a few watts. A conventional desk lamp with an LED bulb is cheaper upfront and draws similar power — typically 8 to 12 watts for a comparable LED. Either is fine. Avoid halogen desk lamps, which can pull 40 watts or more for less light.

## The cheapest efficient picks, by category

**Monitor.** A 24-inch 1080p office monitor from a known brand typically draws 15 to 25 watts. Dell S2421HN is a common budget choice and its spec sheet lists low typical power draw. If you already own a laptop, using the laptop screen alone costs you nothing extra.

**Chair.** This is where you should overspend relative to everything else. A mid-range task chair with adjustable height and lumbar support is the difference between a setup you use for years and one you replace in eight months. Owner reviews on budget task chairs are brutally honest — read the three-star reviews, not the five-star ones.

**Desk.** IKEA Linnmon tabletops paired with separately sold legs are a long-running budget favorite in owner communities. The trade-off is that the top is lightweight and can sag under a heavy monitor arm, so keep the load modest.

**Surge protector.** Belkin SurgePlus is a well-known option with a switch, which lets you kill standby draw in one motion.

**Printer.** If you print rarely, Brother HL-L2350DW is a mono laser that owner reviews frequently describe as cheap to run per page. Laser toner doesn't dry out the way inkjet cartridges do, which matters a lot if you print twice a month.

## Typical scenarios compared

| Scenario | Upfront cost | Monthly electricity | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Laptop only, kitchen table | $0–$60 | $1–$2 | Uses what you own; add a mouse and a stand |
| Laptop + monitor + basic desk | $250–$400 | $2–$4 | The most common budget setup |
| Full setup with decent chair | $450–$700 | $3–$6 | Chair is the big line item |
| Desktop tower + dual monitors | $600–$900 | $6–$12 | Older towers pull far more power |
| Any setup + space heater | Add $0 | Add $25–$35 | The heater dominates the bill |

Electricity figures assume roughly 16 to 17 cents per kWh and about 8 hours of daily use. Your rate and your climate will move these numbers.

## The bottom line

A budget home office doesn't have to be expensive, and it doesn't have to be a power hog. Most people land between **$250 and $600 upfront** with **$3 to $8 a month** in electricity. The one thing that can wreck that monthly number is a space heater, so heat the room, not just your feet, if you can.

Three concrete ways to pay less:

1. **Buy the desk used, the chair new.** Desks are furniture; chairs are equipment. Used desks are everywhere and cheap. Used chairs are a gamble.
2. **Use a switched surge protector.** Flip it off at the end of the day and standby draw disappears. It costs a few dollars and saves a couple of dollars a month.
3. **Check your monitor's power setting.** Many monitors ship at full brightness. Dropping to 70 percent is usually invisible in a normal room and shaves a few watts off every hour you work.

If you're replacing an old desktop tower with a laptop, the running-cost saving alone can pay back a meaningful chunk of the laptop over a couple of years — and you get a quieter room out of it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to run a home office per month?

For a typical laptop-or-desktop-and-monitor setup, expect roughly $3 to $8 a month in electricity at average US rates. The range depends on your local rate, how many hours you work, and whether you're heating or cooling the room. Add a space heater and the number can jump by $25 or more.

### Is a laptop or a desktop cheaper to run?

A laptop is almost always cheaper. Specs typically show a laptop drawing 45 to 65 watts under normal office work, while an older desktop tower plus monitor can pull 150 to 250 watts doing the same tasks. Over a year of daily use, that difference is a real amount of money.

### What should I spend the most on in a budget home office?

The chair. It's the item you physically sit in for hours, and it's the one where a cheap choice tends to fail fastest. Owner reviews on budget chairs are usually candid about this. Spend there first, save on the desk, and keep the electronics simple.
