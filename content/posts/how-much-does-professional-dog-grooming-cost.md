You've just signed a lease on your first apartment together, you've adopted a dog, and now you're staring at a grooming bill that costs more than your internet. The dog needs a bath, a haircut, and their nails trimmed, and every groomer in town seems to want $70 to $120 for the privilege. So you're asking the obvious question: how much does professional dog grooming cost, really, and what would it cost to just do it yourselves at home?

Here are the real numbers, including the part almost nobody mentions — the electricity and running cost of doing it yourself, month after month.

## The quick answer

Professional grooming in a US metro area typically runs $45 to $95 for a basic bath-and-trim on a small dog, $60 to $120 for a medium dog, and $90 to $180 or more for a large or double-coated breed. Add-ons like nail grinding, de-shedding treatments, or medicated shampoo usually push it $10 to $30 higher. Most dogs need grooming every 4 to 8 weeks.

Doing it yourself costs roughly $3 to $12 per session in supplies and electricity once you own the clippers, plus a one-time equipment spend of about $120 to $250. That's the trade: a bigger upfront hit, a much smaller monthly bill.

## How the number is built

Both numbers come from the same three ingredients: equipment, consumables, and time. The only difference is who supplies them.

For professional grooming, the formula is simple. Base price by size and coat type, plus add-ons, times frequency per year. A medium dog at $85 every six weeks is about $735 a year. That's the number to compare against.

For DIY, the running cost is mostly electricity plus shampoo. Electricity is watts times hours times your rate. A typical dog clipper draws somewhere in the 10 to 45 watt range depending on the model, and a session might use it for 20 to 40 minutes. A hair dryer on a low heat setting draws far more — often 1,000 to 1,500 watts — but you'd only run it for 10 to 20 minutes. At an average US residential rate of roughly 16 to 17 cents per kWh, a full DIY grooming session usually lands under 25 cents of electricity. Yes, really. The electricity is almost a rounding error.

What actually costs money is shampoo, conditioner, and blade oil. Expect $0.50 to $2.00 per bath for a mid-size dog using a diluted pet shampoo. So a DIY session is typically $3 to $12 all-in once you own the gear.

## What pushes the professional price up

Size is the biggest lever. A Chihuahua and a Newfoundland are not the same appointment, and groomers price accordingly. Coat type matters just as much — double-coated breeds like Huskies and Goldens take far longer to de-shed and dry, and many shops charge a "heavy coat" surcharge of $15 to $40.

Temperament adds cost too. A dog that bites, panics, or needs two handlers often gets a handling fee. Owner reviews frequently mention that anxious dogs take longer, and time is what you're paying for.

Matting is the wild card. If the coat has to be shaved down rather than scissored, expect an extra $20 to $50, and sometimes a vet visit if the matting is tight against the skin.

Location matters as much as the dog. A suburban shop in a low-cost state might charge $50 for the same groom that runs $110 in San Francisco or Manhattan.

## What pushes the DIY cost up

The equipment is where the real money goes, and it's the reason most people quit after two attempts. Cheap clippers bog down in thick coats, pull hair, and overheat. A quiet, low-vibration clipper is worth the money for a nervous dog, and it's also the single biggest variable in your electricity use — a well-built clipper runs cooler and more efficiently than a bargain model that has to work harder.

For nails, a simple guillotine or scissor-style clipper does the job for most dogs. The [Millers Forge Nail Clipper](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0111SLMUQ?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) is a long-standing budget pick that owner reviews consistently describe as sharp and durable for the price. You'll also want styptic powder for the occasional quick, and a slicker brush and metal comb for daily upkeep.

Drying is the other hidden cost. Human hair dryers run too hot and can burn a dog's skin, so most DIYers either air-dry (slow, and risky in a cold apartment) or buy a pet dryer. Pet dryers draw more power than clippers by a wide margin, but you use them for minutes, not hours, so the monthly electricity impact stays small.

If you're grooming a dog with a thick double coat, a de-shedding rake and a good undercoat rake will save you more money than any other tool, because they cut down the drying time that makes professional grooming expensive in the first place.

## The monthly running cost, honestly

Here's where the apartment angle really shows up. Your electricity bill will barely notice DIY grooming. Twenty to forty minutes of clipper time plus fifteen minutes of dryer time, once every six weeks, is a few cents per session in most markets. Even running a full bath with warm water adds only a little, since water heating is the bigger load and you're using maybe 5 to 10 gallons.

The real monthly cost of DIY is consumables and the amortized equipment. Spread a $180 clipper kit over three years of monthly-ish grooming and it's about $5 a month. Shampoo and conditioner for a medium dog usually run $8 to $15 a bottle and last several baths.

So a realistic DIY monthly cost for a medium dog groomed every six weeks is roughly $8 to $20, equipment included. A professional groom at the same frequency is $55 to $85 a month. That gap — call it $40 to $70 a month, or $500 to $800 a year — is the entire argument.

## The bottom line

Professional grooming is a service, and you're paying for skill, speed, and the fact that someone else wrestles your dog into a tub. For a small, easy-coated dog, the $45 to $95 every six weeks is often worth it. For a medium or large dog, the math tilts hard toward DIY within the first year, and the electricity cost is genuinely negligible.

Three concrete ways to pay less:

First, split the job. Pay a pro for the haircut and do baths and nails at home between visits. Many shops offer a "bath and tidy" tier that costs $25 to $45 less than a full groom.

Second, buy one good tool instead of five cheap ones. A reliable clipper and a decent nail clipper like the [Millers Forge Nail Clipper](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0111SLMUQ?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) will outlast three bargain sets and cost less overall.

Third, groom on a schedule instead of waiting for matting. Consistent brushing every few days keeps the coat manageable, which keeps both the DIY time and the professional add-on fees down.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does professional dog grooming cost for a medium dog?

Typically $60 to $120 per visit in a US metro area, and most dogs need it every 4 to 8 weeks. That works out to roughly $40 to $85 a month.

### Is doing it yourself actually cheaper than professional grooming?

Yes, once you own the equipment. A DIY session usually costs $3 to $12 in shampoo and electricity, versus $60 to $120 for a professional groom, though you'll spend about $120 to $250 upfront on clippers and a dryer.

### How much electricity does grooming a dog at home use?

Very little. A clipper draws roughly 10 to 45 watts and a pet dryer more, but sessions are short, so a full grooming session typically costs under 25 cents of electricity at average US rates.
