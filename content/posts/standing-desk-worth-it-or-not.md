You're a college student in a dorm room that is roughly the size of a walk-in closet, and you keep seeing standing desks in study-with-me videos. Your back hurts from hunching over a bed or a cramped dorm desk. The desk costs more than a month of your meal plan, and your neighbor's wall is about six inches from your chair. So the real question isn't "are standing desks healthy?" — it's "will this thing be worth the money in a room where I also sleep, study, and can't make noise?"

That question — will it actually get used, or will it become an expensive shelf — is the one that decides everything here. And for a dorm, there's a second one riding along: how loud is it, and will the motor wake you up or annoy the person on the other side of the wall?

## The noise question, up front

Height-adjustable desks move with an electric motor, and motors make sound. Published specs and owner reviews put most budget electric desks in the range of roughly 45 to 55 decibels while running — about the level of a quiet conversation or a refrigerator hum. Premium models sometimes claim closer to 40 dB. That's not a jackhammer, but it's not silent either, and it runs for the 20 to 40 seconds it takes to travel up or down.

The bigger issue in a dorm is when you'd move it. If you study late and want to stand up at 1 a.m., that motor whir is going straight through a thin wall. Owner reviews of budget electric desks frequently mention a noticeable hum and occasional motor whine, especially under load (a monitor, a laptop, books). Cheaper single-motor frames tend to be louder and slower than dual-motor ones.

There's also a mechanical noise: the desk surface itself. A wobbly desk at full height rattles when you type. Reviewers of lower-priced frames often note some front-to-back sway at standing height, and sway means clatter.

If noise is your dealbreaker, this is where a manual or converter option wins — no motor, no sound at all.

## What it actually costs

Here's the budget math, honestly.

Purchase price: a basic electric standing desk typically runs somewhere in the $150 to $300 range for a 48-inch frame, and the [SHW 48-Inch Electric Height Adjustable Standing Desk](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CFF17W3V?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) sits in that band. Step up to a sturdier dual-motor model like the [Seville Classics Airlift](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B096LBBFWT?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) and you're usually looking at $250 to $400 depending on the size and top you choose.

Running cost: this is smaller than people fear. The motor draws only while moving — typically under 100 watts during a 30-second adjustment. If you raise and lower the desk four times a day, that's a few minutes of motor time total. At US electricity rates, the yearly cost is usually well under $5. It's a rounding error. The desk is not an energy hog.

Consumable and hidden costs over year one:
- A monitor arm or riser if your laptop screen sits too low — $25 to $60.
- A floor mat, because standing on bare dorm carpet or tile for an hour gets uncomfortable — $30 to $50.
- Cable management (clips, a tray) so cords don't catch when the desk moves — $10 to $20.
- Assembly time: budget models usually take 45 to 90 minutes and often need two people for the top.

So realistically, year one lands around $220 to $450 all-in. That's real money for a student.

## What you get for that money

A standing desk buys you the option to change posture. Specs and ergonomics guidance agree on the basics: alternating between sitting and standing through the day is easier on your back than sitting for four straight hours. You don't have to stand all day — most guidance suggests something like 30 to 60 minutes standing for every couple hours seated.

You also get a genuinely stable, full-size work surface, which a dorm-issued desk often isn't. A 48-inch top fits a laptop, a monitor, and a notebook without stacking things. If you're in a room where the "desk" is a shelf bolted to the wall, that alone can feel like a upgrade.

And you get height memory on many models — press a button and it returns to your sitting or standing height. That's the feature that actually drives daily use. Desks without presets tend to get left in one position.

What you don't get: quiet. And you don't get much floor space back — the frame footprint is roughly the same as a normal desk.

## When it is NOT worth it

Be honest with yourself here, because a lot of students buy one and stop using it by October.

It's not worth it if your dorm is small enough that the desk blocks your only walking path. It's not worth it if you study mostly in the library or a common room and only sit at the desk to scroll. It's not worth it if your roommate sleeps on a different schedule than you and the wall between you is drywall — that motor at midnight is a real conflict.

It's also not worth it if you're in a temporary housing situation. Dorm moves, sublets, and summer storage are hard on a 50-pound desk frame. Owner reviews regularly mention that these are awkward to disassemble and move.

And it's not worth it if your budget is genuinely tight. A $250 desk you use twice a week is worse value than a $30 fix you use daily.

## The bottom line

Yes — a standing desk is worth it, but only under one condition: you'll actually alternate positions most days and you can place it where the motor won't disturb sleep or a neighbor. If you can't check both boxes, skip the electric desk and get a manual converter instead. The health benefit comes from the switching, not from owning the furniture.
