The choice between a cordless drill and a random orbital sander comes down to what you actually need to do — the drill drives holes and screws, while the sander smooths and finishes surfaces. If you're furnishing a first apartment and mostly hanging shelves, assembling flat-pack, and patching drywall anchors, a drill is the tool that earns its shelf space. If you're refinishing a curb-scored dresser or smoothing a landlord's battered cabinet doors, the sander does the work a drill never will.

Here's the honest reality check before we go further. At the budget end — roughly $50 to $120 for either tool — you are not buying a pro-grade machine. A cordless drill in that range usually means a 12V or 20V compact with a plastic chuck and modest torque; it will drive screws into pine and drill pilot holes in drywall all day, but it will bog down in hardwood or masonry. A random orbital sander at that price typically runs on a 5-inch or 5-inch-class pad, draws around 2 to 3 amps, and does fine finish work on flat panels — but it won't remove years of paint in one pass, and the cheap hook-and-loop pads wear out faster. Neither tool will survive being dropped off a ladder. What this price range does buy you is a genuinely useful tool for apartment-scale projects, plus a real question about which one you'll actually reach for.

## Quick verdict

For most first apartments, buy the drill first. It hangs the curtain rods, builds the bookcase, and tightens the cabinet hinges.

If you're specifically planning to refinish furniture, sand down a painted door, or prep walls before repainting, buy the sander first — a drill can't fake that job.

And if you can only justify one purchase and your projects are split evenly, start with the drill and add a sander later; the drill's jobs come up far more often in a rental.

## Cost to own: the drill wins on consumables

Both tools are cheap to buy and cheap to keep running — but the drill's ongoing costs are more predictable. A basic bit set lasts years. The [Bosch Daredevil Set](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00XNFDK6U?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) covers most apartment drilling and driving, and the [DEWALT 27-Piece Hex Key Set](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B005O1RGY8?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) handles the flat-pack furniture bolts that come with the drill's own hex bits.

The sander's costs are quieter but steady. Sandpaper discs are consumable by design — a refinishing project can eat a dozen or more discs, and budget hook-and-loop pads lose grip and need replacing. Replacement parts are the bigger long-term question, and here the drill has a slight edge: batteries and chucks are widely stocked, but sander pads and dust-collection parts are more model-specific. Owner reviews often mention that finding the right replacement pad is the annoying part of owning a cheaper sander.

Verdict: drill, by a nose.

## Space and storage: the drill wins in a small apartment

A compact 12V drill is roughly the size of a large mug and lives happily in a kitchen drawer or a shoebox under the bed. A random orbital sander is a hand-sized body with a 5-inch pad and a cord — it needs a shelf, a bin, or a toolbox slot, and the cord adds tangle.

For a couple in 600 square feet, that difference matters. The drill is a grab-and-go tool. The sander is a "get it out and set up" tool.

Verdict: drill, clearly.

## Cleaning and dust: the sander is the messy one

This is the factor most new buyers underestimate. Sanding produces fine dust that settles on everything within a surprising radius. Many budget sanders include a small dust bag or a port for a shop vac; without extraction, expect to wipe down the whole room afterward. The [Bissell Featherweight](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B06ZY7BTWM?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) is a lightweight vacuum that helps with cleanup on hard floors, and a stack of [Amazon Basics Microfiber Cloths](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B082XSXNJ5?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) handles the wipe-down.

A drill makes a little drywall dust when you drill into a wall, and that's about it.

Replacement filters are worth flagging here: sander dust bags and any shop-vac filters are wear items, and finding the exact replacement bag for a budget model can be a hassle. Owner reviews frequently note that generic bags don't fit well.

Verdict: drill, easily.

## Speed and versatility: split decision

A drill is fast at what it does — a pilot hole takes seconds, a screw goes in a few more. It's versatile within its lane: drilling, driving, and with the right bits, light masonry. It is not a sander, and no attachment changes that.

The sander is slower per task but does something the drill cannot. For smoothing a tabletop or scuff-sanding a cabinet, it saves hours of hand-sanding. But it's a one-job tool.

Verdict: drill for everyday speed and range; sander for the specific finishing jobs.

## Noise: neither is quiet, but the sander is worse

A drill whines intermittently — you run it for a few seconds and stop. A random orbital sander runs continuously for minutes at a time, and the motor plus the orbital motion produces a steady, droning sound that carries through apartment walls. If you have neighbors or thin floors, that's a real consideration for evening work.

Verdict: drill, for apartment living.

## When to choose each one

Choose the cordless drill if your projects are hanging, assembling, tightening, and repairing — the everyday jobs of setting up an apartment. It's the tool you'll use in the first month and every month after.

Choose the random orbital sander if you have a specific refinishing or prep project lined up and a place to make dust — a garage, a balcony, or a well-ventilated room you can wipe down.

Choose both if you're furnishing a place with secondhand furniture and plan to refresh it. The drill builds and hangs; the sander makes the hand-me-downs look intentional.

## The bottom line

For a couple setting up a first apartment, the cordless drill is the more useful first purchase — cheaper to keep running, easier to store, cleaner, and used far more often. The random orbital sander is the specialist: buy it when you have a refinishing job that justifies the dust, the consumables, and the shelf space. On replacement parts and filters, the drill has the edge for availability, but neither tool is hard to maintain if you buy a mainstream model and keep a spare set of consumables on hand.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which should I buy first, a drill or a sander?

Buy the drill first. Its jobs — hanging, assembling, and tightening — come up far more often in a first apartment than refinishing work, and it's cheaper to keep running on consumables.

### Is a random orbital sander messy to use indoors?

Yes. Sanding produces fine dust that settles across the room unless you use the dust bag or a vacuum, so plan to wipe down surfaces afterward with microfiber cloths.

### Do I need to budget for replacement parts?

Yes, for both. Drills need bits and eventually batteries or chucks; sanders need sandpaper discs, backup pads, and dust bags or filters, and finding the right replacement pad for a budget sander can be the annoying part.
