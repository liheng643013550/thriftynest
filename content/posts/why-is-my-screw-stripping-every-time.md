## Why does a screw strip every time I turn it?

Ever turned a screwdriver and watched the screw head turn to mush instead of sinking in? It's one of the most common home-repair frustrations, and the good news is it's almost always fixable with the right bit, a little patience, or a couple of dollars in replacement hardware. You don't need a workshop or a landlord's permission to sort this out. Most of the time the problem is mechanical, not bad luck.

## Cause 1: You're using the wrong screwdriver tip

This is the number-one reason screws strip. A Phillips driver that's too small, too pointy, or worn will cam out of the cross and chew the corners of the head. The screw isn't defective; the tool just doesn't fit.

How to confirm it: Look at the driver tip. If it wobbles in the screw head, or if the tip looks rounded and shiny instead of crisp and sharp, you've found your culprit. A properly matched driver seats fully with almost no play.

The fix: Match the driver to the screw. Phillips comes in sizes #0 through #3, and a #2 driver fits the most common household screws. For hex (Allen) screws, you need the exact metric or SAE size — a loose one will round the socket fast. A dedicated driver set covers nearly every screw you'll meet in a rental. The [DEWALT 27-Piece Hex Key Set](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B005O1RGY8?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) runs typically around $15–$20 and handles furniture, bike, and appliance hex bolts.

Cost: $0 if you already own the right bit. A single quality driver is usually $5–$12.

## Cause 2: The screw is cheap, soft metal

Not all screws are created equal. Builder-grade screws — the ones in flat-pack furniture and hollow-core doors — are often soft zinc or low-grade steel. They deform under pressure long before a good screw would.

How to confirm it: If the head marks up easily when you press the driver in, or if you've stripped more than one screw from the same pack, it's the metal, not you.

The fix: Swap in better screws. Look for hardened steel or "case-hardened" on the label. For wood, a coarse-thread construction screw holds far better than the thin furniture screws that ship with a shelf kit.

Cost: A small box of decent screws is usually $6–$12.

## Cause 3: No pilot hole in hard material

Driving a screw straight into hardwood, MDF, or metal without a pilot hole forces the threads to fight the material. The screw stalls, you push harder, and the head strips.

How to confirm it: If the screw stops turning partway in and the driver starts spinning in the head, you skipped the pilot hole.

The fix: Drill a pilot hole slightly narrower than the screw's shaft. For hardwoods and metal, a cobalt or high-speed-steel bit makes the job quick. The [IRWIN Cobalt Set](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000BO9JMK?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) is built for drilling into metal and hard material, and the [Neiko HSS Set](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0195V5ICO?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) covers everyday wood and drywall pilot holes. Both typically land in the $15–$30 range depending on size count.

Cost: A single pilot bit is usually $3–$6; a set is $15–$30.

## Cause 4: Too much speed, not enough pressure

A drill driver spinning fast with light pressure rubs the head instead of driving the screw. The bit skips, the corners round off, and you get a stripped head. Speed is the enemy here.

How to confirm it: If you hear a high whine and see the bit skating across the head, you're going too fast.

The fix: Switch to low speed, high torque, and press firmly so the bit stays seated. Better yet, drive small screws by hand with a screwdriver — you'll feel the moment it seats instead of guessing.

Cost: $0. This one is technique.

## Cause 5: The bit or driver is worn out

Bits wear out. A driver that's driven a few hundred screws has rounded tips that no longer bite. You'll strip screws even when everything else is right.

How to confirm it: Compare your bit against a new one. If the tip looks blunt or the flutes are rounded, replace it.

The fix: Keep a fresh bit on hand. The [Bosch Daredevil Set](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00XNFDK6U?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) includes impact-rated drivers that hold up well on repeated use, typically around $10–$20 for the set. For a full home kit, the [DEWALT 108-Piece Mechanics Tool Set](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DBWGMZVL?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) or the [Craftsman 109-Piece Mechanics Tool Set](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CPYVKLZQ?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) give you matched bits for almost any screw in a small apartment — both usually fall in the $80–$130 range.

Cost: $10–$20 for a replacement bit set.

## Cause 6: Rust or paint in the threads

In a rental with old fixtures, screws corrode or get painted over. The threads bind, you force them, and the head strips.

How to confirm it: If the screw looks crusty or the hole is filled with paint, that's your problem.

The fix: Add a drop of penetrating oil and give it a few minutes. For painted-over screws, score around the head with a utility knife first so the paint doesn't lock it in place.

Cost: A small bottle of penetrating oil is usually $5–$8.

## When to stop and replace it instead

Sometimes the screw is genuinely done, and forcing it further just makes things worse. If the head is fully rounded, the screw is snapped, or the hardware is stripped beyond saving, replace it.

For a renter, the smart move is buying hardware that's easy to swap again later. Standardized parts mean you won't be hunting for a specialty piece next time. The rule of thumb: if a replacement part costs less than the time you'd spend fighting the stripped one, just replace it. In a small space, keeping a few spares on hand beats another trip to the store.

## Preventing this next time

- Keep a matched bit for every screw type you own.
- Buy hardened screws instead of reusing soft ones.
- Always drill a pilot hole in hard material.
- Drive small screws by hand when you can.
- Store a tiny bottle of penetrating oil with your tools.

## The bottom line

A stripped screw is almost never a disaster. Nine times out of ten it comes down to the wrong bit, a soft screw, or too much speed — all fixable for a few dollars or less. Match your driver, slow down, and use a pilot hole, and stripped heads become rare. When a screw is truly done, replacing it with standard hardware is cheap, quick, and lets you move on. For a renter in a small space, that's the whole game: small fixes, easy parts, no big spend.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the fastest way to stop a screw from stripping?

Match the driver to the screw and slow down. Most stripping happens when a loose or worn bit spins in the head, so a properly seated driver and lower speed fix it immediately.

### Do I really need a pilot hole for every screw?

No — soft wood and drywall often don't need one. But for hardwood, MDF, and metal, a pilot hole is the difference between a clean drive and a stripped head.

### When should I replace a stripped screw instead of fighting it?

If the head is fully rounded or the screw is snapped, replace it. Standard hardware and parts like the Moen 1222 Cartridge are cheap and widely stocked, so a swap usually costs less than the time you'd spend forcing the old one.
