You just signed a lease on a 480-square-foot studio, and the only closet is already full of coats. The landlord's carpet has a mystery stain near the door, your kitchen floor collects crumbs faster than you can sweep them, and the upright vacuum your parents offered you weighs more than your coffee table and takes up a third of your hallway. What you actually need is something that hangs on a hook, reaches under the bed, and — this is the part nobody talks about — still has filters you can buy two years from now.

## What actually matters

- Replacement parts and filters. This is the whole ballgame for a rental. A vacuum is only cheap if you can still buy its filter, brush roll, and battery in 2028. Before you buy anything, search the model name plus "filter" on Amazon and see whether results come back. If they don't, walk away.
- Weight and storage. Under about 8 pounds is comfortable to carry up a stairwell or hold overhead to hit a ceiling corner. Look for a wall mount or a folding handle, because floor space is the thing you don't have.
- Run time versus charge time. Budget cordless stick vacuums typically run 15 to 25 minutes on low and far less on high. If the charge time is 4 hours, that math matters on a Saturday.
- Corded or cordless. A corded stick vacuum never runs out of power and usually costs less, but you're tethered to an outlet. In a one-room apartment, that's often fine.
- Carpet versus hard floor. Most sticks handle both, but brush rolls tangle fast on long hair and pet fur. Check whether the brush roll pops out without tools.
- Bin size and emptying. Half a liter fills up quickly in a small space with a shedding pet. A bin you can dump in one motion beats a bigger bin with a fiddly latch.

## Bissell Featherweight

Roughly $35 to $50. This is the cheapest legitimate answer to "I have 400 square feet and no money." It converts from a stick to a handheld, weighs about 4 pounds, and the cord means it never dies mid-room. Owner reviews consistently mention it's best on hard floors and low-pile carpet, and the filter is a washable foam-and-mesh setup you rinse rather than replace — which is exactly the kind of low-maintenance design a first apartment wants. Downsides are real: the cord is short, the bin is tiny, and it won't pull embedded pet hair out of a plush rug. Buy it if you have mostly hard floors and a hard budget ceiling.

## A note on the boring stuff that saves you money

Two habits keep a cheap vacuum alive. First, rinse washable filters under lukewarm water and let them dry a full 24 hours before putting them back — a damp filter smells and loses suction. Second, replace foam filters every few months if you vacuum daily; they're usually a few dollars for a multi-pack. If you're deciding between two vacuums at the same price, pick the one with more third-party filter listings. That single search result predicts how annoying year three will be.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long do budget cordless stick vacuums actually run?

Budget cordless models typically run 15 to 25 minutes on low and considerably less on high, with charge times that can stretch to several hours. In a small apartment that's usually enough for one full pass, which is why corded options like the Bissell Featherweight stay popular.

### Do I really need to check filter availability before buying?

Yes. A vacuum is only a bargain if you can still buy its filter, brush roll, and battery later. Search the model name plus "filter" before you buy — if nothing comes back, the vacuum becomes disposable the moment the filter clogs.

### Is an upright better than a stick for a carpeted first apartment?

For carpet and pet hair, generally yes. Uprights like the Shark Navigator Lift-Away and Bissell CleanView Swivel Pet offer stronger suction and far better long-term parts availability than budget sticks. The trade-off is weight and storage space, which matters a lot in a very small unit.
