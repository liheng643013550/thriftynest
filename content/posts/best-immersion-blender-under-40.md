## What to Look For

That said, Before you buy, keep these five things in mind. They matter more than the brand name, in my experience.

- **Wattage and Power:** Look for at least 200 watts for basic soups and smoothies. If you plan to crush ice or blend frozen fruit, aim for 300 watts or more. Less power means you’ll be pushing the blender into the food, not the other way around.
- **Shaft Material:** Go with stainless steel. Plastic shafts can crack, stain, and warp over time, especially when blending hot liquids. Steel is easier to clean and won’t absorb odors.
- **Speed Settings:** You want at least two speeds. A single-speed model is fine for pureeing soup, but a variable speed dial gives you control for things like whipping cream or making emulsions where you don't want to over-blend.
- **Comfort and Weight:** You’ll hold this thing for a few minutes at a time. Look for an ergonomic handle that fits your grip. A heavy motor can make your wrist tired, so find a balance between power and weight.
- **Attachments Included:** A whisk or a chopper attachment adds serious value. If you’re paying $35, you should get more than just a stick. A whisk is great for eggs and whipped cream, and a small chopper can handle onions and nuts without pulling out the big food processor.

## Cuisinart Smart Stick CSB-75

To be fair, the Cuisinart Smart Stick is the default pick for a reason. It costs about $35, and it does everything you need without any drama.

What makes it great is the 3-speed motor. That might not sound like much, but it’s enough. I use the low speed to fold in whipped cream or blend a light vinaigrette. The high speed handles roasted tomato soup like a champ, leaving it silky in about 45 seconds. The stainless steel shaft is a must-have; it doesn't scratch my non-stick pot, and it rinses clean in ten seconds under warm water.

The downside is that the motor is a bit noisy, and the included whisk attachment is small. It’s fine for a couple of eggs, but if you’re whipping a pint of cream, it takes a few minutes longer than a hand mixer, honestly.

Who should buy it? If you want one tool that does a bit of everything well, this is it. It’s the safest bet under $40, and the quality matches brands that cost twice as much.

## Hamilton Beach 59765

If you’re on a tight budget, the Hamilton Beach 59765 is your friend. It’s usually around $25, and it punches above its weight.

This model comes with a 3-cup chopper attachment that’s actually useful. I’ve used it to chop a small onion in about five pulses, and it handles garlic and parsley without turning them into a paste. The stainless steel blending shaft is a big plus at this price point, believe it or not.

The motor is rated at 240 watts, which is decent. It purees cooked carrots and cauliflower soup without leaving chunks. But if you try to blend frozen berries for a smoothie, you’ll need to add a lot of liquid and be patient. The speed button is a simple push, and there’s no variable dial, so you have less control.

The downside is the build quality. It feels a little plasticky, and the handle is a bit chunky. It’s not a tool that will last ten years, but for $25, you can’t argue.

Who should buy it? If you’re a college student, a first-time renter, or just want a backup blender, this is a solid, no-frills choice, believe it or not.

## All-Clad UX1

The All-Clad UX1 is a bit of a sleeper hit. It costs about $35, which is surprising for the brand, but it packs a 300-watt motor that feels stronger than the price suggests.

I was impressed by how quiet it is. It’s noticeably less whiny than the Cuisinart. The shaft is a full metal bell, which makes it easy to clean and durable. It handled a thick lentil soup without any strain, and the blades didn’t leave any grittiness behind.

The downside? It only has two speeds, not a variable dial. And it doesn’t come with a whisk or chopper attachment. You’re getting a pure stick blender, nothing else, in practice.

Who should buy it? If you care about build quality and motor power more than extra attachments, this is the one. It feels like a $60 blender that happens to be on sale.

## KitchenAid 5-Speed KHB1231

KitchenAid’s entry-level immersion blender is a great value at around $39. The 5-speed dial gives you more control than most competitors in this price range.

I love the included 2-cup blending jar. It’s perfect for making a single serving of salad dressing or a small batch of pesto. The jar has a lid, so you can store leftovers directly in it, which saves me from washing an extra container. It hasn’t warped or stained in my testing, but I’m a bit wary of it over a long period. Also, the whisk attachment is sold separately, which is a bummer.

Who should buy it? If you make a lot of dressings, sauces, and single-serving smoothies, the blending jar is a game-changer. It’s a great all-rounder for the price.

## Breville BSB510

The Breville BSB510 is the most "premium" feeling option under $40, usually priced right at $39. It’s all about control, believe it or not.

The standout feature is the variable speed dial on the body. You can go from a slow stir to a full-on puree with a simple twist. This is perfect for making aioli or emulsifying a dressing, where you need to start slow and increase speed gradually. The 280-watt motor is smooth and doesn’t vibrate much in your hand.

The cleaning is easy because the shaft is metal and the bell detaches from the motor body. It also has a nice long cord, about 4 feet, which gives you flexibility when using it at a stove.

The downside is that it only comes with the stick. No whisk, no chopper. And the dial can be a little stiff at first, but it loosens up with use, in practice.

Who should buy it? If you love cooking techniques that require fine control, like emulsions and hollandaise, this is your best bet. It’s a precision tool, not a gadget-filled one.

## Ninja BN701

The Ninja BN701 is a different beast. It’s actually a single-serve blender with a stick attachment, and it costs around $30. But hear me out.

This thing has a 700-watt motor, which is more than double the power of the others on this list. It comes with a 16-ounce cup and a blade base, so you can make a smoothie in the cup, then flip it upside down to blend. It also includes a stick blender for soups, in practice.

This is described in use for a berry smoothie with frozen fruit and spinach. In owner reports, it crushed the ice completely in about 30 seconds, which no other model on this list is described as doing. The stick attachment is reported to work fine for soups, but it’s a bit clunky because the motor is heavy.

The downside is that it’s not compact. You have to store the motor base, the cup, and the stick separately. It also has a learning curve with the locking mechanism. It’s not the most intuitive design.

Who should buy it? If you’re a smoothie person who also wants a soup blender, this is the perfect two-in-one. It’s a better smoothie maker than a pure soup stick, but it does both jobs well, believe it or not.
