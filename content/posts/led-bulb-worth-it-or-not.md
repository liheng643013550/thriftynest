You've just signed a lease on your first apartment together, and you're standing in the lighting aisle staring at two bulbs that look identical. One costs about a dollar. The other costs three or four times that and promises to save you money. You're wondering whether the cheap one is fine and the "energy saving" label is just marketing, or whether you're being penny-wise and pound-foolish. That doubt is reasonable, and the honest answer depends on how long you'll stay and how many bulbs you're replacing.

## The doubt, stated plainly

Nobody wants to spend $30 on light bulbs in the same week they're buying a shower curtain and a can opener. But nobody wants to feel tricked later either. The good news is the math here is unusually simple, because bulbs have only two costs: what you pay at the register, and what they add to your electric bill every month. Everything else is marketing.

## What it actually costs

Start with the sticker price. A basic LED bulb typically runs somewhere around $2 to $5 each depending on brand, brightness, and whether it's dimmable. A comparable incandescent is usually under $1.50, and a halogen is in between. So for a first apartment with, say, eight fixtures, you're looking at roughly $20–$40 for LEDs versus maybe $8–$12 for incandescents. That's the whole "expensive" part, and it's a one-time gap of roughly $15–$30.

Now the running cost, which is where the story flips. An LED that produces about as much light as a 60-watt incandescent draws roughly 8 to 10 watts. The specs on the box will tell you — look for the wattage, not the "60W equivalent" claim. If a bulb runs four hours a day, that's about 120 hours a month. At a typical US electricity rate of roughly 15–17 cents per kilowatt-hour (it varies a lot by state), a 9-watt LED costs somewhere around 15–20 cents a month to run. The 60-watt incandescent doing the same job costs roughly $1.10–$1.25 a month.

Multiply that across eight fixtures and the gap is real: maybe $1.50–$2 a month for the LEDs versus $9–$10 for the incandescents. Over a year that's roughly $18–$24 versus $110–$120. The LEDs have paid back their purchase premium in a few months and then keep going.

There's a second cost people forget: replacement. Incandescents typically last around 1,000 hours; owner reviews and spec sheets for LEDs commonly cite 15,000 to 25,000 hours. In a four-hours-a-day fixture, that's roughly seven months for the incandescent versus many years for the LED. So the incandescent isn't just costing you electricity — you're also buying it again and again, and hauling yourself back to the store.

## What you get for that money

Beyond the bill, LEDs give you a few practical things that matter in a first apartment. They run cool — an incandescent wastes most of its energy as heat, which is genuinely unwelcome in a small bedroom in July. They usually dim more smoothly than cheap halogens, and they don't hum or flicker the way some budget dimmable options do. They also come in different color temperatures, so you can put a warmer bulb (around 2700K) in the living room and a cooler one in the bathroom without changing fixtures.

The one thing you don't automatically get is quality. Cheap LEDs can have poor color rendering, a slight delay when switched on, or a short lifespan if they're in an enclosed fixture that traps heat. Owner reviews are your friend here — look for complaints about flicker and early failure, not just star averages.

If you want to know exactly what a given bulb or appliance is costing you, a plug-in meter removes all the guesswork. The [P3 P4460 Kill A Watt EZ](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07W8WTQYT?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) is a well-known model that reads watts and accumulates kilowatt-hours, so you can check a lamp, a TV, or a space heater and see the real number instead of estimating. It's overkill for bulbs alone, but useful in a first apartment where you're still figuring out what's draining power.

## When it is NOT worth it

Here's where honesty matters. LEDs are not automatically the right call.

If you're subletting for four months and the lease ends in spring, the payback window may not arrive before you move. A handful of $1.50 incandescents is a perfectly rational choice for a short stay, especially in fixtures you rarely use.

If a fixture is on a dimmer that's older or incompatible, a cheap LED may buzz or flicker, and the fix (a compatible dimmer or better bulbs) can cost more than the electricity you'd save. Same story for enclosed fixtures and outdoor fixtures in extreme cold — some LEDs underperform there, and the specs will usually warn you.

And if a bulb is in a closet or a guest room that's lit for ten minutes a week, the running-cost savings are trivial. You're buying longevity, not energy savings, and that's a weaker case.

## The cheaper alternative

If you want to spend less up front, plain incandescent or halogen bulbs remain the cheapest purchase, and for low-use fixtures they're fine. There's no product link worth forcing here — the honest advice is to buy the cheapest bulb that fits the socket for closets and hallways, and put your money into LEDs only where lights actually burn for hours.

The smarter "cheaper" move is often to cut waste elsewhere in the apartment instead. A smart plug lets you kill standby draw from a TV or a charger brick on a schedule, and the [Kasa Smart Plug HS103](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08K91R4DL?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) is a widely reviewed single-outlet option that works with common voice assistants. Pair it with the [Kasa Smart Plug HS103P2](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08M1Q3CW1?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) two-pack if you want to cover more than one device. That's a different kind of saving than bulbs, but it's real and it's cheap.

## The bottom line

Yes — LEDs are worth it, on one condition: you're staying long enough to collect the savings. If your lease runs a year or more and your lights are on a few hours a day, the purchase premium typically pays itself back within a few months and the rest is profit. If you're in a short-term sublet or lighting a closet, buy the cheap bulb and don't feel bad about it. The mistake isn't choosing incandescent; it's paying LED prices for fixtures that never get used.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does an LED bulb actually add to my electric bill each month?

Based on the numbers above, a bulb drawing roughly 9 watts for about four hours a day costs somewhere around 15–20 cents a month at typical US rates. An equivalent 60-watt incandescent doing the same job runs roughly $1.10–$1.25 a month.

### How long before the higher purchase price pays for itself?

With a one-time gap of roughly $15–$30 across eight fixtures and monthly savings of a few dollars, the premium is usually recovered within a few months of regular use. After that, the savings keep accumulating for as long as the bulbs last.

### Are LEDs worth it if I'm only staying in the apartment a short time?

Probably not. If your lease ends before the payback window closes, cheap incandescent or halogen bulbs are the rational choice for low-use fixtures. Save the LED spending for lights that burn for hours a day and for places you'll actually stay.
