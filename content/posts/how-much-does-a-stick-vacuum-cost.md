If you are downsizing into a smaller place, the vacuum question changes completely. You are no longer cleaning 2,000 square feet of carpet — you are chasing crumbs off 700 square feet of hard floor, and you want to know what a stick vacuum actually costs to buy and to run. Here are the real numbers, including the monthly electricity cost most buying guides skip.

## The quick answer

Most stick vacuums sell for somewhere between $150 and $600. Budget corded models and basic cordless ones typically land in the $80 to $200 range. Mid-tier cordless models from brands like Shark and Bissell usually run $200 to $400. Premium cordless sticks from Dyson and similar brands often sit between $400 and $700, and prices swing a lot during sales events.

Running cost is a different story, and it is much smaller than most people expect. In a small home, a stick vacuum typically adds somewhere between 30 cents and $2 a month to your electric bill. That is not a typo. The purchase price is the whole game; the electricity is a rounding error.

## How the number is built

Electricity cost is simple math: watts times hours times your rate, divided by 1,000.

Say your utility charges 16 cents per kilowatt-hour, which is close to the current US average. A cordless stick vacuum draws roughly 100 to 400 watts while running, depending on the model and power setting. If you vacuum 15 minutes a day, that is about 7.5 hours a month.

At 300 watts: 300 × 7.5 = 2,250 watt-hours, or 2.25 kWh. Times $0.16 = about 36 cents a month.

A corded stick vacuum usually pulls more power — often 400 to 900 watts — but corded models tend to be used in shorter bursts and often on hard floor where you move faster. Even at 800 watts for 5 hours a month, you are looking at roughly 64 cents.

The real running cost of a cordless stick vacuum is not the wall outlet. It is the battery. Most cordless sticks use a lithium-ion pack that lasts roughly two to four years of regular use before capacity drops noticeably. Replacement batteries typically cost $40 to $120 depending on brand, which works out to a few dollars a month amortized over the pack's life. That is still cheap — but it is the line item that actually shows up.

For a downsizer, there is one more number worth knowing. Smaller homes mean less square footage, which means shorter runtimes matter less. A stick vacuum with 20 minutes of runtime is a real limitation in a 2,500-square-foot house and barely a limitation in a 700-square-foot condo.

## What pushes the price up

Battery technology and runtime are the biggest drivers. Cheaper cordless sticks often use older cell designs with 15 to 25 minutes of runtime on low power and much less on high. Better models push 40 to 60 minutes and charge faster.

Filtration is the second driver. Models with sealed HEPA-style systems and washable filters cost more up front but avoid $15 to $30 replacement filter packs every few months. If you have allergies, that trade-off usually pays for itself.

Attachments and floor-type versatility add cost too. A stick that converts to a handheld, ships with a crevice tool and a pet brush, and handles both hard floor and low-pile carpet will always cost more than a bare-bones model.

The [Shark WANDVAC](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CBSV2TNM?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) is a good example of a light, handheld-style stick aimed at small spaces and quick cleanups rather than whole-home carpet work. It sits in the mid range rather than the budget tier, and owner reviews generally describe it as easy to grab and easy to store — which matters a lot when you have less closet space.

## What pushes the price down

Corded stick vacuums are the cheapest category and the most underrated for small homes. No battery, no charging dock, no replacement pack in two years. The [Bissell Featherweight](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B06ZY7BTWM?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) is a well-known example: light, inexpensive, and widely reviewed as fine for hard floors and light debris, less so for deep carpet. For a downsizer in a hard-floor apartment, that is often the whole job.

Cordless sticks from value-focused brands also undercut the premium names substantially. The [Wyze Cordless Vacuum](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0G2S2XK8L?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) is generally priced well below comparable Shark and Dyson models, and owner reviews commonly mention that it handles everyday messes without the premium price tag. The trade-offs usually show up in suction on deep carpet, build feel, and long-term parts availability.

Buying last year's model is another reliable way to save $80 to $150. Stick vacuums get refreshed constantly, and the previous generation usually keeps the same motor and battery with a slightly different body.

If you are keeping a full-size vacuum for deep carpet days, the [Shark Navigator Lift-Away](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08TT4YHG1?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) is an upright rather than a stick, but it is often cited by owners as a durable option that costs less than a premium cordless stick — worth knowing if your "downsize" still includes one rug.

Those numbers assume typical US electricity rates, which vary by region — Hawaii and parts of California run two to three times the national average, while many southern and midwestern states sit well below it. Your actual bill will move with your rate, not with the vacuum.

## The downsizing angle nobody mentions

When you move into a smaller home, you usually also lose storage. That changes the math more than electricity does.

A stick vacuum that lives on a wall mount in a closet takes up almost nothing. A full-size upright takes a corner. If your new place is short on closets, the stick's real value is the space it does not consume — and the fact that you will actually use it daily because it is right there instead of buried behind coats.

There is a second, quieter benefit. Smaller homes have less floor to cover, so you can vacuum more often without it feeling like a chore. Running a 300-watt stick for 10 minutes a day costs you about 24 cents a month. Running a 900-watt upright for 30 minutes twice a week costs about $1.20. Neither will show up on your bill in any meaningful way, but the daily habit is easier to sustain with a tool that takes 15 seconds to grab.

One caveat on cordless models: batteries do not like heat. If your only storage is a hot garage or a sunny utility closet, expect the pack to fade faster and factor in a replacement sooner.

## The bottom line

A stick vacuum costs $150 to $600 to buy, and roughly 30 cents to $2 a month to run in a small home. The purchase price is 99% of the decision. Electricity is not worth optimizing — but battery replacement and filter replacements are, because those are the costs that quietly add up over three years.

Three concrete ways to pay less:

- Buy corded if your home is mostly hard floor. You skip the battery entirely and often save $100 or more.
- Buy the previous model year. Same motor, same battery, usually $80 to $150 cheaper.
- Buy a washable filter model and actually wash it. Replacement filter packs typically run $15 to $30, and skipping them is free money.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a stick vacuum expensive to run every month?

No. In a small home, a cordless stick vacuum typically adds somewhere between 30 cents and $2 a month to your electric bill, based on typical US electricity rates and about 7.5 hours of use per month. The battery, which usually costs $40 to $120 to replace every two to four years, is the bigger long-term cost.

### What is the cheapest type of stick vacuum to buy?

Corded stick vacuums are the cheapest category, with budget models often selling in the $80 to $200 range. They skip the battery and charging dock entirely, which is why models like the Bissell Featherweight are commonly recommended for hard floors and light debris in small homes.

### How long do stick vacuum batteries last before I need a new one?

Most cordless stick vacuums use a lithium-ion pack that lasts roughly two to four years of regular use before capacity drops noticeably. Replacement batteries typically cost $40 to $120 depending on the brand, and heat speeds up the decline, so avoid storing the vacuum in a hot garage.
