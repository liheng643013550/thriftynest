You flip the switch and nothing happens. Or worse, it flickers for two weeks, makes a buzzing noise you can hear from the hallway, and dies right after the return window closes. So you buy another pack, and eighteen months later you're standing in the same aisle doing the same math. That's the real problem with cheap bulbs — not the two dollars you hand over, but how often you hand it over.

This guide is about the total cost of the first year. Purchase price, electricity, and how likely you are to be back at the store before next spring.

## What actually matters

- **Wattage and lumens, not "equivalent" watts.** A bulb marketed as a 60W replacement should put out roughly 800 lumens. If the box only shouts about wattage, check the lumen number before you buy. Lower lumens at the same price is a quiet downgrade.
- **Color temperature (Kelvin).** 2700K is warm and yellowish, 3000K is a soft white, 4000K–5000K reads cool and clinical. Mixing temperatures in one room is the fastest way to make new bulbs look wrong.
- **Rated hours versus real hours.** Most LED packaging claims somewhere in the 10,000–25,000 hour range. Owner reviews are the better guide: heat, enclosed fixtures, and cheap drivers kill bulbs long before the label's number.
- **Dimmability.** A non-dimmable LED on a dimmer switch will flicker, buzz, or strobe. If you have dimmers, buy bulbs explicitly rated for them.
- **First-year electricity cost.** A 9W LED run 3 hours a day uses roughly 10 kWh a year. At a typical US rate of about 15 cents per kWh, that's around $1.50 per bulb per year. An old 60W incandescent in the same socket burns about 66 kWh — roughly $10. Multiply by the number of bulbs in your house and the gap gets real fast.
- **Whether they're actually in stock in the size you need.** A great bulb you can't buy in the right base and shape is not a great bulb.

## How to sanity-check the math

If you want your own numbers instead of mine, a plug-in meter like the [P3 P4460 Kill A Watt EZ](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07W8WTQYT?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) typically runs in the $30–$45 range and will tell you what any lamp or appliance actually draws. It won't tell you how long a bulb will last, but it removes the guessing from the electricity half of the equation.

Smart plugs are the other cheap way to cut waste, since a bulb left on by accident costs the same as one left on on purpose. The [Kasa Smart Plug HS103](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08K91R4DL?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) is usually around $8–$12 for a single plug, and the [Kasa Smart Plug HS103P2](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08M1Q3CW1?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) two-pack typically lands near $15–$20. The [Amazon Smart Plug](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D9C2V83V?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) sits in a similar band and is the simplest option if you already use Alexa. The [Govee Smart Plug](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CKM8CF7N?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__), [Wyze Plug](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07XZT24B8?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__), and [Leviton Decora Smart Plug](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01NAO4B9Z?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) all do the same basic job at comparable prices. None of these are bulb brands — they're the supporting cast that keeps a lamp from running all night.

## What the first year actually costs

Here's the honest breakdown for a single bulb, assuming 3 hours of use per day:

- **Purchase:** typically $2–$6 for a decent name-brand LED, more for specialty shapes or high-CRI versions.
- **Electricity:** about $1.50 for a 9W LED at 15 cents per kWh.
- **Replacement risk:** this is the wild card. A bulb that dies at month 14 costs you the full purchase price again, which doubles your year-one cost. A bulb that lasts five years spreads that $4 across 60 months.

So the real question isn't "which bulb is cheapest?" It's "which bulb am I least likely to rebuy?" That's why owner reviews matter more than spec sheets here. A bulb with 20,000 rated hours and a pattern of early failures in reviews is a worse buy than a slightly dimmer bulb people have had in their ceiling for four years.

## Products worth a look

These are real, widely sold bulbs and bulb-adjacent items. I have no test lab and haven't measured any of them — everything below comes from published specs and the pattern of verified owner reviews.

### Philips LED Dimmable A19

**Price band:** typically $8–$14 for a 4-pack.

**Why it's a reasonable pick:** Philips is one of the few brands with a consistent reputation across multiple bulb lines, and their dimmable A19 is the default "just works" bulb for most rooms. Specs put it around 800 lumens at roughly 8–9W in the soft white version, with a rated life in the 10,000–15,000 hour range.

**Downsides:** Not the cheapest per bulb, and the dimming performance depends heavily on which dimmer switch you have. Some owner reviews mention flicker on older dimmers.

**Who should buy it:** Anyone replacing bulbs in a room they actually use daily and doesn't want to think about it again.

### Cree LED A19

**Price band:** usually $10–$16 for a 4-pack.

**Why it's a reasonable pick:** Cree has a long track record in LED specifically, and their standard A19 is frequently cited in owner reviews for holding its color temperature well over time — the light doesn't drift noticeably yellow or blue as it ages.

**Downsides:** Availability is spotty in physical stores, and the price per bulb runs higher than store brands. Not every Cree line is dimmable, so check the box.

**Who should buy it:** People who care about consistent color across a room and are willing to pay a little more per bulb.

### GE Relax LED

**Price band:** typically $9–$15 for a 4-pack.

**Why it's a reasonable pick:** The Relax line is tuned to 2700K with a high CRI, which makes skin tones and wood floors look normal instead of gray. Specs list it around 800 lumens at 8–10W depending on the version.

**Downsides:** The warmer color isn't for everyone, and some owner reviews note that the "relax" tint looks dimmer than a standard soft white at the same lumen rating. It isn't — it just reads that way.

**Who should buy it:** Bedrooms, living rooms, and anywhere you want warm light rather than bright light.

### Feit Electric LED A19

**Price band:** usually $6–$12 for a 4-pack.

**Why it's a reasonable pick:** Feit is the value option that shows up consistently in big-box stores and online. Lumens per dollar is genuinely competitive, and for closets, hallways, and utility spaces the difference between a Feit and a premium bulb is hard to notice.

**Downsides:** Owner reviews are more mixed on lifespan than the bigger names. Some report failures inside two years. Treat the rated hours as optimistic.

**Who should buy it:** Low-use fixtures where a shorter lifespan won't hurt much, and anyone bulking up on cheap replacements for storage.

### Philips Hue White A19

**Price band:** typically $30–$50 for a 2-pack, more with a hub.

**Why it's a reasonable pick:** If you want dimming and scheduling without a wall dimmer, Hue is the most mature system. The bulbs are solid on their own, and the app control means you can cut wasted hours without rewiring anything.

**Downsides:** This is the expensive path. You're paying for the ecosystem, not the light output. If you just want a bulb that turns on, this is the wrong purchase.

**Who should buy it:** Renters who can't install smart switches, or anyone who genuinely uses scheduling and scenes.

### Sylvania LED A19

**Price band:** usually $7–$13 for a 4-pack.

**Why it's a reasonable pick:** Sylvania's standard LED is a solid middle-of-the-road option with decent dimming compatibility and a wide range of color temperatures on the shelf. Specs are in line with the rest of the mainstream field.

**Downsides:** Quality varies noticeably between their budget and premium lines. The cheapest Sylvania multipacks are the ones with the most mixed owner reviews.

**Who should buy it:** Anyone who wants a mainstream brand at a mainstream price and doesn't need anything special.

## Cutting the rest of the year-one cost

Bulbs are only part of the lighting bill. A few cheap fixes around them pay for themselves:

The [Amazon Smart Thermostat](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GKGW4N9N?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) and [Emerson Sensi Touch](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01N3CEUDH?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) won't change your lighting, but they're the same logic applied to heating — small upfront cost, ongoing savings. If your house leaks heat around windows, the [3M Indoor Shrink Film Kit](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000VSD9DG?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) or the [Window Insulation Kit by Frost King](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CT43WHJS?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) typically run $10–$25 and cut the load your heating system carries all winter.

For drying clothes instead of running a dryer, the [Honey-Can-Do Foldable Drying Rack](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BVRWJJR2?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) usually costs $25–$40 and does more for your annual utility bill than any bulb swap. And a [Bissell Featherweight](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B06ZY7BTWM?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__) at roughly $30–$40 keeps the dust off the bulbs so you're not replacing them because they look dim.

## The bottom line

If you hate rebuying things, buy fewer bulbs at a slightly higher price and stop thinking about it. A $3 bulb that lasts five years costs you about 60 cents a year plus roughly $1.50 in electricity. A $1.50 bulb that dies in 18 months costs you a dollar a year in purchase price alone — plus the trip. The premium brands aren't magic, but their owner-review track records on lifespan are measurably better than the no-name multipacks, and that's the number that actually shows up in your wallet.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does a single LED bulb cost to run for a year?

Roughly $1.50 if it's a 9W bulb running 3 hours a day at about 15 cents per kWh. An old 60W incandescent in the same socket runs closer to $10.

### Are the more expensive bulbs actually worth it?

Often yes, if you're comparing a $2–$6 name-brand LED against a no-name multipack. The purchase price difference is a few dollars, but a bulb that fails early means you pay that price twice.

### Do I need a smart plug to save money on lighting?

No, but it helps if lights get left on. A single Kasa Smart Plug HS103 usually runs $8–$12, and the two-pack typically lands near $15–$20.
