First apartments rarely come with a yard, and most raised-bed guides assume you have one. Here is the honest truth: what you need is not a raised *garden* bed, it is a compact raised *planter* — something that sits on a balcony or in a bright window, holds a few herbs and a salad crop, and takes under ten minutes a week to keep clean. That is the whole game at this budget.

This guide is written for the home cook on a strict monthly budget, so the focus is on cleaning time, refill cost, and what actually survives apartment life. Most picks below are raised planters, plus a couple of indoor potted options that behave like a raised bed for people with no outdoor space at all.

## A short reality check on this price range

At the low end — roughly $25 to $80 — you are buying a plastic or resin planter, not a cedar garden bed. It will not hold a tomato forest. It will hold herbs, lettuce, radishes, green onions, and maybe one pepper plant. Drainage trays are usually thin, and cheap saucers crack within a season or two if you leave them in direct sun.

What you *can* expect: a self-watering reservoir on some models (typically 1 to 2 gallons, varies by size), a footprint under 2 square feet, and a wipe-clean interior. What you cannot expect: frost protection, pest exclusion, or anything that survives a hard freeze without being moved indoors.

Weekly time cost is the part most guides skip. A single small planter with a reservoir realistically takes 5 to 10 minutes a week — mostly topping up water and pulling dead leaves. Skip the reservoir and you are watering every other day in summer, which is closer to 30 to 40 minutes a week. That difference matters more than the sticker price.

## What actually matters

- Cleaning time per week. A smooth interior with rounded corners wipes out in under a minute. Textured or slatted designs trap soil in the seams and take three or four times as long.
- Whether it has a reservoir. Self-watering designs cut watering frequency significantly, which is the single biggest time saver at this price.
- True footprint, not "capacity." A planter listed at 37 gallons is far too large for most balconies. Measure your ledge or floor space first.
- Drainage you can actually empty. A plug or removable tray beats a fixed saucer you have to tip the whole planter to drain.
- Refill cost. Potting mix runs roughly $8 to $15 per small bag, and you will typically replace it once a year. Cheap mix that compacts costs you more in replacement plants than it saves.
- Saucer or tray included. Buying one separately usually adds $8 to $15, which can be 30% of the planter's price.

## FCMP Outdoor 37 Gallon

Rough price band: typically $60 to $90.

This is the "I actually want a raised bed" option — a rectangular resin planter with a large soil volume, which means fewer waterings and more room for root crops. Owner reviews consistently report the plastic holds up well across multiple seasons outdoors, and the wide opening makes it easy to reach in with a cloth and wipe the sides.

The real downside is size. At 37 gallons it is heavy when full and awkward to move once planted, and it is genuinely too big for a small balcony. It also has no reservoir, so plan on watering every couple of days in hot weather.

Who should buy it: someone with a real balcony or patio who wants the most growing space per dollar and does not mind watering more often.

Rough price band: typically $25 to $40.

This is the practical apartment pick. ## Outsunny Walk-In Greenhouse

Rough price band: typically $70 to $130.

Not a raised bed in the classic sense, but for a first apartment with a balcony it does something a planter cannot: it extends your season by several weeks on either end, which means more harvest from the same cheap pots. The frame-and-cover design also keeps rain off, which cuts how often you need to clean soil splash off the outside of your planters.

The downside is assembly time — budget an hour or more — and the cover is the weak point. Owner reviews often mention the plastic degrading after a season or two in strong sun, and it needs anchoring in any real wind.

Who should buy it: someone with a balcony who wants to grow through shoulder seasons and does not mind a one-time setup.

[Outsunny Walk-In Greenhouse](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DP9KFZVK?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__)

## Miracle-Gro Potting Mix

Rough price band: typically $8 to $15 per bag.

This is the refill that makes every planter above work. A quality mix with perlite drains better than cheap garden soil, which means fewer root problems and less time spent troubleshooting. It is also the single biggest variable in how often you replace plants, so it is worth not skimping here.

Downside: it is not sterile, and bags stored damp can grow mold. Buy only what you will use in a season, and keep the rest dry.

Who should buy it: everyone with a planter on this list. This is the one purchase you should not try to save $3 on.

[Miracle-Gro Potting Mix](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B087F7GNFG?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__)

## Golden Pothos

Rough price band: typically $15 to $30.

If your "raised bed" is really a shelf by a window, this is the lowest-maintenance living thing you can put there. It tolerates low light, forgives missed waterings, and needs essentially no cleaning beyond wiping dust off the leaves with a damp cloth every couple of weeks — under two minutes.

Downside: it is ornamental, not edible. It also grows fast, and you will need to trim it or it will sprawl across whatever is nearby.

Who should buy it: a first-apartment renter who wants greenery with near-zero weekly time cost.

[Golden Pothos](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0H3PN93JJ?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__)

## ZZ Plant

Rough price band: typically $20 to $40.

The ZZ Plant is the plant for people who travel, work late, and forget things. It stores water in its rhizomes, so it can go two to three weeks between waterings in average indoor conditions, and the waxy leaves wipe clean in seconds. Owner reviews consistently describe it as nearly indestructible in low light.

Downside: slow growth, and it is toxic to pets if chewed. It also does not want much water — overwatering is the main way people kill it.

Who should buy it: a renter who wants a plant that costs almost no weekly time and survives a dark corner.

[ZZ Plant](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0H29M27XQ?tag=__AMAZON_TAG__)

## Amazon Basics Microfiber Cloths

Rough price band: typically $10 to $18 for a multi-pack.

The unglamorous pick that saves the most time. A dedicated cloth for wiping planter rims, saucers, and leaves keeps the whole routine to a couple of minutes a week, and microfiber lifts soil residue without soap, so you are not rinsing twice. Keep one cloth per planter and rotate them through the wash.

Downside: they pick up and hold grit, so a cloth used on soil should never touch a countertop. Wash separately.

Who should buy it: anyone who wants the cleaning step to take two minutes instead of ten.

## The bottom line

For a first apartment on a tight budget, the OXO Good Grips 1.75 Gallon is the pick that respects your time most — small, easy to wipe clean, and cheap enough that a failed first season is not a disaster. If you have genuine balcony space and want more than herbs, the FCMP Outdoor 37 Gallon gives you far more growing room for the money, at the cost of more watering. Pair either with Miracle-Gro Potting Mix and a stack of Amazon Basics Microfiber Cloths, and your weekly upkeep lands somewhere around 5 to 10 minutes. If you would rather grow nothing edible at all, the ZZ Plant and Golden Pothos are the lowest-effort way to have something alive in the room.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much time does a small planter take each week?

Realistically 5 to 10 minutes a week for a small planter with a reservoir — mostly topping up water and pulling dead leaves. Without a reservoir, expect to water every couple of days in summer, which works out closer to 30 to 40 minutes a week.

### Do I need potting mix, or can I use soil from outside?

Use potting mix. Miracle-Gro Potting Mix drains better than garden soil, which means fewer root problems and less time spent replacing plants. A bag typically runs $8 to $15 and you will usually replace it once a year.

### What is the easiest thing to keep in a first apartment?

The ZZ Plant and Golden Pothos are the lowest-effort options — both tolerate low light, and the ZZ Plant can go two to three weeks between waterings. If you want something edible, the OXO Good Grips 1.75 Gallon planted with herbs is the smallest, easiest-to-clean setup.
